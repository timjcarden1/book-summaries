The History of Rome

Ab Urbe Condita: from the founding of the city to the conquest of the Mediterranean

“What chiefly makes the study of history wholesome and profitable is this, that you behold the lessons of every kind of experience set forth as on a conspicuous monument.”

Livy, Preface (B. O. Foster’s translation)

Titus Livius · c. 59 BCE – AD 17

The whole book in one breath

Livy spent some forty years writing Rome’s history from its first day, and he never held office, commanded a soldier or, so far as we know, left Italy. What he produced is a national epic in prose, organised year by year, whose declared purpose is to show readers what to imitate and what to avoid. Of 142 books, 35 survive. They cover the legendary kings and the early Republic’s long quarrel between patricians and plebeians, the conquest of Italy, the war with Hannibal, and the subjugation of the Greek East. The early books are legend, and Livy says so; the later ones, where he follows the Greek historian Polybius, are history in a recognisable sense. Throughout runs one thesis: a poor, disciplined Rome conquered the world by absorbing the peoples it beat, and was then corrupted by what it won.

The work

142 books, 35 left

Ab Urbe Condita Libri, “books from the founding of the city”, ran from the fall of Troy to 9 BCE in 142 books, published in instalments over about forty years. Livy began it in the late 20s BCE and was still writing it when Augustus died in AD 14. Roughly three-quarters is gone.

  • The man. Titus Livius came from Patavium, modern Padua, a rich city of northern Italy. Ancient tradition gives his birth as 59 BCE and his death, at Padua, as AD 17; Ronald Syme argued for 64 BCE. He held no office we know of and commanded no army, unusual for a Roman historian. He knew Augustus, who is said to have teased him as a Pompeian, and he encouraged the young future emperor Claudius to write history.
  • Dates from inside the text. In Book 1 Livy says the temple of Janus had been closed only twice since Numa, the second time “by the emperor Augustus Caesar, after the battle of Actium” (1.19). He uses the title Augustus, granted in January 27 BCE, and does not mention the next closing, in 25 BCE, so Book 1 was probably finished between those dates. Book 28 says Spain was fully conquered “not until our own times” under Augustus, which means after 19 BCE (28.12). The summary of Book 121 notes that it was said to have been published after Augustus’s death.
  • The plan. Livy wrote in blocks of five and ten books, and marks the joins with new prefaces: after the Gallic sack (6.1), before Hannibal (21.1), and after him (31.1). Modern editions and translations, including the four Penguin volumes, follow those blocks.
  • The losses. We have Books 1–10 (753–293 BCE) and 21–45 (219–167 BCE), the last five damaged. Everything after 167 is gone, including his account of the Gracchi, Marius, Sulla, Caesar and Augustus: the part he lived closest to, and the part historians would most like to have.
  • The pace. His scale expands as his sources thicken (see the figure below). He himself noticed the problem at the start of Book 31: the sixty-three years of the two Punic wars had taken as many books as the 487 years before them.

If you remember nothing else

Twelve things the surviving books teach

Preface

History is a gallery of examples

Livy’s stated purpose is moral: the past is a monument showing what to imitate and what to avoid. He is writing a curriculum of conduct, and he says so.

Books 1–5, 6.1, 8.40

He knows the early books are shaky

The records burned in 390; family eulogies falsified the rest; no contemporary wrote. The early books tell you what Augustan Rome believed about its ancestors, which is a real fact about a real period.

Book 2.1

Liberty meant the annual magistracy

The Republic did not weaken the consuls’ power; it made it last a year and gave it a colleague. Everything else followed from that.

Books 1, 4, 8

Rome grew by letting outsiders in

Romulus’s asylum, the Alban nobles made patricians, Canuleius’s kings of foreign birth, the settlement of 338: Rome absorbed the people it beat.

Books 2–10

The constitution was built by concessions

The plebs withheld their bodies from the army; the patricians gave up an office; the office became law. Two centuries of this produced a working state without a revolution.

Books 8, 22, 27

The alliance was the real weapon

Hannibal destroyed Roman armies and could not break the confederation that replaced them. When twelve colonies refused more men, eighteen said yes.

Books 22, 42

Rome did not negotiate after a defeat

It refused to ransom the Cannae prisoners and refused Perseus peace after losing to him. Its enemies could win battles; they could not win a peace.

Books 8, 21–30

The one-year command cut both ways

It prevented tyranny for centuries and handed Hannibal his victories. Rome learned to extend commands and trust a Scipio, and the road to the Caesars began there.

Books 31–45

Disarm, don’t annex

Rome took fleets, elephants and the right to make war, declared Greeks free and left kings on their thrones. It ruled the Mediterranean by 167 while governing almost none of it.

Book 39.6

Decline has a date

Livy puts the start of foreign luxury in 187 BCE, when the army came back from Asia with bronze couches and expensive cooks, at the moment of greatest success.

Throughout

Religion was civic machinery

Prodigies were reported and expiated, gods were invited to change sides, a foreign cult was crushed. Livy half believes in it, and says his mind “becomes antique” when he writes of old times.

Books 3, 9, 42, 45

He reports what embarrasses Rome

The land grab at Ardea, the escape clause after the Caudine Forks, the “new wisdom” of deceiving Perseus, the sack of Epirus: a patriot, but not a liar.

Before Book 1

The preface

Livy’s preface is short and among the most quoted pages in Latin. It sets out why he wrote and how he wanted to be read.

  • Modesty, of a kind. He is not sure the work is worth doing, with so many historians before him, but it will satisfy him to have done what he could “to commemorate the deeds of the foremost people of the world”, and if his own fame is eclipsed, it will be by great men.
  • An escape. Readers, he expects, will hurry past the early centuries to “these modern times, in which the might of a people which has long been very powerful is working its own undoing”. He will take the opposite pleasure: turning away from the troubles of his own lifetime, the civil wars, to the distant past.
  • Legends admitted as legends. Traditions from before the founding, “rather adorned with poetic legends than based upon trustworthy historical proofs”, he will neither affirm nor refute. If any people may claim a god for its founder, it is Rome; the world accepts that claim as it accepts Rome’s rule.
  • The lesson. What matters is how Romans lived, by what men and methods the empire was won, and how discipline slipped and morals sank, until the present, “when we can endure neither our vices nor their cure”.
  • The monument. History is useful because in it “you behold the lessons of every kind of experience set forth as on a conspicuous monument”, to imitate or to avoid. And no state was ever greater or richer in good examples, or held out longer against greed and luxury.

Books 1–5 · The Early History of Rome

From Aeneas to the Gallic sack

Five books for 363 years, and the most famous pages Livy wrote. He tells the reader in the preface that the early material is closer to poetry than to evidence, and he keeps saying so: sources disagree, dates are confused, a story is “more fit to be displayed on the stage”. As history the first pentad is fragile. As a picture of what Augustan Rome believed about its own beginnings, and of how Livy thought a free state is built, it is the most valuable thing in the work.

Book 1 · c. 1184–509 BCE

The Kings

Seven kings, each of whom gives Rome an institution, and a last one whose family’s crime makes kings impossible.

Before Rome

  • Livy starts at the fall of Troy. The Greeks spare two Trojans who had argued for returning Helen: Antenor, who leads the Heneti to the head of the Adriatic (the Veneti, Livy’s own people), and Aeneas, who lands in Latium, marries Lavinia, daughter of King Latinus, and founds Lavinium. He unites Trojans and natives under one name, Latins, and dies fighting Turnus and the Etruscan Mezentius (1.1–2).
  • Livy is openly unsure of the details. He cannot say whether the Ascanius who founded Alba Longa was Lavinia’s son or Creusa’s son Iulus, whom “the Julian family call Iulus and claim as the author of their name”. That is the family of Caesar and Augustus, and Livy lets the claim stand without endorsing it (1.3).
  • A list of Alban kings bridges the three centuries to Numitor and his usurping brother Amulius, who makes Numitor’s daughter Rhea Silvia a Vestal to end the line. She bears twins and names Mars as the father, “whether actually so believing, or because it seemed less wrong if a god were the author of her fault” (1.4).

Romulus · 37 years

  • The twins are exposed at the Tiber, found by a she-wolf and raised by the shepherd Faustulus. Livy offers the rationalist reading too: Faustulus’s wife Larentia may have been called lupa, slang for a prostitute, and the miracle grew from the nickname (1.4).
  • An augury decides who founds the city. Remus sees six vultures first, Romulus twelve second, and both sides claim the win. In one version Remus dies in the brawl; in “the commoner story” he jumps the new wall and Romulus kills him: “So perish whoever else shall leap over my walls!” (1.7).
  • Romulus fills his city by opening an asylum to anyone, free or slave, and creates a hundred senators, the patres, whose descendants are the patricians. His twelve lictors, Livy thinks, were borrowed from the Etruscans (1.8).
  • With no women and no neighbour willing to intermarry, the Romans seize the Sabine daughters at the Consualia games. In the war that follows, Tarpeia sells the citadel for what the Sabines wear on their left arms and is crushed under their shields; the Sabine women then run between the armies and make the two peoples one (1.9–13). Romulus names the thirty curiae after them.
  • Romulus dedicates Rome’s first temple, to Jupiter Feretrius, for the spolia opima, the armour a Roman general strips from an enemy leader he has killed himself. Livy notes that in all the centuries since, such spoils had been won only twice (1.10).
  • He vanishes in a thunderstorm while reviewing the army. The people hail him a god; Livy records the whispered alternative, that the senators tore him apart, and the convenient vision of Proculus Julius, in which Romulus orders Rome to cultivate war and rule the world (1.16).

Numa to Ancus · 43, 32 and 24 years

  • Numa Pompilius, a Sabine, sets out to found the city a second time, on law and religion rather than arms. Livy dismisses the legend that Pythagoras taught him: Pythagoras lived more than a century later, far to the south, and spoke another language (1.18). Numa builds the temple of Janus, whose doors close only in peace. Livy says it had been closed twice since: after the First Punic War, and by Augustus after Actium in his own lifetime (1.19).
  • Numa’s religion is presented as policy. He “pretended to have nocturnal meetings with the goddess Egeria” because a fierce people needed the fear of the gods before it could be ruled without war. He creates the priests of Jupiter, Mars and Quirinus, the Vestals, the leaping Salii and a pontiff with written rules, and reforms the calendar (1.19–21).
  • Tullus Hostilius is more warlike than Romulus. War with Alba is settled by three Roman brothers, the Horatii, against three Albans, the Curiatii. Livy records the treaty formula as the oldest he knows, and admits the sources disagree about which triplets were Roman (1.24). The survivor kills his sister for mourning a Curiatius, is condemned, appeals to the people and is acquitted “more in admiration of his valour than from the justice of his cause” (1.26).
  • When the Alban dictator Mettius Fufetius deserts in battle, Tullus has him tied between two chariots and torn apart: “the first and last punishment among the Romans of a kind that disregards the laws of humanity”. Alba, “the work of the four hundred years” it had stood, is razed in a single hour; its nobles, among them the Julii, become Roman patricians (1.28–30). Tullus later botches a secret rite and is killed by lightning (1.31).
  • Ancus Marcius, Numa’s grandson, gives war its law: the fetial procedure of formal demand, thirty-three days’ wait, a senate vote and a bloodied spear thrown over the border (1.32). He settles conquered Latins on the Aventine, fortifies the Janiculum, builds the first bridge over the Tiber, a prison over the forum, and Ostia with its salt pans (1.33).

The Etruscan kings · 38, 44 and 25 years

  • Tarquinius Priscus is the son of a Corinthian exile, Demaratus, pushed to Rome by his ambitious wife Tanaquil after an eagle lifts his cap on the Janiculum (1.34). He is the first to canvass for the throne. He adds a hundred senators, lays out the Circus Maximus, drains the low ground with sewers and begins a stone wall (1.35–38). The augur Attus Navius, challenged to cut a whetstone with a razor, does it, and nothing is done without auspices thereafter (1.36).
  • Servius Tullius, marked out as a boy when his head blazed with harmless fire, succeeds by Tanaquil’s management of Priscus’s murder: she hides the death and speaks from a window while Servius takes over (1.39–41). Livy rejects the tradition that Servius was born a slave.
  • Servius is Livy’s constitutional hero. He creates the census and sorts citizens into classes by property, so that military service and votes both follow wealth (1.42–43; see the figure below). Eighty thousand citizens are counted at the first census (1.44). He adds the Quirinal and Viminal hills and extends the sacred boundary, and persuades the Latins to build a joint temple of Diana on the Aventine, a quiet admission that Rome was their capital (1.45).
  • His daughter Tullia and Lucius Tarquinius murder their own spouses, marry, and seize the throne. Servius is thrown down the senate-house steps and killed in the street, and Tullia drives her carriage over her father’s body, at the spot afterwards called the Wicked Street (1.46–48). Livy adds that “just and lawful kingship perished with him”.
  • Tarquin the Proud rules by terror: no burial for Servius, a bodyguard, capital trials without advisers, a senate left to shrink (1.49). He frames and drowns Turnus Herdonius of Aricia to cow the Latins, takes Gabii through his son Sextus (the silent message of the poppies struck off in the garden) and builds the great temple of Jupiter on the Capitol with forced labour, along with the Cloaca Maxima (1.50–56). The god Terminus refuses to be moved from the site, and a human head found in the foundations is read as Rome’s future as “head of the world” (1.55).

Lucretia and the end of the kings

  • An omen of a snake sends two of Tarquin’s sons to Delphi with their cousin Lucius Junius Brutus, who has survived the king’s purges by playing the fool (his name means “dullard”). The oracle says the next ruler will be whoever first kisses his mother; Brutus pretends to trip and kisses the earth (1.56).
  • At the siege of Ardea the princes argue over whose wife is best, and ride home to check. The king’s daughters-in-law are at a banquet; Lucretia, wife of Collatinus, is spinning wool among her maids late at night (1.57).
  • Sextus Tarquinius returns and rapes her, forcing her with the threat of leaving a murdered slave naked in her bed. She summons her father and husband, each with one friend, names Sextus, and kills herself: “not in time to come shall ever unchaste woman live through the example of Lucretia” (1.58).
  • Brutus draws the knife from the wound and swears to expel the king and his line. Lucretia’s body is carried into the forum, Brutus speaks to the people as tribune of the Celeres, and the gates are shut against Tarquin. The monarchy has lasted 244 years. Two consuls are elected, Brutus and Collatinus, “in accordance with the commentaries of Servius Tullius” (1.59–60).

Book 2 · 509–468 BCE

The Free Republic and Its First Enemies

Liberty survives a royalist plot, an Etruscan siege and a Latin war, then turns on itself over debt.

What liberty meant

  • Book 2 opens with an argument, not an event. Liberty dates from this year, Livy says, because the consuls’ power was made annual, not because it was reduced. And it came at the right time: had Brutus expelled an earlier king, a mob of refugees and shepherds would have been torn apart by tribunes’ agitation before wives, children and love of the soil had bound them into a people. The kings nursed the state until it could bear freedom (2.1).
  • The people swear never to allow a king. Collatinus, though a liberator, is pushed out of office and the city simply because his name is Tarquin (2.2). The senate is refilled to three hundred, which Livy gives as the origin of the formula “fathers and enrolled” (patres conscripti).
  • Young nobles who miss the court plot to bring the king back. Their complaint is Livy’s neatest statement of the case against law: a king can be moved by favour and can forgive, but “the law was a thing without ears, inexorable, more salutary and serviceable to the pauper than to the great man” (2.3). A slave overhears them. Among the conspirators are Brutus’s own sons, and as consul he watches them flogged and beheaded (2.4–5).

Porsena and the heroes of the bridge

  • Brutus and the king’s son Arruns kill each other in single combat, and the matrons mourn Brutus for a year (2.6–7). His colleague Publius Valerius, suspected of wanting a throne because his house stands on a hilltop, has it pulled down, lowers his rods of office before the people and passes the law of appeal. He is called Publicola, “friend of the people” (2.7–8).
  • Lars Porsena of Clusium besieges Rome for the Tarquins. Horatius Cocles holds the Sublician bridge, first with two companions and then alone, until it is cut behind him, and swims the Tiber in armour, a feat, Livy says, “destined to obtain more fame than credence with posterity” (2.10).
  • Gaius Mucius slips into the Etruscan camp, kills the king’s secretary by mistake, and holds his right hand in the altar fire to show what Porsena faces: three hundred young Romans sworn to the same attempt. He is freed and called Scaevola, “left-handed” (2.12–13). The hostage Cloelia escapes across the river with other girls; Porsena demands her back, then honours her. Porsena makes peace and, when the Tarquins press him again, withdraws for good once the senate tells him the Romans had resolved “to throw open their gates to enemies sooner than to kings” (2.13–15).
  • During the siege the senate buys the plebs’ loyalty: grain imported, the salt trade taken over by the state, and the poor excused taxes, on the ground that “the poor paid dues enough if they reared children” (2.9).

Regillus, debt and the first secession

  • Thirty Latin towns combine under Tarquin’s son-in-law Octavius Mamilius, and Rome names its first dictator; Livy cannot establish the year or even the man (2.18). At Lake Regillus the dictator Postumius wins a desperate battle, orders his own guard to cut down Roman fugitives, and vows a temple to Castor (2.19–20). Livy confesses that dates in this period are hopeless: the sources disagree so much that one cannot tell which consuls followed which, or in what year anything happened (2.21).
  • Tarquin dies in exile at Cumae, and at once the patricians begin to oppress the plebs (2.21). An old centurion appears in the forum in rags, shows his war scars and his flogged back, and explains how war, taxes and interest took his farm and then his body; the city erupts (2.23).
  • The plebs refuse to fight until debt bondage is eased, and the consul Servilius’s promise, broken by his colleague Appius Claudius, is the pattern of the century: concessions extracted in wartime and withdrawn in peace (2.24–31).
  • In 494 the plebs march out to the Sacred Mount three miles away and simply stay. Menenius Agrippa brings them back with the fable of the limbs that starved the belly and so starved themselves. The price is the tribunes of the plebs: sacrosanct officers, closed to patricians, who can protect any citizen from a magistrate (2.32–33).

Coriolanus, Cassius and the Fabii

  • Gaius Marcius wins the name Coriolanus by storming Corioli (2.33). In a famine he proposes to sell grain to the plebs only if they give up their tribunes: “When I would not brook Tarquinius as king, must I brook Sicinius?” He is tried, exiled, and leads the Volscians to within five miles of Rome. Envoys and priests fail; his mother Veturia and wife Volumnia succeed: “Suffer me to learn, before I accept your embrace, whether I have come to an enemy or a son” (2.34–40). Livy reports several versions of his death, noting that Fabius Pictor has him live to old age.
  • In 486 the consul Spurius Cassius proposes the first agrarian law, to share conquered land with the plebs and the Latins. He is condemned for aiming at kingship and executed; Livy says no land law, down to his own day, was ever debated without the greatest upheaval (2.41).
  • The Fabii hold the consulship seven years running, win back the plebs by caring for wounded soldiers, and then offer to fight Veii as a private family war. Three hundred and six Fabii march out, fortify a post on the Cremera, and are ambushed and killed to a man, leaving one boy to carry on the line (2.48–50).
  • The tribunes gain teeth. Volero Publilius, an ex-centurion ordered to serve as a private, fights off the lictors and, as tribune, has plebeian officials elected by tribes, which shuts out the patricians’ clients (2.55–58). The younger Appius Claudius, the plebs’ great enemy, has his sullen army decimated after it deliberately loses to the Volscians, and dies before his trial without once putting on mourning (2.59–61).

Book 3 · 467–446 BCE

Cincinnatus and the Decemvirs

The plebs ask for written law; they get it, and then a tyranny from the men who wrote it.

A decade of obstruction

  • In 462 the tribune Terentilius Arsa says the consuls’ power is “only in name … less hateful than that of a king”, and proposes a board to write down the laws that bind them (3.9). The patricians block it for a decade.
  • Their enforcer is Kaeso Quinctius, Cincinnatus’s son, who drives tribunes from the forum. Charged with a killing on dubious evidence, he is the first Roman to give public sureties, ten of them at 3,000 asses each, and flees. His father pays the forfeit, sells his property and moves to a small farm across the Tiber (3.11–13).
  • In 460 a Sabine, Appius Herdonius, seizes the Capitol by night with 4,500 exiles and slaves. The tribunes obstruct the levy even then; the consul Publius Valerius retakes the hill and dies doing it, with help from Tusculum that came unasked (3.15–18).

Sixteen days

  • In 458 a consul’s army is trapped by the Aequi on Mount Algidus and five horsemen bring the news. The senate names Lucius Quinctius Cincinnatus dictator. Livy stops to point a moral for “those who despise all human qualities in comparison with riches”: the envoys find him working his four-acre farm, and his wife Racilia fetches his toga from the hut (3.26).
  • He orders every man of military age to the Campus Martius by sunset with five days’ food and twelve stakes, marches through the night, and by dawn has walled the besiegers in with a trench of their own (3.27–28). The Aequi pass under the yoke, which Livy describes: two spears in the ground and a third tied across (3.28).
  • He gives all the plunder to his own men, demotes the rescued consul to legate, triumphs, and “on the sixteenth day Quinctius surrendered the dictatorship which he had received for six months” (3.29).

The Twelve Tables

  • The tribunes are raised to ten in 457 (3.30). In 454 three envoys go to Athens to copy the laws of Solon and study other Greek constitutions (3.31).
  • In “the three hundred and second year from the founding of Rome”, 451, the consulship and tribunate are suspended and ten men, the decemvirs, rule with no appeal and write the code. Each takes the twelve rods of office in turn every tenth day (3.33). Their ten tables are posted in the forum for every citizen to read and criticise, then passed by the assembly. Livy says that even in his day, “in this great welter of statutes piled one upon another”, they were “the fountain-head of all public and private law” (3.34).
  • A second board is elected to add two tables. Appius Claudius, once the plebs’ enemy, has canvassed as their friend; now he shows his nature. The new decemvirs appear with a hundred and twenty lictors carrying axes, stay in office past the Ides of May in 449 as private men, and lose battles because their armies will not win for them (3.35–42). A soldier who talks of restoring the tribunes, Lucius Siccius, is murdered on their orders (3.43).

Verginia

  • Livy draws the parallel himself: “not only did the same end befall the decemvirs as had befallen the kings, but the same cause deprived them of their power”. Appius desires Verginia, daughter of a centurion serving at Algidus and betrothed to a former tribune. His client Marcus Claudius claims her in court as a slave born in his house (3.44).
  • Appius, judging his own plot, awards her to the claimant. Her father asks to speak to her and the nurse aside, takes a knife from a butcher’s stall near the shrine of Cloacina, and kills her: “Thus, my daughter, in the only way I can, do I assert your freedom!” Then, to the tribunal: “’Tis you, Appius, and your life I devote to destruction with this blood!” (3.48).
  • The armies secede to the Aventine and then the Sacred Mount. The decemvirs resign; Appius dies in prison before his trial (3.50–58).
  • The consuls of 449, Valerius and Horatius, pass the laws that fix the settlement: resolutions of the plebeian assembly bind the whole people; no magistrate may be created without appeal, and whoever kills one who is may do so lawfully; the tribunes’ sacrosanctity is restored; decrees of the senate are lodged with plebeian aediles so consuls can no longer alter them (3.55).
  • The book ends with a shabby verdict. Asked to arbitrate between Aricia and Ardea over a strip of land, the Roman people, prompted by an old soldier who remembers serving there, award it to themselves (3.71–72). Livy calls it disgraceful.

Book 4 · 445–404 BCE

Intermarriage, Censors and Pay

The plebs win the right to marry patricians and, in principle, to hold supreme command, and keep voting for patricians anyway.

Canuleius’s speech

  • In 445 the tribune Gaius Canuleius proposes to legalise marriage between the orders and open the consulship to plebeians. The patricians say their blood would be polluted and the auspices confused (4.1–2).
  • His speech is one of the best in the book. Numa was not a patrician or even a Roman; Priscus was the son of a Corinthian exile; Servius the son of a captive woman. Every institution Rome has, pontiffs, census, consuls, once did not exist. “So long as men despised no family that could produce conspicuous excellence, the dominion of Rome increased.” A ban on intermarriage is “exile within the same walls”: why not a law against the intermarrying of rich and poor (4.3–5)?
  • The consuls’ answer is the real one: plebeians cannot take the auspices (4.6). Marriage is conceded. The consulship is dodged with a compromise, military tribunes with consular power, open to both orders.
  • Then the first surprise of Roman democracy: at the elections the people choose only patricians. Livy’s comment is that men think one way while fighting for liberty and dignity and another once the fight is over (4.6). The first such tribunes take office in the 310th year of the city and resign within three months over a flaw in the auspices (4.7).

Censors, a corn dealer and a king’s armour

  • In 443 the censorship is created to take the census the busy consuls neglect. It began, Livy says, as a clerical chore, and grew until it controlled morals, the senate’s membership and the state’s revenues (4.8). In 434 the dictator Mamercus Aemilius cuts its term from five years to eighteen months and resigns on the spot; the censors take revenge by disenfranchising him (4.24).
  • In a famine in 440–439 the rich knight Spurius Maelius buys grain in Etruria and gives it away. Accused of aiming at a crown, he resists a summons and is killed by Servilius Ahala. The aged Cincinnatus, dictator again, rules that Maelius was justly killed even if innocent of the charge, because he refused to answer it, a corn-dealer who thought that “for a couple of pounds of spelt he had purchased the liberty of his fellow citizens”. His house is razed (4.13–16).
  • When Fidenae revolts to Lars Tolumnius of Veii and Roman envoys are murdered, Aulus Cornelius Cossus kills Tolumnius and wins the second spolia opima (4.17–20).
  • Here Livy steps out of his narrative. All his sources make Cossus a military tribune, but Augustus, who rebuilt the temple of Jupiter Feretrius, told Livy he had read the word “consul” on the linen breastplate. Livy will not contradict the emperor’s testimony, and leaves the chronology open (4.20). It is the one place he quotes Augustus as a source, and scholars still argue whether it was deference, politics or honest doubt.

Toward Veii

  • Military tribunes and consuls alternate for decades. A defeat at Veii under three quarrelling colleagues becomes Livy’s lesson on the harm of divided command (4.31). Fidenae is finally taken in 426 after its people sally out carrying firebrands (4.33–34).
  • Livy records in passing that the Carthaginians, “destined to be such mighty enemies”, then first sent an army into Sicily (4.29). That is a slip: Carthage had fought in Sicily for generations, and lost the battle of Himera there in 480.
  • In 409 the plebs, still shut out of the top posts, elect three plebeian quaestors out of four. The patricians complain that there is no longer any point in educating their sons (4.54).
  • In 406, after the capture of Anxur, the senate votes the army pay from the treasury, “whereas till then every man had served at his own costs”. The plebs mob the senators with gratitude; senators cart their bronze to the treasury in wagons, because there is no silver coinage yet (4.59–60). Pay makes a long siege possible, and the siege of Veii begins (4.61).

Book 5 · 403–390 BCE

Veii, and Then the Gauls

Rome’s greatest victory and its worst humiliation in one book, joined by the career of one man.

The ten-year siege

  • The army winters before Veii in huts, “a new thing to the Roman soldier”, and the tribunes protest that pay was a trap to keep the plebs under arms all year (5.2). When a sally burns the siege works, men of the equestrian census volunteer to serve on their own horses, the plebs volunteer too, and senators weep with joy on the steps: “Then for the first time cavalry-men began to serve on their own horses” (5.7).
  • The Alban lake rises with no rain. A captured Veientine soothsayer says Veii cannot fall until it is drained, Delphi agrees, and the Romans cut an outlet onto the fields (5.15–17).
  • Marcus Furius Camillus, named dictator in 396, has a tunnel dug into the citadel by six shifts working six hours each (5.19). He vows a tenth of the spoil to Apollo and invites Juno of Veii to leave her city for a temple in Rome (5.21).
  • The tunnel opens into Juno’s own temple and the city falls. Livy reports the story that the diggers overheard the king’s sacrifice and snatched the entrails, and dismisses it: “in matters of so great antiquity I should be content if things probable were to be received as true”, and this one is “more fit to be displayed on the stage” (5.21). A young soldier asks the statue, “Wilt thou go, Juno, to Rome?” and his friends swear she nodded (5.22).
  • Veii, after “ten continuous summers and winters”, fell “by siege-works and not by force” (5.22). Camillus triumphs behind four white horses, the team of Jupiter and Apollo, and the city takes offence; his demand for Apollo’s tithe out of plunder already spent makes it worse (5.23).
  • Besieging Falerii, Camillus is offered the town’s children by their schoolmaster. He has the man stripped and whipped home by his pupils: “There are rights of war as well as of peace.” Falerii surrenders to Roman good faith (5.27).

The Allia

  • A plebeian, Marcus Caedicius, hears a voice at night telling the magistrates the Gauls are coming; he is ignored (5.32). Camillus is prosecuted over the Veii spoil, and his own tribesmen offer to pay his fine but not to acquit him. He goes into exile at Ardea, praying that Rome may soon regret him (5.32).
  • Livy then gives the Gauls a history: Celtic migrations over the Alps from the time of Tarquinius Priscus, two centuries before; the Insubres founding Milan; the Senones last (5.33–35). He reports and doubts the tale that a jealous husband from Clusium lured them south with wine.
  • Rome sends three Fabii to mediate at Clusium. They join the fighting and one kills a Gallic chief, a breach of the law of nations. The Gauls demand them, and the Roman people elect them military tribunes instead (5.36).
  • Livy lists the failures: no dictator named, no camp built, no auspices taken. At the river Allia, eleven miles out, the line breaks almost without fighting; most of the army flees to Veii rather than home, and many drown in the Tiber (5.37–38).

The sack

  • The young men garrison the Capitol. The old magistrates wait in their houses in full regalia on ivory chairs; the Gauls stare at them like statues until one strokes the beard of Marcus Papirius, who strikes him with his ivory staff, and the killing starts. The city burns (5.41).
  • Gaius Fabius Dorso walks through the enemy lines to perform his family’s sacrifice on the Quirinal and back again (5.46). Pontius Cominius floats down the Tiber on cork to get the senate’s approval for Camillus’s recall; Livy insists Camillus would not act until the law was passed (5.46).
  • The Gauls climb the Capitol by moonlight so quietly that even the dogs do not wake. The sacred geese of Juno, spared despite the famine, cackle and wake Marcus Manlius, who pushes the first Gaul off the rock (5.47).
  • Starving, the garrison agrees to pay a thousand pounds of gold. The Gauls use false weights; when the tribune protests, “the insolent Gaul added his sword to the weight”, and the Romans hear “Woe to the conquered!” (vae victis, 5.48). Livy does not name the thrower; later tellings make it Brennus.
  • Camillus arrives before the weighing is finished, declares the bargain void because it was made without the dictator, tells his men to win their country back “with iron instead of gold”, and destroys the Gauls, leaving no one to carry the news home. He is hailed “a Romulus and Father of his Country and a second Founder of the City” (5.49). The Greek historian Polybius, writing earlier, has the Gauls go home with the gold; see what held up.

The speech that kept Rome at Rome

  • With the city in ashes, the tribunes propose moving to the empty, intact Veii. Camillus’s answer closes the book and the first pentad (5.51–54). Rome was founded under auspices, and every spot is full of gods: the Capitol where the head was found, Terminus who would not move, Vesta’s fire, the sacred shields.
  • Then the site itself: “Not without cause did gods and men select this place for establishing our City, with its healthful hills; its convenient river … a situation in the heart of Italy” (5.54). The city is only in its 365th year, he adds, and no neighbour can match it.
  • What settles it is an accident. A centurion leading his men through the forum calls out, “Standard-bearer, fix your ensign; here will be our best place to remain,” and the senate takes it as an omen (5.55).
  • Rome is rebuilt within a year, with tiles supplied by the state and every man building where he liked. That, Livy explains, is why in his day the old sewers ran under private houses, and the city looked like one “where the ground has been appropriated rather than divided” (5.55).

Books 6–10 · Rome and Italy

The century that made a peninsula Roman

Livy opens Book 6 with a second preface: everything so far was obscure, because writing was rare and the records burned with the city; from this “second origin” he can speak more clearly (6.1). What follows is a hundred years of annual campaigns, the plebeians’ final victory, and the settlement that turned beaten enemies into soldiers. It is the collection readers skip, and the one that explains why Hannibal later failed.

Book 6 · 389–367 BCE

The Second Founding

Rome rebuilds, executes the man who saved the Capitol, and finally opens the consulship to plebeians.

A city without records

  • The new preface explains the fog over Books 1–5: “there was but slight and scanty use of writing, the sole trustworthy guardian of the memory of past events”, and the pontiffs’ commentaries and other records “nearly all perished in the conflagration of the City” (6.1). The magistrates collect what treaties and laws survive, the Twelve Tables among them, and the pontiffs keep the religious ones secret.
  • The day of the Allia, 18 July, which Livy notes was also the day of the Fabii’s massacre at the Cremera, is declared unlucky for any public or private business (6.1).
  • Camillus, kept in office, beats the Volsci, the Aequi and the Etruscans in a single year. As an old man he wins at Satricum by seizing a standard-bearer by the hand and dragging him at the enemy (6.2–8). Four new tribes bring the total to twenty-five (6.5).

Manlius Capitolinus

  • Marcus Manlius, who held the Capitol against the Gauls, is jealous of Camillus and turns to the debtors. He pays off a decorated centurion’s debt in the forum, sells his best farm to free others, and claims the patricians have hidden the Gallic gold (6.11–14).
  • At his trial for aiming at kingship he produces four hundred men he had freed from debt, thirty sets of spoils, forty decorations including eight civic crowns, and points up at the Capitol he had saved. The tribunes move the trial to a grove from which the Capitol cannot be seen, and he is condemned and thrown from the Tarpeian rock (6.20). His clan bans the first name Marcus.
  • Livy’s verdict: “Such was the end of a man who, had he not been born in a free state, would have left a memorable name.” The people soon missed him, and blamed a plague on his death (6.20).
  • Tusculum, caught helping the Volsci, disarms Rome by not resisting at all: Camillus finds the town going about its business with open gates, and it becomes the first Latin town absorbed as a community of Roman citizens (6.25–26).
  • The debts grow again because of a tax “to build a wall of hewn stone, which the censors had contracted for” (6.32). That wall, not a king’s, is most of what survives of the so-called Servian Wall today; see what held up.

Licinius and Sextius

  • Livy traces the decisive reform to a family slight. The younger daughter of Marcus Fabius Ambustus, married to a plebeian, is startled when a lictor raps at the door of her sister’s patrician husband, and her sister laughs at her. Her father promises she will see the same honours in her own house (6.34).
  • Her husband, Gaius Licinius Stolo, and Lucius Sextius, as tribunes in 376, propose three laws together: interest already paid to be deducted from debts and the rest paid in three annual instalments; no one to hold more than five hundred iugera of public land; and an end to the consular tribunes, with one consul always a plebeian (6.35).
  • When their colleagues veto them, they veto every election in return, and for five years Rome has no senior magistrates at all (6.35). Their argument: one man was holding the share of nearly three hundred citizens, and in forty-four years of the “open” military tribunate hardly a plebeian had been elected (6.36–37).
  • In 367, after ten years and a last Gallic scare beaten off by Camillus in his fifth dictatorship, the laws pass. Lucius Sextius becomes the first plebeian consul, and the patricians are compensated with a new praetor for the city’s justice (6.42).

Book 7 · 366–342 BCE

Champions, Plays and the Samnites

Twenty-five years of Gauls, plague and single combats, ending in the war that would decide Italy.

A generation of firsts

  • A plague in 365 kills Camillus, whom Livy calls, after Romulus, a second founder of Rome (7.1). To appease the gods Rome imports its first stage plays: Etruscan dancers to a piper, from whose word for performer, hister, the Romans got histrio. Livy traces how improvised verses grew into drama with plots (7.2).
  • In 362 a chasm opens in the forum. Soothsayers say it must receive Rome’s greatest strength, and a young soldier, Marcus Curtius, rides into it in full armour. Livy admits a rival story for the name of the “Lake of Curtius” and says that where time has destroyed the evidence one must follow tradition (7.6).
  • The same year the first plebeian consul to lead an army under his own auspices, Lucius Genucius, is ambushed and killed, and the patricians go about saying the gods had given their verdict (7.6).
  • Two duels produce two family names. Titus Manlius kills a giant Gaul who had stuck out his tongue at the Romans and takes his torque, becoming Torquatus (7.9–10). Marcus Valerius is helped by a crow that settles on his helmet and attacks the Gaul’s face, and becomes Corvus; he is consul at twenty-three (7.26).
  • The plebeian firsts come quickly. Gaius Marcius Rutilus becomes the first plebeian dictator in 356, triumphing by vote of the people when the senate refuses, and the first plebeian censor in 351 (7.17, 7.22). Licinius Stolo is fined under his own land law for holding a thousand iugera with his son, having emancipated the boy to dodge it (7.16).
  • Livy pauses in 349, when ten legions are raised against the Gauls, to say that such an army could hardly be found now “from the existing resources of the Roman People, though the world hardly contains them; so strictly has our growth been limited to the only things for which we strive,—wealth and luxury” (7.25). In 348 a treaty is made with Carthage (7.27).

The Samnite war begins

  • Livy marks the turn: “How vast a series of events! How many times the extremity of danger was incurred, in order that our empire might be exalted to its present greatness, hardly to be maintained!” The Samnites would lead to Pyrrhus, and Pyrrhus to Carthage (7.29).
  • The Samnites, powerful hill peoples of the central Apennines, attack Capua. The Campanians ask Rome for help, and Rome refuses because it has a treaty with the Samnites. So the Campanians surrender themselves and everything they own to Rome, which makes an attack on Capua an attack on Roman property (7.30–31). Livy tells it straight; it is the most transparent legal device in the book.
  • Valerius Corvus wins at Mount Gaurus (7.32–33). Publius Decius Mus saves a consul’s army trapped in a defile by seizing a hill above the enemy and breaking out at night, and the Samnite camp is taken with 30,000 dead; Decius receives a gold crown and a hundred oxen (7.34–37). Carthage sends congratulations and a gold crown of twenty-five pounds to the Capitol (7.38).
  • The garrison left in Capua plots to seize the city, then marches on Rome. Valerius Corvus, as dictator, talks it round without bloodshed (7.38–41). Livy notes that the sources agree on nothing except that there was a mutiny and it was settled (7.42).

Book 8 · 341–322 BCE

The Latin War and the Settlement

Rome’s own allies demand equality, lose, and are reorganised into the system that conquered Italy.

The demand

  • In 340 the Latin spokesman Lucius Annius of Setia tells the senate on the Capitol that Latium is as strong as Rome: one consul and half the senate should be Latin, and “there should be one people and one state”, all called Romans (8.5). The consul Torquatus swears he will kill any Latin he finds in the senate. Annius, leaving in a rage, falls down the temple steps; Livy will not say whether he died, and suggests the fall and the thunder may have been “invented aptly to express the resentment of heaven” (8.6).
  • The war is fought against men with the same language, weapons and drill as the Romans, many of whom had served beside them. So the consuls restore the strictest discipline (8.6). Torquatus’s own son accepts a challenge from a Tusculan officer against orders, wins, and is executed by his father before the army. The “orders of Manlius”, Livy says, “became a type of severity with succeeding ages” (8.7).

The legion, and the Decius devotion

  • Livy stops to describe the army. Rome once fought in a phalanx like the Macedonians, with round shields; after pay came in it switched to oblong shields and small companies, maniples, drawn up in three lines with gaps between the companies (8.8; see the figure below).
  • At the battle near Mount Vesuvius the Roman left gives way, and the consul Publius Decius Mus asks the pontiff to dictate the words of a devotio. With his toga over his head, standing on a spear, he offers himself and the enemy’s legions to the gods below and the Earth, then rides into the Latin ranks and is killed. The enemy line breaks where he falls (8.9).
  • The Latins are crushed. Torquatus returns to Rome, and the young men refuse to go out to meet him, then and all his life (8.12).

The settlement of 338

  • The consul Lucius Furius Camillus asks the senate whether it wants to destroy Latium or to grow by admitting the defeated. The senate decides each town’s fate separately (8.13–14; see the figure below).
  • The key clause is the one against the rest of the Latins: they lose the right to marry, trade and hold meetings with one another. Each community is tied to Rome alone. The captured beaks of Antium’s warships are nailed to the speakers’ platform in the forum, which is called the Rostra from then on (8.14).

The 320s

  • Quintus Publilius Philo, the first plebeian praetor, becomes in 326 the first commander whose authority is extended past his year of office, so that he can finish the siege of Palaepolis, the old town beside Naples, and triumph afterwards (8.15, 8.23–26). It is the device that would later let generals keep armies for decades.
  • In 331 deaths in the city are blamed on poison. Twenty matrons are caught brewing drugs, two drink their own potions and die, and a hundred and seventy are condemned. Livy says he “should be glad to believe” the story false (8.18).
  • In 326 a moneylender, Lucius Papirius, flogs a young man held in bondage for his father’s debt because he refused his advances. The boy’s bleeding back sparks the Poetelian law: no one may be chained for debt, only his goods answer for it. Livy calls it a new beginning of liberty for the plebs (8.28).
  • In 325 the dictator Papirius Cursor tries to execute his master of the horse, Quintus Fabius, for winning a battle against orders. The army, the senate, Fabius’s father and finally the whole people beg for his life; Papirius yields to the people while insisting that the rule was right (8.29–35).
  • The book ends with Livy’s sharpest statement of his problem: “The records have been vitiated, I think, by funeral eulogies and by lying inscriptions under portraits, every family endeavouring mendaciously to appropriate victories and magistracies to itself” (8.40). No writer contemporary with these events survives.

Book 9 · 321–304 BCE

The Caudine Forks, and Alexander

Rome’s most humiliating defeat, a legal trick to escape it, and Livy’s one great digression.

The trap

  • In 321 the Samnite general Gaius Pontius sends ten soldiers dressed as shepherds to tell Roman foragers that the Samnites are besieging Luceria in Apulia. The consuls take the short road to relieve it, through the Caudine Forks: two narrow wooded passes with a grassy plain between. They find the far pass blocked with felled trees and boulders, and the one behind them too (9.2).
  • Pontius asks his old father Herennius what to do. Let them all go with kindness, he says, or kill them all; a middle course “is in sooth a policy that neither wins men friends nor rids them of their enemies” (9.3). His son ignores him.
  • The consuls say they cannot make a treaty without the people’s order, so they, their officers and quaestors swear a personal pledge, and six hundred knights are left as hostages (9.5). Both consuls, stripped almost naked, then every officer and every legion, pass under the yoke while the Samnites jeer (9.6).
  • At Capua the army will not speak or lift its eyes. The Campanians think Rome’s spirit is broken; one old Campanian says that silence means vengeance (9.6).

The escape clause

  • At Rome, the consul Postumius himself proposes that the men who swore the pledge be handed over to the Samnites, which would free the people, who had sworn nothing. At the handover he kicks the Roman herald and declares himself a Samnite now, so that Rome has a fresh grievance (9.8–10).
  • Pontius refuses to accept them and gives the speech Livy clearly felt: if Rome believes in the gods, let it put the army back in the trap or keep its word, instead of handing over a few men and calling the account settled (9.11). Livy reports all of it. It is the plainest case in the book of his honesty outrunning his patriotism.
  • Rome recovers Luceria within the year, frees the six hundred hostages, retakes the lost standards and sends seven thousand Samnites under the yoke in turn (9.15).

Could Alexander have beaten Rome?

  • Livy says he has never digressed for his own pleasure, but the question has to be asked: what if Alexander the Great, who died in 323, had turned west (9.17)?
  • Rome had generals as good as any Macedonian: Torquatus, Corvus, the two Decii, Papirius Cursor, Fabius. Its senate was “an assembly of kings”, in the phrase Livy borrows, without naming him, from Cineas, the envoy of Pyrrhus. Alexander had beaten Darius, who dragged “a train of women and eunuchs” to war (9.17).
  • Rome could raise 250,000 men by census and field four or five armies at once; Alexander would have crossed with perhaps 30,000 foot and 4,000 horse (9.19). The phalanx was rigid, the legion divisible. “Alexander, if overcome in one battle, would have been overcome in war”; Rome survived Caudium and later Cannae (9.19).
  • Livy adds that it is unfair to set the career of one young man against a nation that had been fighting for centuries, and that the Alexander of the later years, spoiled by success, would have come to Italy more like Darius than like Alexander (9.18). He ends with a hope rather than a claim: Rome will beat a thousand armies more formidable, so long as its present love of peace and civil harmony lasts (9.19).

Appius and Flavius

  • The censor Appius Claudius (312) builds the road and the aqueduct that bear his name, fills the senate with sons of freedmen, stays in office past the legal eighteen months when his colleague resigns, and hands the ancient rites of Hercules from the Potitii family to public slaves. All thirty grown Potitii die within the year, and Appius later goes blind (9.29, 9.33–34).
  • Quintus Fabius takes an army through the Ciminian forest into Etruria, a wood then thought as frightening as the German forests of Livy’s own day, and Perusia, Cortona and Arretium sue for a truce (9.36–37). A Samnite army in gilded and silvered armour is beaten, and the Campanians dress their gladiators as “Samnites” in mockery (9.40).
  • In 304 Gnaeus Flavius, a freedman’s son and a clerk, is elected curule aedile. He publishes the forms of legal action the pontiffs had kept secret and posts the calendar round the forum on white boards, so anyone can know when the courts sit. The nobles take off their gold rings in protest (9.46). Fabius, as censor, confines the city’s poor to four urban tribes, and is called Maximus for it (9.46).

Book 10 · 303–293 BCE

Sentinum

Four peoples combine against Rome and are beaten one at a time; a second Decius dies like his father.

The last walls come down

  • In 300 the Ogulnian law opens the priesthoods: the pontiffs rise from four to eight and the augurs from four to nine, with the new places all plebeian (10.6–9). Livy stops over the arithmetic: augurs ought to come in threes, one for each ancient tribe, so how could there have been four (10.6)?
  • The same year a Valerius passes the law of appeal for the third time, always a Valerius. Its only penalty was to call a violation “wicked”, which Livy thinks shows how strong shame was then; nobody would take such a threat seriously in his day (10.9).
  • In a debate on the priesthoods, Decius Mus lists the plebeian firsts: Sextius consul, Licinius Stolo master of the horse, Marcius Rutilus dictator and censor, Publilius Philo praetor (10.8).

The coalition

  • The Samnite Gellius Egnatius builds an alliance of Samnites, Etruscans, Umbrians and Gauls (10.18). In 295 Fabius and Decius, consuls together again, meet it at Sentinum in Umbria.
  • The allies plan well: the Samnites and Gauls will fight while the Etruscans and Umbrians storm the Roman camp. Three deserters from Clusium betray the plan, and Roman armies ravage the land round Clusium so that the Etruscans go home to defend it (10.27). Livy says plainly that with all four present “the Romans must have been defeated”.
  • Before the battle a hind chased by a wolf runs between the lines. The Gauls kill the hind; the Romans let the wolf, the beast of Mars, pass through (10.27).
  • Fabius holds the right against the Samnites and waits for them to tire. Decius, younger, attacks the Gauls at once, and his cavalry is scattered by Gallic chariots, a way of fighting new to them. Crying that it is the fate of his family, he repeats his father’s devotion and dies in the Gallic line (10.28).
  • Fabius sends Campanian cavalry round the Gauls’ rear and wins. Livy gives 25,000 enemy dead and 8,000 prisoners, with Roman losses of 7,000 in Decius’s army and 1,200 in Fabius’s (10.29). He dismisses other writers who gave the enemy 40,330 foot, 6,000 horse and 1,000 chariots as beyond belief (10.30).

Aquilonia and the end of the decade

  • The Samnites swear their best men into a “linen legion” in an enclosure roofed with linen, killing those who refuse the oath at the altars (10.38).
  • At Aquilonia in 293 the chicken-keeper, seeing the army’s eagerness, reports that the sacred chickens ate greedily when they had refused. The consul Papirius Cursor, son of the dictator, rules that any false omen falls on the man who reported it, and puts the keeper in the front line, where a javelin kills him (10.40). The Samnites lose 30,340 dead (10.42).
  • Livy notes that his account of the Samnite wars has already run “continuously throughout four books, covering a period of forty-six years”, with more to come (10.31).
  • The book, and the surviving first decade, ends with a census of 262,322 citizens and a plague, for which the Sibylline books order that Aesculapius be brought from Epidaurus (10.47).

Books 11–20 · Lost

The missing decade

Ten books covering 292 to 219 BCE are gone. We know their outline from the Periochae, one-paragraph summaries of every book written in late antiquity, which survive for all but two of the 142.

Books 11–20 · 292–219 BCE · known from the summaries

Pyrrhus and the First Punic War

The half-century in which Rome finished Italy and crossed the sea, told now in a few hundred words.

  • Italy completed. Aesculapius arrives from Epidaurus as a serpent that swims ashore on the Tiber island (291). Curius Dentatus beats the Samnites and Sabines (290). A debt crisis ends with a secession to the Janiculum and the dictator Hortensius (287). The Senones kill Roman envoys (283) and the Tarentines insult them (282).
  • Pyrrhus (281–272). The king of Epirus comes to help Tarentum. He wins at Heraclea with elephants and remarks that every Roman dead lay facing the enemy. He tries to bribe Fabricius and fails; the old, blind Appius Claudius has himself carried into the senate to reject Pyrrhus’s terms; Fabricius returns a traitor who offered to poison the king. Pyrrhus goes to Sicily, comes back, is beaten, and leaves Italy (275) (Summaries 12–14).
  • Firsts. The first plebeian censor to close the census (280), Rome’s first silver coinage (269), the first gladiator show, given by Decimus Junius Brutus in honour of his dead father (264), and the first plebeian chief pontiff, Tiberius Coruncanius (253) (Summaries 13, 15, 16, 18).
  • The First Punic War (264–241). Rome answers the Mamertines of Messana and crosses to Sicily. Duilius wins the first naval triumph (260). Regulus invades Africa, is captured by the Spartan mercenary Xanthippus, is sent home on parole to negotiate, advises the senate against the terms, returns to Carthage as he swore he would, and is tortured to death. Claudius Pulcher drowns the sacred chickens that will not eat and loses his fleet (249). Lutatius wins at the Aegates islands (241) and Carthage makes peace (Summaries 16–19).
  • Between the wars. Rome takes Sardinia and Corsica, fights the Illyrians, crosses the Po for the first time against the Insubrian Gauls, and Marcellus wins the third and last spolia opima (222). Flaminius builds his road and circus (Summary 20).

The loss matters. Livy’s first decade explains how Rome built its Italian system; the third shows the system under Hannibal’s hammer. The chapters that showed it being extended overseas, and Rome learning to fight at sea, are the ones that are gone.

Books 21–30 · The War with Hannibal

Ten books on seventeen years

Livy opens with a claim borrowed from Thucydides: “the war which I am going to describe was the most memorable of all wars ever waged” (21.1). The pace slows to under two years a book, the sources improve (the Greek historian Polybius, and Fabius Pictor, who lived through it), and the story is the best in ancient history. A general crosses the Alps with elephants, destroys three Roman armies in three years, and cannot finish the job, because Rome’s answer to catastrophe is to raise another army and refuse to talk.

Book 21 · 221–218 BCE

Saguntum and the Alps

How the war began, who Hannibal was, and what the march to Italy cost.

The causes

  • Livy puts the war down to hatred as much as power. The Romans resented being attacked by people they had beaten; the Carthaginians resented the way they had been treated since, above all the loss of Sardinia, seized by Rome during Carthage’s war with its own mercenaries, with a payment added on top (21.1).
  • He tells the story of Hannibal at about nine years old, begging to go to Spain with his father Hamilcar and made to swear at the altar that he would be an enemy of Rome as soon as he could (21.1). Hamilcar ran Spain for nine years and his son-in-law Hasdrubal for eight, the second by diplomacy more than force, and agreed the Ebro as the boundary with Rome (21.2).

Hannibal

  • Livy’s portrait is the most famous in the work (21.4). The veterans saw the young Hamilcar in his face. He could obey and command equally well, endure heat and cold, eat and sleep only as need required, and was often seen asleep on the ground in a soldier’s cloak among the sentries. He dressed like his men; only his weapons and horses stood out. He was first into battle and last out.
  • Then the other side: “his cruelty was inhuman, his perfidy worse than Punic; he had no regard for truth, and none for sanctity, no fear of the gods, no reverence for an oath, no religious scruple” (21.4). The vices are asserted rather than shown, and much of what follows in the ten books shows the virtues.

Saguntum and the declaration

  • Hannibal attacks Saguntum, a city allied to Rome though south of the Ebro, in 219, and takes it after eight months. Its leading men throw their gold and silver onto a fire and many throw themselves in after it (21.7–15). In Carthage only Hanno argues for handing Hannibal over (21.10).
  • A Roman embassy led by Quintus Fabius asks whether the siege was the city’s policy. A Carthaginian answers with a lawyer’s point: Rome itself had refused to be bound by the first draft of the treaty of 241 until its own people ratified it, so why should Carthage be bound by an agreement its general made (21.18)?
  • Fabius folds his toga: “We bring you here both war and peace; choose which you will!” They tell him to give whichever he likes. He shakes out the fold and gives war, and they accept it (21.18).

The march

  • Hannibal crosses the Ebro with 90,000 foot and 12,000 horse, leaves 11,000 to hold the land, and sends home 10,000 more who were deserting or unwilling (21.23). A dream, he says, showed him a vast serpent following his army and destroying everything behind it: the devastation of Italy (21.22).
  • At the Rhône the Gauls hold the far bank. Hannibal sends a column 25 miles upstream to cross and attack them from behind, and floats his elephants over on rafts covered with earth so they will think they are on land (21.26–28).
  • In the Alps his men face snow, mountain tribes who roll rocks down on the column, and a landslip that has taken away the path. They cut a new one by building a huge fire against the rock and pouring vinegar on it to crack it, then zigzag the descent for the elephants (21.32–37).
  • They reach Italy, Livy says, five months after leaving New Carthage, having crossed the Alps in fifteen days (21.38). How many arrived he cannot say, and he gives the spread honestly (see the figure below). He rejects the idea that the Pennine Alps took their name from the Carthaginians (Poeni): the locals named them after a god.

The first two battles

  • At the Ticinus the consul Publius Scipio is wounded in a cavalry fight and saved by his son, “just reaching manhood”, the future Africanus. Livy notes that the historian Coelius gave the credit to a Ligurian slave, and adds: “I should prefer, for my own part, that the story about his son were the true one” (21.46).
  • At the Trebia, on a snowy winter day, the other consul, Sempronius, is eager to win before the elections. Hannibal hides his brother Mago with 2,000 men in an overgrown stream bed, sends Numidian horse to provoke the Romans at dawn, and lets them wade the freezing river unfed while his own men eat and oil themselves by their fires (21.53–55).
  • The Roman wings collapse and Mago attacks from behind; about ten thousand Roman infantry cut straight through the Carthaginian centre and escape to Placentia. Rain and sleet afterwards kill most of Hannibal’s elephants (21.56). At Rome people ask what generals and legions are left, now that both consuls and both consular armies have been beaten (21.57).

Book 22 · 217–216 BCE

Trasimene, Fabius and Cannae

Two catastrophes separated by a year of not fighting, and Rome’s response to the second.

Trasimene

  • Hannibal takes the route through the flooded marshes of the Arno, four days and three nights without sleep, and loses the sight of one eye riding the last surviving elephant (22.2). The new consul, Gaius Flaminius, is a popular politician the senate distrusts, and he ignores the omens: his horse throws him and the standard will not come out of the ground (22.3).
  • Hannibal ravages Etruria to provoke him and waits where the hills of Cortona come down to Lake Trasimene, leaving a narrow way along the shore into a small plain. His infantry stand in plain view on the hills ahead; his light troops and cavalry hide on the slopes and at the entrance. Flaminius marches in before full daylight without scouting, and a morning mist lies on the plain (22.4).
  • The Romans fight as individuals, without formation, for about three hours, and nobody notices an earthquake that throws down towns across Italy (22.5). An Insubrian horseman, Ducarius, recognises Flaminius and kills him; men drown trying to escape into the lake (22.6).
  • Fifteen thousand Romans die and ten thousand scatter. Livy rejects larger figures, saying he takes as his main authority “Fabius, who was contemporary with the events of this war” (22.7). At Rome the praetor tells the crowd simply: “A great battle has been fought, and we were beaten” (22.7).

The Delayer

  • With the surviving consul unreachable, Rome does “what had never been done until that day, and created a dictator by popular election”: Quintus Fabius Maximus (22.8). He vows a “sacred spring”, the offering of all the animals born in the coming spring, and games costing 333,333⅓ asses (22.9–10).
  • Fabius shadows Hannibal along the high ground and refuses battle. His own master of the horse, Minucius, mocks him as a new Camillus going to rescue Italy by walking over mountains (22.14). Hannibal burns Fabius’s neighbours’ farms and spares Fabius’s own to make him look like a collaborator (22.23).
  • Fabius does trap Hannibal in the Falernian plain, but Hannibal escapes by night by tying burning faggots to the horns of about two thousand oxen and driving them up the hills, so the Roman guards on the pass think the army is breaking out there (22.16–17).
  • The assembly gives Minucius equal command, a thing unheard of. He is promptly ambushed and saved by Fabius, calls him Father, and puts himself back under his orders (22.25–30). When the senate will not fund an exchange of prisoners Fabius negotiated, he sells his own farm to pay for it (22.23).

Cannae

  • For 216 the people elect Gaius Terentius Varro, a butcher’s son and the popular candidate, with the aristocrat Lucius Aemilius Paullus (22.25–35). The army is the largest Rome has fielded: Livy reports that some authorities put it at 87,200 men (22.36). Fabius warns Paullus that time is the only weapon against Hannibal (22.39).
  • The consuls command on alternate days, and Varro offers battle on his day, by the river Aufidus near Cannae. Hannibal has 40,000 foot and 10,000 horse. The sun is to one side and the local wind blows dust into Roman faces (22.44–46).
  • Hannibal bows his centre of Gauls and Spaniards forward toward the Romans, with his African veterans, armed with captured Roman equipment, held back at either end. The Roman infantry drives the bulge in until it bends the other way, and the Africans turn inward on both flanks (22.47; see the figure below). Five hundred Numidians pretending to desert attack the Roman rear with hidden swords (22.48).
  • Paullus, wounded early by a sling stone, refuses a horse from the tribune Lentulus and sends a last message: tell Fabius that “Lucius Aemilius lived, and now dies, mindful of his injunctions” (22.49).
  • Livy gives 45,500 infantry and 2,700 cavalry killed, both quaestors, twenty-nine military tribunes and eighty senators or men eligible to be senators, with 3,000 foot and 1,500 horse captured (22.49). Varro escapes to Venusia with about fifty horsemen.

The response

  • Maharbal urges Hannibal to march on Rome and dine on the Capitol in five days. When Hannibal hesitates he says: “In very truth the gods bestow not on the same man all their gifts; you know how to gain a victory, Hannibal: you know not how to use one.” Livy adds that “that day’s delay is generally believed to have saved the City and the empire” (22.51).
  • At Canusium a group of young nobles led by Lucius Caecilius Metellus plan to abandon Italy. The military tribune Publius Scipio, the future Africanus, walks in with a drawn sword and makes them swear never to desert the Republic (22.53).
  • Rome, terrified, convicts two Vestals of unchastity and buries a Gallic couple and a Greek couple alive in the cattle market, which Livy calls “a sacrifice wholly alien to the Roman spirit”. It sends Fabius Pictor to Delphi, arms eight thousand slaves, and refuses to ransom the prisoners (22.57–61).
  • The book ends with Varro, whose rashness caused the disaster, met outside the city by crowds who thank him “because he had not despaired of the state”. Had he been a Carthaginian general, Livy notes, there is no punishment he would not have suffered (22.61).

Book 23 · 216–215 BCE

Capua Defects

The south of Italy goes over to Hannibal, and the Roman system holds anyway.

  • Capua. Italy’s second city goes over to Hannibal, led by Pacuvius Calavius, who first saves the unpopular senate by locking it in and then “reconciles” it with the people (23.2–4). Hannibal promises the Campanians their own laws and no compulsory service; the Roman citizens in Capua are shut in the bath-houses and suffocated (23.7). One Campanian, Decius Magius, argues against Hannibal to his face and is sent away in chains (23.10).
  • The rings. Mago reports to the Carthaginian senate and pours out the gold rings taken from dead Roman knights, a heap some said filled three and a half measures, and more likely one (23.12). Hanno asks two questions: has any Latin people defected, and has Rome asked for peace? The answer to both is no, so, he says, the war is not won (23.12–13). Carthage votes 4,000 Numidians and 40 elephants.
  • Nola. Marcellus repulses Hannibal outside Nola, the first check he suffers; Livy declines to vouch for the claim that Hannibal lost 2,800 men to Rome’s 500 (23.16). Later, rallying his men, Marcellus says “Capua had been Hannibal’s Cannae”: the army’s winter of comfort there had done to it what battle could not (23.18, 23.45).
  • Refilling the senate. So many senators have died that Rome names a second dictator, which has never been done, to fill the gaps. Marcus Fabius Buteo enrols 177 new senators by strict rules of past office and bravery, then resigns the same day (23.22–23). A proposal to give seats to two men from each Latin town is shouted down.
  • More disasters. A consul-elect, Postumius, is ambushed by the Boii in a forest whose trees they had cut almost through, so they topple onto the column; his skull is gilded and used as a cup (23.24). In Spain, though, the Scipio brothers beat Hasdrubal at the Ebro and keep him from joining his brother (23.29).
  • Philip. Philip V of Macedon makes an alliance with Hannibal. The envoys carrying the treaty are captured at sea and the text read in Rome, which opens the First Macedonian War (23.33–39).
  • The contractors. With the treasury empty, nineteen businessmen in three companies agree to supply the Spanish armies on credit, asking only exemption from service and state insurance for their cargoes (23.48–49). Livy presents it as patriotism; two books later some of the same class are caught sinking worthless old ships to claim that insurance (25.3).

Book 24 · 215–214 BCE

Slaves, Syracuse and Archimedes

Rome arms its slaves and loses its oldest ally in Sicily.

  • Syracuse turns. King Hiero, Rome’s ally for fifty years, dies. His fifteen-year-old grandson Hieronymus switches to Carthage and is assassinated within months; two Syracusan brothers raised in Hannibal’s service, Hippocrates and Epicydes, take over the city amid massacres, including of Hiero’s daughters (24.4–7, 24.21–32).
  • The slave army. At Beneventum Tiberius Gracchus commands volunteer slaves promised freedom for bringing back an enemy’s head. When the heads slow them down he cancels the rule, wins, and frees them all: “I bid you all be free.” Those who hung back must eat standing for the rest of their service. Beneventum lays tables in the streets for them (24.14–16).
  • Ships from the rich. To man the fleet, citizens supply sailors and their pay in proportion to their wealth, from one sailor for the modestly rich to eight for senators (24.11).
  • Archimedes. Marcellus attacks Syracuse by land and sea and is stopped by one man, “of unrivalled skill in observing the heavens”, whose engines were built with Hiero’s money. Catapults are set for every range; the wall is pierced with small loopholes for archers; and an iron grapple on a counterweighted beam lifts ships by the prow and drops them (24.34). The assault becomes a blockade. There are no burning mirrors in Livy; that legend is later.
  • Philip humbled. Philip besieging Apollonia on the Adriatic is surprised by a night attack and escapes half-dressed, burning his own ships (24.40).
  • Spain and Africa. The Scipios win again and make an ally of the Numidian king Syphax, sending centurions to train his infantry. Syphax is then beaten by a Numidian prince fighting for Carthage, Masinissa, seventeen years old (24.48–49).

Book 25 · 213–212 BCE

Tarentum Lost, Syracuse Taken

A year of disasters in Italy and Spain, and the fall of the greatest Greek city in the west.

  • Scipio’s first office. Challenged that he is too young to stand for aedile, Scipio replies: “If all the citizens want to make me aedile I have years enough” (25.2).
  • A fraud. The contractor Postumius of Pyrgi loads rotten ships with worthless cargo, sinks them, and claims the state’s insurance. When the tribunes prosecute, his friends riot, and he goes into exile (25.3–4).
  • Tarentum. Two young Tarentines, released by Hannibal after Trasimene and Cannae, win the garrison’s trust by bringing back game from nightly hunts, and one night let Hannibal in. The Roman garrison holds the citadel for five years, and Hannibal hauls the Tarentine fleet through the streets on wagons to get it out of the blocked harbour (25.7–11).
  • Three more defeats. Gracchus is betrayed and killed (Hannibal gives him an honourable funeral); an ex-centurion given eight thousand men to fight Hannibal loses nearly all of them; and a praetor loses sixteen thousand of eighteen thousand at Herdonea (25.16–21).
  • Syracuse falls. Marcellus takes the heights of Epipolae during a festival and then the inner city. Archimedes is killed “while intent on some figures which he had described in the dust” by a soldier who does not know who he is; Marcellus grieves and honours his family (25.23–31).
  • Greek art comes to Rome. Marcellus ships the statues and paintings of Syracuse home. Livy calls it the beginning of Rome’s craze for Greek art and of the plundering of temples that followed, which struck first at the very temples Marcellus had adorned (25.40).
  • Spain lost. Both Scipio brothers are killed within a month after their Celtiberian mercenaries are bribed away (25.32–36). A Roman knight, Lucius Marcius, rallies the survivors and takes two Carthaginian camps; Livy gives three writers’ casualty figures, from 37,000 to 5,000, and does not choose between them (25.37–39).

Book 26 · 211–210 BCE

Hannibal at the Gates

Hannibal marches on Rome and fails to save Capua; a 24-year-old takes Spain’s capital in a day.

The march on Rome

  • To draw the besiegers off Capua, Hannibal marches on Rome, camps on the Anio three miles away and rides with two thousand horsemen to the Colline gate to look at the walls (26.7–10). Rome brings in part of the army from Capua but does not lift the siege.
  • Two days running the armies draw up for battle and two days running a hailstorm sends them back to camp (26.11). Hannibal is said to have remarked that sometimes he lacked the will to take Rome, and sometimes the chance.
  • Two things discourage him. Reinforcements for Spain march out of the city under his eyes, and a prisoner tells him that the land his camp stood on had just been sold, “with no reduction in price on that account”. In reply he orders the silversmiths’ shops around the forum put up for auction (26.11).

Capua’s end

  • Vibius Virrius, who had led Capua over to Hannibal, holds a last banquet at which he and twenty-seven senators take poison (26.13–14). The proconsul Fulvius Flaccus has the other Capuan leaders beheaded, putting the senate’s letter recommending clemency in his pocket unread until the executions are done (26.15–16).
  • Capua survives as a city but not as a state: no senate, no magistrates, its land confiscated, its people governed by a Roman prefect (26.16). Livy approves the policy for its moderation.
  • With no money left for rowers, the consul Laevinus proposes that senators set the example: all their gold, silver and bronze to the treasury, keeping only a ring, the children’s amulets, a pound of silver and five thousand asses. The knights and the people follow (26.35–36).

Scipio

  • No one will stand for the command in Spain, where two generals have just died. Publius Scipio, about twenty-four, puts himself forward and is elected (26.18). Livy analyses his manner: he did nothing public or private without first sitting alone in the temple on the Capitol, and he neither affirmed nor denied the story, told of Alexander too, that a serpent had fathered him (26.19).
  • He strikes at New Carthage, Carthage’s capital in Spain. Fishermen from Tarraco tell him the lagoon behind the city can be waded at low water; at midday the ebb and a north wind drain it, and Scipio tells his men to follow Neptune across. The city falls in a day (26.42–46).
  • Livy lists the haul, ten thousand free men, two thousand artisans made public slaves, 276 gold bowls, 18,300 pounds of silver, then admits the sources disagree on everything from the number of hostages (300 or 725) to the scorpion catapults (60, or 6,013), and suggests the middle figure is nearest the truth (26.47–49).
  • A beautiful captive is brought to Scipio. He returns her to her fiancé, Allucius, and gives the ransom her parents brought as a dowry. Allucius tells his people that “a most godlike youth” had come, “conquering everything by arms and especially by generosity”, and brings Scipio 1,400 horsemen (26.50).

Book 27 · 210–207 BCE

The Colonies, and the Metaurus

Rome’s allies reach breaking point, and a single forced march decides the war in Italy.

Twelve refuse, eighteen hold

  • In 209 twelve of Rome’s thirty Latin colonies tell the consuls they have no more men or money to give. Livy names them, and then names the eighteen who said they were ready to give more, adding that their names should never be forgotten: “By the support of these colonies the empire of the Roman people then stood” (27.9–10). The twelve are punished by the senate’s silence, and later with double levies (29.15).
  • The census of that year counts 137,108 citizens, “a considerably smaller number than it had been before the war” (27.36; see the figure below). Rome draws on the reserve gold kept for emergencies, 4,000 pounds (27.10).

Tarentum and Marcellus

  • Hannibal, beaten one day by Marcellus after winning the day before, says: “Of a truth we have to deal with an enemy who can bear neither good fortune nor bad” (27.14).
  • The old Fabius retakes Tarentum by the same kind of treachery that lost it. He carries off its wealth but leaves the colossal statues of its gods: let the Tarentines keep their angry gods. Hannibal, arriving too late, says: “The Romans also have their Hannibal; by the same art by which we had captured Tarentum we have lost it” (27.15–16).
  • When the commander who had held the citadel for five years claims the credit, Fabius replies that he owed the recapture to him, since it would not have needed recapturing if he had not lost the town (27.25).
  • Marcellus, over sixty and consul for the fifth time, rides out to inspect a hill near Venusia with his colleague and 220 horsemen and is killed in an ambush. Livy blames a rashness unworthy of his years (27.26–27). Hannibal takes his signet ring and uses it to forge letters (27.28).

The Metaurus

  • In 207 Hasdrubal crosses the Alps with a second army and writes to his brother arranging to meet in Umbria. The letter is intercepted (27.39–43).
  • The consul Gaius Claudius Nero, facing Hannibal in the south, takes six thousand foot and a thousand horse and marches north to join his colleague Livius Salinator, leaving his camp in place so Hannibal will not notice. His men enter Livius’s camp at night and double up in the tents so it will not look bigger (27.43–46).
  • Hasdrubal hears the trumpet sounded twice, the signal for a consul, guesses both consuls are present, and retreats. His guides desert him at the Metaurus river (27.47). In the battle, Nero takes troops from the Roman right, marches them behind the line and falls on the enemy’s flank. Hasdrubal dies fighting. His elephant drivers kill their own maddened animals with a mallet and chisel driven in behind the ear (27.48–49).
  • Livy gives 56,000 enemy dead and 5,400 captured, and about 8,000 Romans and allies killed (27.49); Polybius’s figure for the enemy is far lower. Nero is back in his own camp within six days (27.50).
  • Nero throws Hasdrubal’s head in front of Hannibal’s outposts. Hannibal, Livy says, “recognized the destiny of Carthage”, and pulls back into the toe of Italy (27.51). At Rome, business resumes as if in peacetime.

Book 28 · 207–205 BCE

Spain Won, Africa Argued

Scipio finishes the Carthaginians in Spain and persuades Rome to let him invade Africa.

  • Ilipa. Hasdrubal son of Gisgo gathers 50,000 foot (some said 70,000) and 4,500 horse; Scipio has about 45,000. For days both sides deploy the same way. Then Scipio suddenly reverses his order, weak Spanish allies in the centre and Romans on the wings, and attacks early before the enemy has eaten (28.12–15). The Carthaginian army dissolves; Hasdrubal escapes with about six thousand (28.16).
  • Two guests of Syphax. Scipio sails to Numidia to meet Syphax and finds Hasdrubal there too. The king seats them on the same couch at dinner, and Scipio charms both (28.17–18).
  • Iliturgi and Astapa. Scipio destroys Iliturgi, which had massacred Roman fugitives. The people of Astapa pile their families and goods on a pyre, guard it with fifty young men, and die fighting while it burns (28.19–23).
  • Mutiny. When Scipio falls ill, eight thousand men at Sucro mutiny over pay. He summons them unarmed, speaks to them (“language never failed me” before this day), and executes thirty-five ringleaders while the rest watch (28.24–29).
  • The debate. Back in Rome, consul for 205 though he had held no senior office, Scipio wants Africa. The old Fabius argues against it: remember Regulus; the place to fight Hannibal is where he is; and a consul’s first duty is Italy, not his own glory (28.40–42). Scipio answers that Agathocles of Syracuse had shown Carthage could be attacked at home, and that the way to get Hannibal out of Italy is to make Carthage call him back (28.43–44).
  • Etruria equips the fleet. Scipio gets Sicily with permission to cross, but no state levy. The Etruscan towns volunteer: grain from Caere, iron from Populonia, sailcloth from Tarquinii, 30,000 shields and helmets and 50,000 javelins from Arretium. Thirty warships are launched forty-five days after the timber was cut (28.45).

Book 29 · 205–204 BCE

The Mother of the Gods, and the Landing

Rome imports a goddess, investigates Scipio’s staff, and finally lands an army in Africa.

  • Locri. Scipio retakes Locri in the toe of Italy and leaves it to his legate Pleminius, who plunders the temple of Proserpina and tortures Roman officers who object (29.6–9). A Locrian embassy to the senate gives Fabius the chance to attack Scipio’s Greek habits in Syracuse, the gymnasium and the Greek cloak. A commission goes out, finds Scipio’s army in perfect order, and clears him (29.16–22).
  • The Idaean Mother. The Sibylline books say a foreign enemy can be driven out of Italy if the Idaean Mother is brought from Pessinus in Phrygia. King Attalus of Pergamum hands the envoys “a sacred stone” (29.10–11). The goddess must be received by the best man in Rome, and the senate picks Publius Scipio Nasica, too young even to be quaestor; Livy says he will not guess which of his virtues won the vote, because the writers nearest the time did not record it (29.14).
  • Peace with Philip. The First Macedonian War ends in a compromise, the Peace of Phoenice (29.12).
  • The landing. Scipio sails from Lilybaeum with fifty warships and four hundred transports; the sources give his army as 10,000 foot and 200 horse, 16,000 and 1,600, or 35,000 in all (29.25–26). It is the first Roman army in Africa for almost fifty years, since Regulus (29.28).
  • Masinissa. Livy tells the young prince’s adventures: deprived of his kingdom by Syphax, hunted through the mountains, down to sixty horsemen, and at last joining Scipio (29.29–33). Syphax, meanwhile, has married Sophonisba, daughter of Hasdrubal son of Gisgo, and gone over to Carthage.
  • Salinator. At home the censors Livius and Nero put a new tax on salt, heaviest in the districts whose tribes had once condemned Livius, which is how he got the nickname Salinator. The census of 204 counts soldiers in the armies abroad for the first time, and gives 214,000. The two censors then disgrace each other (29.37).

Book 30 · 203–201 BCE

Sophonisba, and Zama

Scipio burns two camps, loses Masinissa’s bride, meets Hannibal, and ends the war.

  • The burning camps. Scipio’s envoys to Syphax take centurions dressed as servants to study the huts of reeds and matting in the enemy camps. At night Masinissa and Laelius set Syphax’s camp alight; Hasdrubal’s men run out unarmed to help and are cut down, and then their own camp is fired. Livy gives 40,000 killed or burned and only 2,500 escaping (30.3–6).
  • Sophonisba. Syphax is captured. Masinissa marries his captive wife, Sophonisba, the same day. Scipio claims her as Roman booty and rebukes him privately. Masinissa sends her poison with a message that he is keeping his other promise, that she would not fall alive into Roman hands; she drinks it, saying she would have died more gladly had she not married on the day of her funeral (30.12–15). Scipio then publicly names Masinissa king.
  • Hannibal recalled. Carthage sues for peace and recalls Hannibal, who gnashes his teeth: “The conqueror of Hannibal is therefore not the Roman people, so often cut to pieces and put to flight, but the Carthaginian senate by carping and envy” (30.20). He kills the Italian soldiers who refuse to sail in the sanctuary of Juno Lacinia, and looks back at Italy cursing himself for not marching on Rome after Cannae. The old Fabius dies the same year (30.26).
  • The meeting. Before the last battle the two generals meet between the armies with one interpreter each, and for a moment say nothing out of admiration. Hannibal observes that fortune has had its joke: he first fought the father and now asks peace of the son. Scipio will not trust terms from a city that has just broken a truce (30.29–31).
  • Zama. Hannibal puts eighty elephants in front, more than ever before, and his veterans from Italy in reserve. Scipio leaves lanes between his maniples for the elephants to run through. Laelius and Masinissa, returning from chasing off the Carthaginian cavalry, hit the rear. Livy gives over 20,000 Carthaginians killed and as many captured, and about 2,000 Romans dead (30.32–35).
  • The terms. Carthage keeps its laws and its African lands, but surrenders deserters, all its elephants and all its warships but ten, may make no war in or out of Africa without Rome’s consent, restores Masinissa, and pays 10,000 talents over fifty years (30.37). When a Carthaginian argues against accepting, Hannibal drags him down from the platform and apologises to the assembly: “At nine years of age I left you, and after thirty-six years I have returned” (30.37).
  • Hannibal laughs. When the Carthaginian senate weeps over the first payment, Hannibal laughs. Their tears come too late, he says: the time to weep was when the fleet burned. And Rome will not stay quiet either: “No great state can long be in peace. If it lacks an enemy abroad it finds one at home, just as powerful bodies seem protected against infection from without, but are of themselves weighed down by their very strength” (30.44).
  • Africanus. Scipio triumphs and puts 123,000 pounds of silver into the treasury. Livy does not know whether the name Africanus came from his soldiers, the people or his flatterers, but notes “he was the first general to be distinguished by the name of a nation conquered by him” (30.45).

Books 31–45 · Rome and the Mediterranean

Thirty-four years to one master

Livy begins the fourth decade with a sigh of relief and a warning. He is glad to be done with Hannibal, “as if I myself had borne a share of the toil”, but notes that the sixty-three years of the two Punic wars took him as many books as the 487 years before them; he is like a man who wades out from the shallows and finds the water getting deeper (31.1). The next thirty-four years bring down Macedon twice and humble the Seleucid empire, while Rome annexes almost nothing. Most of it follows Polybius, whom Livy calls “an authority worthy of credence on all matters of Roman history and especially on occurrences in Greece” (33.10).

Book 31 · 201–200 BCE

War Again, Against Macedon

An exhausted people is talked into a new war within a year of the last one.

  • Athens, Rhodes and King Attalus of Pergamum complain of Philip V’s aggression in the Aegean, and Rome remembers the treaty he made with Hannibal (31.1–3).
  • The assembly votes the war down almost unanimously. People are tired, and a tribune accuses the patricians of starting one war after another. The consul Sulpicius Galba argues that the choice is to fight Philip in Macedonia now or in Italy later, as with Pyrrhus and Hannibal, and the second vote passes (31.6–8).
  • At Abydos on the Hellespont the besieged vow to kill their wives and children and themselves if the walls fall. A young Roman envoy, Marcus Aemilius Lepidus, delivers Rome’s ultimatum to Philip in his camp. When the city is taken the Abydenes carry out their vow, and Philip gives the survivors three days to die (31.16–18).
  • Philip ravages Attica, burning temples and even tombs; the Athenians respond by cursing him and pulling down every statue of him and his ancestors (31.24–26, 31.44).
  • In the north a Carthaginian named Hamilcar leads the Gauls in sacking Placentia, and the praetor Furius Purpureo kills 35,000 of them (31.10, 31.21).

Book 32 · 199–197 BCE

Flamininus

A young consul unlocks Macedonia and wins over the Greeks.

  • Carthage makes its first indemnity payment, and Roman assayers find the silver a quarter short; the Carthaginians borrow in Rome to make it up (32.2).
  • Titus Quinctius Flamininus, consul in 198 at about thirty, faces Philip in the gorge of the river Aous for forty days. A herdsman sent by an Epirote chief leads four thousand men round the heights; a smoke signal starts the attack from both sides, and Philip is driven out (32.9–12).
  • The Achaean League debates changing sides. Its general Aristaenus argues that it cannot hold out against a Roman fleet and army, and the League joins Rome, though some cities walk out of the assembly (32.19–23).
  • At a conference with Philip on the Malian gulf, Livy says openly that Flamininus was playing for time: he wanted peace if he was going to be replaced and war if his command was extended. His friends in Rome got it extended (32.32–37).
  • Philip hands Argos to Nabis, tyrant of Sparta, who immediately courts the Romans (32.38–40). From 197 Rome elects six praetors a year, two of them for its new Spanish provinces (32.28).

Book 33 · 197–195 BCE

Cynoscephalae and the Freedom of Greece

The legion beats the phalanx, and Rome declares the Greeks free.

The battle

  • In 197 the two armies stumble into each other in fog on a line of hills called Cynoscephalae, the Dogs’ Heads. The skirmish grows, and Philip, told the Romans are running, commits his army though he dislikes the ground (33.7–8).
  • His right-wing phalanx, charging downhill, drives the Roman left back. His left wing is still coming up in marching column. Flamininus sends his elephants at it and it breaks (33.9).
  • Then the decisive stroke, by an officer Livy does not name: “one of the tribunes of the soldiers, forming a plan to fit the emergency, took the soldiers of twenty companies” from the winning Roman right and attacked the victorious Macedonian right from behind. A phalanx cannot turn round (33.9).
  • The Macedonians raise their pikes upright, their sign of surrender. The Romans do not know it and keep killing (33.10). Livy gives 8,000 dead and 5,000 captured, with about 700 Romans killed. He sets aside Valerius Antias, “who is prone to increase numbers without restraint” and claimed 40,000, and follows Polybius (33.10).

The proclamation

  • The peace takes Philip’s fleet but for five ships and a huge royal galley of sixteen banks of oars, forbids him elephants and any war outside Macedonia without the senate’s leave, and costs him a thousand talents and his son Demetrius as a hostage (33.30).
  • At the Isthmian Games at Corinth in 196, a herald proclaims that the Corinthians, Phocians, Locrians, Euboeans, Magnesians, Thessalians and others are free, ungarrisoned and under their own laws. The crowd cannot believe it and makes him read it again (33.32).
  • Flamininus, “only about thirty-three years old”, is nearly crushed by the crowd. The Greeks tell each other that there was “one people in the world which would fight for others’ liberties at its own cost, to its own peril and with its own toil”, even across the sea (33.33).
  • Livy reports all this without comment. Within a few pages, Rome’s envoys are telling Antiochus III to stay out of Europe and free the Greek cities of Asia (33.34).

Hannibal leaves Carthage

  • Elected to high office at Carthage, Hannibal audits the treasury, exposes embezzlement and shows the city can pay Rome without new taxes. The embezzlers denounce him to Rome as plotting with Antiochus (33.45–47).
  • Scipio opposes Rome taking sides in Carthaginian faction fights, and loses. Hannibal escapes by night to Cercina, invites the ship-masters in the harbour to a feast shaded by their own sails so none can follow, and reaches Tyre, then Antiochus at Ephesus (33.47–49).

Book 34 · 195–194 BCE

Cato, the Women, and Nabis

A debate about women’s jewellery, a hard campaign in Spain, and the Romans leave Greece.

The Oppian law

  • In the crisis of 215 the Oppian law had forbidden any woman to own more than half an ounce of gold, wear dyed clothes or ride in a carriage in town. In 195 two tribunes propose its repeal, and the women of Rome fill the streets and the magistrates’ doorways to lobby for it (34.1).
  • The consul Cato opposes repeal in a speech Livy writes with relish. Women are kept in check by laws; loosen one and the rest will go: “The moment they begin to be your equals, they will be your superiors” (34.2–4).
  • The tribune Valerius answers that the law is only twenty years old and was an emergency measure; laws made for a crisis are mortal and change with the times; and why should Latin allies’ wives wear gold that Roman wives cannot (34.5–7)? The law is repealed.

Spain and Greece

  • Cato sails to Spain and announces: “This war will support itself” (34.9). He wins at Emporiae. When the leaders of the tribes north of the Ebro have no answer to his question of how to stop them rebelling, he pulls down the walls of all their strongholds in a single day (34.17). Livy thinks the campaign harder than his predecessors’, and says so (34.18).
  • Flamininus makes war on Nabis, besieges Sparta and imposes terms: Argos freed, the coastal towns and ships surrendered, hostages and money, but Nabis left in power (34.22–41).
  • At Corinth Flamininus announces the withdrawal of every Roman garrison, including from the three fortresses Philip had called “the fetters of Greece” (34.48–50). The Greeks buy back the Roman prisoners of the Hannibalic war who had been sold as slaves in Greece, 1,200 in Achaea alone, and they walk behind his chariot in his three-day triumph with shaven heads, like freed slaves (34.50, 34.52).
  • At the games of 194 the senators sit apart from the people for the first time. Some call it overdue; others say that for 558 years all Romans had watched together. Livy adds that Africanus, who proposed it, came to regret it (34.54).

Book 35 · 193–192 BCE

Antiochus Comes West

Hannibal at the Seleucid court, and the Aetolians invite a king into Greece.

  • A Roman envoy at Ephesus meets Hannibal several times, and Antiochus starts to distrust him (35.14).
  • Livy passes on, as a report from the annalist Claudius following the Greek history of Acilius, the story that Africanus was on that embassy and asked Hannibal who was the greatest general. Alexander, said Hannibal; then Pyrrhus, who taught the art of laying out a camp; then himself. And if you had beaten me? “Then, beyond doubt, I should place myself both before Alexander and before Pyrrhus and before all other generals” (35.14). The telling is careful: it is Claudius’s story, not Livy’s.
  • Hannibal wins back the king’s trust by telling him of the oath he swore as a child, and that he had fought Rome for thirty-six years (35.19).
  • Philopoemen, general of the Achaean League, is beaten at sea by Nabis in an ancient leaky warship and then outmanoeuvres him on land (35.26–30). The Aetolians, resentful that Rome gave them less than they expected in 196, invite Antiochus in and murder Nabis to seize Sparta (35.31–35).
  • Antiochus lands at Demetrias with 10,000 foot, 500 horse and six elephants, “a force scarcely sufficient to take possession of Greece if it were undefended” (35.43).

Book 36 · 191 BCE

Thermopylae Again

A king spends the winter on his wedding, and Cato finds the path the Persians used.

  • Antiochus falls for a girl of Chalcis, marries her, and spends the winter feasting while his army goes soft (36.11). Hannibal’s advice, to bring in Philip and carry the war to Italy, is ignored (36.7).
  • The king holds the pass of Thermopylae, with the Aetolians guarding the mountain paths above. The consul Acilius Glabrio sends two legates, one of them the ex-consul Cato, over the heights at night; Cato surprises the Aetolians, appears above the king’s army, and it breaks (36.15–19).
  • Of the ten thousand men Antiochus brought, only the five hundred with him escaped; Livy notes Valerius Antias’s claim of 60,000 as a further sign of his inflation (36.19).
  • The Aetolians surrender “to the good faith of the Roman people”, discover that it means unconditional surrender, and are threatened with chains when they protest (36.27–28). A Roman fleet beats Antiochus’s off Corycus (36.43–45).

Book 37 · 190–189 BCE

Magnesia

The first Roman army in Asia breaks the Seleucid king.

  • The consul of 190 is Lucius Scipio, with his brother Africanus serving under him as legate; he is the first Roman general to take an army into Asia (37.1). Africanus’s son is captured and later sent back by Antiochus without ransom, a kindness that will be used against both brothers (37.34).
  • At Magnesia near Sardis, Antiochus fields scythed chariots, camels ridden by Arab archers with long swords, and elephants. King Eumenes of Pergamum sends slingers and archers at the chariots, the horses panic and plough into their own lines, and the royal army collapses (37.38–43).
  • Livy gives up to 50,000 foot and 3,000 horse killed on the king’s side, against 300 Roman foot and 24 horsemen, plus 25 of Eumenes’s men (37.44).
  • Antiochus’s envoys ask for mercy. Africanus replies that Romans are neither lifted up by success nor broken by defeat, and offers the terms they had refused before the battle: leave Asia west of the Taurus mountains, pay 15,000 talents, and hand over Hannibal (37.45). Eumenes and Rhodes are rewarded with the lands Antiochus gives up (37.53–56).

Book 38 · 189–187 BCE

The Galatians, and the Fall of the Scipios

An unauthorised war in Asia, and the victor of Zama driven from Rome.

  • The consul Manlius Vulso campaigns against the Galatians, Gauls settled in central Asia Minor, storming their hill refuges (38.12–27). A captive Galatian chief’s wife, raped by a centurion who then sells her ransom, has her kinsmen kill him as he weighs the gold and throws his head at her husband’s feet (38.24).
  • At home Manlius is accused of making war without authority and of turning his army into looters; he gets his triumph anyway (38.44–50).
  • The tribunes then attack Africanus, charging that he took money from Antiochus. On the day of his trial, the anniversary of his victory in Africa, he says it is no day for lawsuits, goes up to the Capitol to thank the gods, and the whole assembly follows him, leaving the tribunes alone (38.51). He retires to his estate at Liternum and never returns. His enemy, the tribune Tiberius Gracchus, blocks the prosecution, and later marries his daughter Cornelia, mother of the Gracchi (38.52–57).
  • His brother Lucius is fined; the sale of his estate does not even cover the fine, which suggests the charge was false (38.60).
  • Livy then admits he cannot sort out the story. The sources disagree on the prosecutor, the year, and where Africanus died and was buried; at Liternum he has seen his statue on the tomb, “lately … cast down by a storm”, and at Rome a tomb of the Scipios outside the Capena gate carries statues of two Scipios and the poet Ennius (38.56). That Roman tomb has been found.

Book 39 · 187–183 BCE

Luxury, the Bacchanalia and Cato

Livy dates the start of the decline, and the state polices a religion.

Where the rot began

  • Livy puts the turn here, in 187: “the beginnings of foreign luxury were introduced into the City by the army from Asia”. Bronze couches, rich coverlets and tapestries, tables on a single pedestal, girls playing the harp at dinner, and food prepared with new care, so that “the cook, to the ancient Romans the most worthless of slaves … began to have value, and what had been merely a necessary service came to be regarded as an art” (39.6).
  • These things, he adds, were barely the seeds of the luxury to come (39.6). It is the moment the preface promised: the point where discipline began to slide.

The Bacchanalia

  • A secret rite of Bacchus, brought by a Greek to Etruria, spreads to Rome “like the contagion of a pestilence”, with nocturnal meetings of men and women, and in Livy’s telling forged wills, poisonings and murders (39.8–9).
  • It is exposed by a love story. A young man, Publius Aebutius, is to be initiated by his mother and stepfather, who have embezzled his inheritance; his mistress, a freedwoman named Hispala Faecenia, begs him not to go, and when she is questioned by the consul Postumius she tells everything (39.9–14).
  • The consul warns the people that the cult is a conspiracy within the state. More than seven thousand men and women are said to be involved. Those merely initiated are imprisoned; those who committed crimes are executed, and “a greater number were executed than thrown into prison”. Women are handed to their families for punishment, and the rites are banned across Italy except in groups of five with a praetor’s permission (39.15–18).

Cato the censor, and three deaths

  • Livy gives Cato a full portrait as he stands for censor in 184: a man of humble birth fitted for any position, soldier, lawyer, orator, farmer, harsh in temper and tongue, perpetually in feuds with the nobles (39.40). As censor he expels Flamininus’s brother Lucius from the senate for executing a man at a dinner party to amuse a favourite, and taxes luxury goods at ten times their value (39.42–44).
  • In 183 Flamininus demands Hannibal from King Prusias of Bithynia. Hannibal finds all seven exits from his house guarded and takes the poison he has long kept ready. “Let us relieve the Roman people of their long anxiety,” he says, since they cannot bear to wait for an old man to die (39.51).
  • Livy then argues from documents about when Scipio died, against Polybius and others. Scipio could not have been alive in 184, when another man was named leader of the senate in his place, but a speech of his against a tribune who took office in December 185 still existed. So he died between the two dates (39.52). He closes on the coincidence that Hannibal, Scipio and Philopoemen, the greatest men of three nations, died about the same time, none of them as their lives deserved (39.52).

Book 40 · 182–179 BCE

The Tragedy of Philip’s House

A king kills the son Rome liked, and dies knowing it.

  • Philip’s cruelty in his last years: when he orders the children of executed nobles rounded up, a mother named Theoxena gives her own children poison and a sword and throws herself into the sea with her husband (40.4).
  • His sons are rivals: Perseus, the elder, and Demetrius, who had been a hostage in Rome and was liked there. Perseus accuses Demetrius of plotting murder, and a letter supposedly from Flamininus, brought by two Macedonian envoys, seems to prove Demetrius a Roman agent. Philip has him killed (40.5–24).
  • Too late, Philip learns the letter was forged. He plans to leave the kingdom instead to Antigonus, nephew of the regent who had brought him up, and dies in 179, haunted by his son and cursing Perseus, who succeeds him (40.54–56).
  • In Rome, workmen on the Janiculum dig up two stone chests labelled as the coffin and books of King Numa. The coffin is empty; the books, seven in Latin on pontifical law and seven in Greek on philosophy, look new. The praetor reads them, swears they would undermine religion, and the senate has them burned in the comitium (40.29).
  • Forty thousand free Ligurians, with their wives and children, are moved from their mountains to Samnium at public expense (40.38).

Book 41 · 178–174 BCE · damaged

A Strange King

A book with large gaps, remembered for one portrait.

  • Much of Book 41 is lost in the single manuscript that preserves Books 41–45. What remains covers campaigns in Istria, Liguria, Sardinia and Spain, where Tiberius Gracchus (the father) founds a town named after himself.
  • Its best passage is on Antiochus IV of Syria, who had been a hostage in Rome. He walks about Antioch in a Roman toga, canvassing like a candidate for office; sits on an ivory chair to judge petty cases; gives sweets to great men and fortunes to strangers; and some thought him mad. Yet he was royally generous to cities and gods, building a temple to Olympian Zeus at Athens and one to Capitoline Jupiter at Antioch (41.20).
  • The summary of the book adds that the sacred fire of Vesta went out, and that a new law, supported by Cato, restricted women’s inheritances.

Book 42 · 173–171 BCE

Toward the Last Macedonian War

Rome decides on war with Perseus and wins the diplomacy by trickery, to some senators’ disgust.

  • A censor strips half the marble roof tiles from the famous temple of Juno at Lacinium, which Pyrrhus and Hannibal had spared, to roof his own new temple in Rome. The senate orders them returned; no one in the south knows how to relay them, and they lie in the temple precinct (42.3).
  • King Eumenes comes to Rome to denounce Perseus, and on his way home is ambushed and stoned at Delphi, supposedly by Perseus’s agents (42.11–16).
  • A veteran named Spurius Ligustinus, asked to serve again below his old rank, gives Livy’s fullest portrait of an ordinary soldier. He inherited an acre and a cottage, married his cousin, raised six sons and two daughters, enlisted in 200, and has served twenty-two years, four times as chief centurion, with thirty-four decorations and six civic crowns. He is over fifty, and he will serve wherever he is put (42.34).
  • Two envoys return boasting that they had tricked Perseus into a truce so Rome could get ready. Most senators approve. The older ones say there is nothing Roman in it: their ancestors declared war before they made it and did not glory in cunning. But, as Livy puts it, “that part of the senate who paid more regard to utility than to honour, prevailed” (42.47).
  • Perseus wins the first cavalry battle, at Callinicus, and offers peace on the terms of his father’s treaty. Rome, as always after a defeat, refuses to negotiate (42.57–62).

Book 43 · 171–169 BCE · damaged

Complaints from the Provinces

Roman governors plunder allies, and Livy explains why he still records prodigies.

  • Envoys from both Spanish provinces kneel before the senate and complain that their governors treat them worse than enemies. The senate sets up judges to hear their claims, with Cato, Scipio Nasica, Paullus and Sulpicius Gallus as their advocates; two former governors leave Rome for exile (43.2).
  • Roman commanders in Greece plunder allied cities and sell their people; the senate orders the captives freed (43.4, 43.7–8).
  • Livy stops to explain himself. People now think the gods foretell nothing, so prodigies go unreported and unrecorded. “But for my part, while I am writing the transactions of ancient times, my sentiments, I know not how, become antique,” and he will record what wise men of those days thought worth expiating (43.13).

Book 44 · 169–168 BCE

Paullus and Pydna

An old general silences the armchair strategists and ends Macedon in an afternoon.

  • The elderly consul Marcius Philippus forces his way into Macedonia by a mountain pass near Tempe, and then stalls (44.1–9).
  • Lucius Aemilius Paullus, elected for 168, makes a famous speech. “In every circle, and, truly, at every table, there are people who lead armies into Macedonia,” who know where to camp and when to attack. Let any of them come with him: he will provide “a ship, a horse, a tent; and even his travelling charges”. Otherwise let them not play pilot on dry land (44.22).
  • On the eve of battle a military tribune, Gaius Sulpicius Gallus, warns the soldiers that the moon will be eclipsed that night, from the second hour to the fourth, and that this is natural and predictable. It happens as he says; the Romans think his wisdom almost divine, the Macedonians take it as an omen of a king’s fall (44.37).
  • The battle of Pydna begins by accident in the afternoon, when a horse breaks loose near a river between the camps and men chase it (44.40). Paullus’s legions work into the gaps that open as the phalanx crosses broken ground, and the “many distinct fights” first disorder it and then break it (44.41).
  • Livy gives 20,000 Macedonians killed, 6,000 captured at Pydna and 5,000 more rounded up, and not more than a hundred Romans dead, most of them Pelignian allies who had attacked too early and run onto the spears (44.41–42). Perseus flees to the sanctuary of Samothrace (44.43–46).

Book 45 · 168–167 BCE · the last surviving book

The Circle in the Sand

Macedon is abolished, Epirus is sold, and the victor loses his sons.

A line drawn round a king

  • Perseus surrenders at Samothrace and is brought to Paullus, who lectures his officers on the uncertainty of fortune (45.6–8).
  • In Egypt, Antiochus IV is closing in on Alexandria when a Roman envoy, Popilius Laenas, hands him the senate’s order to withdraw. The king says he will consult his friends. Popilius draws a circle round him in the sand with his staff: “Before you step out of this circle, give me an answer which I may take back to the senate.” After a moment Antiochus says: “I shall do what the senate decrees” (45.12). He leaves Egypt.

The settlement

  • The senate declares the Macedonians and Illyrians free, so that it will be clear “that the forces of the Roman People brought not slavery to free peoples, but on the contrary, freedom to the enslaved”. It also closes the royal mines and estates, because they could not be run without tax contractors, “and where there was a contractor, there either the ownership by the state lapsed, or no freedom was left to the allied people” (45.18).
  • At Amphipolis Paullus announces the terms. Macedonia becomes four republics with their own laws and magistrates, paying Rome half the taxes they had paid the king; no one may marry, or buy land or houses, across their borders; the gold and silver mines stay shut (45.29–30).
  • The same year, on the senate’s orders, Paullus’s army sacks seventy towns of Epirus in a single day and sells 150,000 people into slavery (45.34). Livy records it without comment.

The triumph

  • Paullus’s soldiers, angry at their share of the booty, nearly block his triumph; an old commander’s speech carries it (45.35–39).
  • For three days the spoils of Macedon pass through Rome. Perseus walks “in chains, before the chariot of the general”, with his children; behind the chariot ride Paullus’s two sons who had been adopted into other families, one of them the future Scipio Aemilianus (45.40).
  • His two other sons, the ones he had kept to carry his name, die: the younger, about twelve, five days before the triumph, the elder, fourteen, three days after it. Paullus tells the people that fortune has balanced the public success with his private loss, and that Perseus, a captive, still has his children, while he has none left to bear his name (45.40–41).
  • The last surviving pages show King Prusias of Bithynia arriving in Rome with a shaved head and a freedman’s cap, calling himself the freedman of the Roman people (45.44). Then the manuscript breaks off.

Books 46–142 · Lost

The ninety-seven books we do not have

Everything after 167 BCE survives only as the Periochae, a set of one-paragraph summaries (two of them, for Books 136 and 137, also lost), plus a few quotations and a damaged papyrus summary from Oxyrhynchus. The summaries are enough to show how the work was built, and how much we have lost.

Books 46–142 · 167–9 BCE · known from the summaries

From Carthage Destroyed to the Death of Drusus

The century and a half Livy lived closest to, told at more than a book a year.

  • The pace. Livy gave the 158 years from 167 to 9 BCE ninety-seven books, more than one book for every two years, against five books for the first 363 years. The summary of Book 121 notes that it “is said to have been published after the death of Augustus”, so Livy was still writing about the triumvirs in the last years of his life.
  • What he thought of Caesar and Augustus. We cannot tell from the summaries. The historian Tacitus reports that Livy praised Pompey so warmly that Augustus called him “the Pompeian” and stayed his friend, and the philosopher Seneca says Livy wondered whether Caesar’s birth had done the state more good or harm. That is almost all the evidence.
  • Why it was lost. A work of 142 books on papyrus rolls was expensive to copy; readers used epitomes instead, and the full text survived only in part. The first decade, the third and half the fourth came down in separate manuscript traditions; Books 41–45 in a single damaged manuscript found in 1527 in the abbey of Lorsch.

Most of the early ones turn on someone accepting a private loss, a life, a hand, a daughter, a son, an office, to make a public point about what Rome is. That is not how events usually happen; it is how a moral curriculum is built. These were the exempla a Roman education ran on, and because they were built to be repeated, they survived.

Reading Livy

How Livy works

  • The year as the unit. Livy writes annals: each year opens with its magistrates, provinces and levies, reports prodigies and their expiation, then the wars abroad and the quarrels at home. The frame makes the history feel like a record even where the content is legend, and it is why the early books are full of consuls about whom nothing is known but their names.
  • Other writers, not documents. Livy mostly worked from earlier historians, whom he names when they disagree: Fabius Pictor, “the oldest”; Cincius Alimentus; Calpurnius Piso; Claudius Quadrigarius; Licinius Macer; Valerius Antias; Coelius Antipater; and above all Polybius. He compares them constantly, and says so: the dates of the early Republic cannot be fixed (2.21), the sources give different consuls from the same “linen books” (4.23), family eulogies have falsified the record (8.40), and his own figures for battles are chosen from a spread (10.30, 25.39, 26.49, 29.25, 33.10).
  • But sometimes documents. He cites Augustus reading an inscription on an ancient breastplate (4.20), notes monuments he has seen himself (38.56), refers to speeches of Cato and Scipio that still existed (39.42, 39.52), and in one place dates a death by working out who had replaced whom in the senate’s list (39.52).
  • The speeches. A large share of the text is direct and indirect speech, and none of it was recorded. Following the convention Thucydides set, the historian writes what the moment required. Read them as essays on a decision, not as evidence of what was said.
  • The moral frame. The preface promises a story of discipline slowly giving way, and Livy keeps the promise: foreign luxury is suspect, popular leaders are usually demagogues, the senate is usually right, and old-fashioned honour is preferred to advantage even when advantage wins the vote (42.47). Knowing the bias makes the narrative more useful, not less.
  • Doubt, and the stage. Livy repeatedly separates what is probable from what is merely told. The entrails snatched at Veii are “more fit to be displayed on the stage” (5.21), the naming of the Curtian Lake is uncertain (7.6), the poisoning scandal he would like to disbelieve (8.18). He records prodigies because the men of the time thought them worth recording (43.13).
  • The usual errors. Doublets (the same kind of story told twice, decades apart), late-Republican institutions projected into the fifth century, geography inferred rather than seen, round and inflated numbers, and a few slips, such as dating Carthage’s first army in Sicily to the fifth century, when it had fought there for generations (4.29).

Context

What held up: Livy against the evidence

Livy makes few predictions, but he makes hundreds of datable claims, and two centuries of archaeology, epigraphy and astronomy have tested some of them. The verdicts are our judgement; the findings are well documented.

Held

A settlement on the Palatine in the eighth century Book 1

Post-holes of Iron Age huts on the Palatine show people living there in the period of Livy’s founding date. The city is older than the legend implies, and the legend itself is legend, but the date is not absurd.

Held

Etruscan kings and a great Capitoline temple 1.34–56

Sixth-century Rome shows heavy Etruscan influence, and the podium of the temple of Jupiter, dated to the late sixth century, is enormous, as Livy’s story of Tarquin’s ambition implies.

Partly

Publius Valerius and the first Republic Book 2

An inscription found at Satricum in 1977, from about 500 BCE, names the companions of a Poplios Valesios, a Publius Valerius. It does not prove Publicola, but it shows a man of that name and standing at the right time.

Missed

King Servius’s city wall 1.44

The surviving “Servian” wall is built of stone quarried in Veii’s territory, available only after 396, and is of the fourth century. Livy himself records a contract for a wall of squared stone in 378 (6.32).

Partly

The Gallic sack burned the city and its records 5.41, 6.1

The sack happened, but excavators have not found the city-wide burn layer the story implies, and the claim that almost all records perished may be an explanation for their absence rather than a fact.

Missed

Camillus drives out the Gauls 5.49

Polybius, writing earlier, has the Gauls leave with the gold because their homeland was invaded. The rescue that leaves “not even one person” alive looks like a later patriotic addition.

Held

Hannibal arrived with about 26,000 men 21.38

Livy’s lowest figure matches the one Polybius read on Hannibal’s own bronze tablet, and modern historians accept it. The pass he used is still argued; a 2016 study of an ancient layer of horse dung at the Col de la Traversette is the latest claim.

Held

The senate’s decree against the Bacchanalia 39.14–18

A bronze copy of the decree, found at Tiriolo in Calabria in 1640, survives. Its rules, including the limit of five worshippers at a rite, match Livy’s account closely.

Held

The eclipse before Pydna 44.37

Astronomers date the lunar eclipse Gallus predicted to 21 June 168 BCE. Livy dates it by the Roman calendar to early September, which shows the calendar had drifted about ten weeks ahead of the sun during the wars.

Held

The tomb of the Scipios outside the Capena gate 38.56

The family tomb was rediscovered on the Appian Way in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, with inscribed sarcophagi of the Scipios, where Livy said it was.

Missed

Seven kings in 244 years 1.60

An average reign of 35 years, in an age of short lives and violent deaths, is implausible. Most historians think the list was either shortened or stretched to fit a chronology worked out later.

Context

Where it has been argued with

How much of the early books is true?

Barthold Niebuhr’s Roman History (1811–12) argued that the early stories came from lost ballads, and began the critical tradition. George Cornewall Lewis’s Inquiry into the Credibility of the Early Roman History (1855) rejected nearly everything before the wars with Pyrrhus. R. M. Ogilvie’s commentary on Books 1–5 (1965) traced the stories to their sources and motives. Tim Cornell’s The Beginnings of Rome (1995) argued that the structure of the narrative, the kings, the institutions, the rough sequence, is more reliable than its colourful episodes; Gary Forsythe’s Critical History of Early Rome (2005) is more sceptical again.

Court historian, or independent?

Livy wrote under Augustus, praised the regime’s restoration of temples and morals, and quoted the emperor as a source (4.20). But Tacitus says he praised Pompey so warmly that Augustus called him a Pompeian, and Seneca reports his doubt whether Caesar’s birth had helped or harmed the state. Ronald Syme’s “Livy and Augustus” (1959) made the case for a Paduan provincial who kept his distance; the argument continues, and the lost books that would settle it are the ones we lack.

Historian, or orator?

Ancient readers praised Livy as a stylist: Quintilian spoke of his “milky richness” and set him beside Herodotus, while Asinius Pollio sneered at his “Paduan-ness” and the emperor Caligula called him wordy and careless. Modern scholars ask whether he was trying to find out what happened at all. A. J. Woodman’s Rhetoric in Classical Historiography (1988) argued that ancient history was a branch of rhetoric in which invention was expected; Livy’s own asides show a writer who knew the difference between the probable and the merely told, and chose to tell both.

Was Rome’s empire an accident?

Livy’s fourth decade reads like the story of a reluctant power pulled east by allies’ complaints, and historians from Mommsen to Tenney Frank’s Roman Imperialism (1914) described a Rome that expanded to protect itself. W. V. Harris’s War and Imperialism in Republican Rome (1979) argued the opposite: Roman aristocrats needed war for glory and wealth, and Rome chose it. Livy supplies evidence for both, which is part of his value.

Was the strife of the orders a disease or a cure?

Livy regrets the quarrels between patricians and plebs as disorder that nearly destroyed Rome. Machiavelli, in the Discourses on the First Decade of Titus Livy (written about 1517, printed 1531), argued the reverse: the tumults were the cause of Roman liberty and strength, and the tribunes made Rome free. Two opposite political theories have been built on the same chapters.

Where did Hannibal cross the Alps?

Livy and Polybius give descriptions that fit no pass exactly, and Livy’s route, by way of the river Durance, is hard to square with Polybius’s. Candidates have included the Little St Bernard, Mont Cenis, the Montgenèvre, the Col du Clapier and the Col de la Traversette, argued for by Gavin de Beer in Alps and Elephants (1955) and by a microbiological study of a churned layer of animal dung near the pass published in 2016. There is no consensus.

Reading Livy

What Livy became

For fifteen centuries, “Roman virtue” meant largely the stories in these books. Dante, in the Inferno, calls him “Livy, who does not err”. Petrarch gathered the first, third and fourth decades into a single manuscript, and Lorenzo Valla corrected its text. Books 41–45 were found in 1527 in a single fifth-century manuscript from the abbey of Lorsch and printed at Basel in 1531. In the seventeenth century Johann Freinsheim wrote Latin supplements to fill the lost books.

No ancient historian has had more practical political effect. Machiavelli’s Discourses read Books 1–10 as an engineering manual for durable republics. The American founders knew Cincinnatus and the Horatii from Livy; the French revolutionaries staged Brutus and David painted him. The least reliable part of Livy, the legendary first books, had the largest effect: Lucretia, Brutus, Horatius, Cincinnatus and Verginia are almost certainly not historical people, and they supplied the working examples for two thousand years of argument about liberty and tyranny.

Terms

Glossary

Annals
History written year by year, with each year’s magistrates, wars and prodigies.
Auspices
Signs read from birds or feeding chickens before public acts; at first held to be a patrician right.
Censor
Magistrate, from 443, who took the census, revised the senate’s membership and let public contracts.
Census and lustrum
The count of citizens and their property, closed by a purifying sacrifice, the lustrum.
Comitia centuriata
The assembly organised by Servius’s property classes, which elected consuls and declared war; weighted to the rich.
Consul
One of two chief magistrates, elected for a year; the heart of Livy’s idea of liberty.
Consular tribunes
Military tribunes with consular power, elected in place of consuls in many years between 444 and 367.
Decemvirs
The ten men of 451–449 who wrote the Twelve Tables and then refused to leave office.
Deditio
Formal surrender of a people and all they own to Rome, used by Capua in 343 to obtain protection.
Devotio
A commander’s ritual offering of himself and the enemy to the gods below, as by the two Decii.
Dictator
An emergency magistrate with supreme power for up to six months.
Evocatio
Inviting an enemy city’s god to move to Rome, as Camillus did with Juno of Veii.
Exemplum
A moral example from history, told for imitation or avoidance.
Fetials
Priests who demanded redress from enemies and declared war by ritual.
Hastati, principes, triarii
The three lines of the manipular legion, from youngest to veterans.
Maniple
A company of the legion, able to move on its own; the key to beating the phalanx.
Nexum
Debt bondage, in which a debtor pledged his own body; abolished by the Poetelian law of 326.
Patricians and plebeians
Rome’s hereditary aristocracy and everyone else; their conflict fills Books 2–10.
Periochae
Short ancient summaries of each book, our main source for the lost ones.
Phalanx
The deep Macedonian formation of long pikes: overwhelming from the front, clumsy on broken ground or when attacked from behind.
Prodigy
An unnatural event, such as a rain of stones or a talking cow, read as a sign of divine anger and expiated by the state.
Proconsul, prorogation
Extending a magistrate’s command beyond his year, first done in 326.
Secession
The plebeians’ strike: leaving the city and refusing military service.
Spolia opima
Armour taken by a Roman commander from an enemy commander he killed himself; won only three times.
Tribune of the plebs
A sacrosanct officer of the plebs who could protect citizens and veto magistrates.

Using it

How to read him

  1. Start with Hannibal (21–30)

    The best-told and most reliable decade, and it needs no background. Book 22, Trasimene to Cannae, is the single best book to begin with.

  2. Then the preface and Books 1–5

    The legends, read as national myth. Book 5, Veii to the Gallic sack, is the finest single book of the first pentad.

  3. Then the East (31–45)

    If the Hellenistic world interests you: Polybius’s story with a Roman conscience. Read Books 33, 39 and 44–45 if you read nothing else.

  4. Rome and Italy (6–10) last

    The hardest going and the structural answer to why the rest happened. Books 8 and 9 are the ones to read.

  5. Keep the moral frame in view

    Foreign luxury is always suspect, popular leaders are usually demagogues, the senate is usually right. Knowing the bias makes the narrative more useful.

Read alongside

  • Polybius, The Histories: the same wars from the Greek side, with the constitutional analysis Livy never attempts.
  • Machiavelli, Discourses on Livy (c. 1517): the most consequential reading of Books 1–10.
  • Mary Beard, SPQR (2015): a modern history of Rome that shows what survives of Livy’s stories under scrutiny.
  • The Story of Civilization, summarised on this site: Volume III, Caesar and Christ, tells the Republic in the long view.
  • A History of Warfare, summarised on this site: the legion and the phalanx in the history of battle.
