The History of Rome

Ab Urbe Condita: from the founding of the city to the conquest of the Mediterranean

“What chiefly makes the study of history wholesome and profitable is this, that you behold the lessons of every kind of experience set forth as on a conspicuous monument.”

Livy, Preface (B. O. Foster’s translation)

Titus Livius · c. 59 BCE – AD 17

The whole book in one breath

Livy spent some forty years writing Rome’s history from its first day, and he never held office, commanded a soldier or, so far as we know, left Italy. What he produced is a national epic in prose, organised year by year, whose declared purpose is to show readers what to imitate and what to avoid. Of 142 books, 35 survive. They cover the legendary kings and early Republic, Rome’s conquest of Italy, the war with Hannibal, and the subjugation of the Greek East. The early books are myth; the later ones, where Livy follows the Greek historian Polybius, are history in a recognisable sense. Throughout runs one thesis: a poor, disciplined Rome conquered the world and was then corrupted by what it won.

The book

142 books, 35 left

Ab Urbe Condita Libri, “books from the founding of the city,” was published in instalments across Livy’s working life, from the 20s BCE into the reign of Tiberius. It began with Aeneas and ended in 9 BCE. Roughly three-quarters is gone.

The losses aren’t random in effect. We have Rome’s legendary origins and its conquest of Italy and the Mediterranean. We have lost his account of the Gracchi, Marius, Sulla, Caesar and Augustus: the part he lived closest to, and the part historians most want.

Livy’s scale also expands as his sources thicken. The first five books average about seventy years each, and the Hannibal books under two. He isn’t compressing for effect. He simply has almost nothing to work with at the start, and his preface admits the early material is closer to poetry than evidence.

If you remember nothing else

Six lessons the surviving books teach

Books 6–10, 21–30

Rome’s edge was institutional

It lost battles constantly. No rival could match a citizenship that turned defeated enemies into future soldiers, or a Senate that raised another army after losing tens of thousands in a day.

Books 21–30

The annual magistracy cut both ways

It prevented tyranny for centuries and handed Hannibal three victories. Against him Rome learned to keep good commanders in post, and the road to the Caesars begins there.

Books 1–5

The early books describe Livy’s Rome

They tell you what the Augustan age believed about its ancestors. That is a real fact about a real period, just not the one described.

Books 39–45

Decline has a date in the text

Livy places the turn in the 180s BCE, at the moment of greatest success, when eastern money arrives, the same argument Sallust and later Gibbon make.

Books 31–45

Empire before anyone admitted it

Rome declared Greece free in 196 and abolished Macedon in 167. Livy shows the whole sequence without deciding whether the reluctance was real.

Afterlife

What survived shaped Europe

For fifteen centuries “Roman virtue” meant the stories in this text, and Machiavelli built a theory of republics on the first ten books.

Books 1–5 · The Early History of Rome

From Aeneas to the Gallic sack

The most famous and least reliable pages Livy wrote, and he knows it. Family tradition, priestly records, Greek speculation and patriotic invention become the founding stories every educated Roman knew by heart. As evidence they are nearly worthless. As a portrait of what Rome wanted to be, they are the most valuable thing in the book.

Book 1 · 753–509 BCE

The Seven Kings

A constitutional argument told as biography.

  • Each king supplies an institution, so every Roman custom gets a father (see the table below).
  • Romulus and Remus, the rape of the Sabine women and Numa’s religious calendar come first. Two Etruscan kings bring the engineering, and a tyrant whose family’s crime against Lucretia justifies the Republic comes last.
  • Brutus leads the expulsion of Tarquin the Proud in 509 BCE. From then on Rome is ruled by two annually elected consuls.

Books 2–4 · 509–406 BCE

The Republic Learns to Argue

A two-hundred-year quarrel between patricians and plebeians that never quite becomes a revolution.

  • The plebeians’ weapon is not violence but secession. They walk out of the city and refuse army service, and since Rome is always at war, they are always needed back.
  • Each walkout ends in a concession, and the concessions add up to a constitution (see the figure below).
  • Livy’s patrician sympathies are plain. He dislikes demagogues and admires senatorial restraint, yet the story he tells is one of steady plebeian victory.
  • The episodes include Coriolanus, turned back from attacking Rome by his mother, and Cincinnatus, called from his plough to be dictator, who won his war and resigned within sixteen days.

Book 5 · 406–390 BCE

Veii, and Then the Gauls

The best-built of the early books turns on one reversal.

  • Rome takes Veii after a ten-year siege, a deliberate echo of Troy with a tunnel instead of a horse, and doubles its territory.
  • Camillus, the victor, is exiled on a charge of mishandling the spoils. Within a few years a Gallic army destroys the Roman force at the Allia and burns the city. Only the Capitol holds, saved when the sacred geese wake the defenders.
  • Brennus throws his sword onto the scale where the ransom gold is weighed and says “vae victis,” woe to the conquered.
  • Camillus returns, drives the Gauls out and talks the Romans out of abandoning their ruins for Veii. The sack also explains why nothing earlier can be verified: the records burned.

Books 6–10 · Rome and Italy

The century that made a peninsula Roman

The collection readers skip, and the one that answers the question the others raise: how did Rome survive defeats that would have finished any other state?

Books 6–8 · 390–322 BCE

Turning Enemies into Soldiers

Rome’s real invention wasn’t a weapon. It was a way of absorbing the defeated.

  • Rebuilding after the Gauls, Rome fought its neighbours year after year and bound them into a web of alliances, citizenships and colonies.
  • After the Latin War of 340–338 BCE, the settlement gave defeated communities graded rights and obliged them to supply soldiers.
  • Livy never calls it a policy. It builds up settlement by settlement, and it is why Hannibal could later destroy three Roman armies and still not win: Rome could draw on a manpower pool of perhaps 700,000.

Books 9–10 · 321–293 BCE

The Samnite Wars

A confederation of hill peoples beat Rome often and lost anyway.

  • In 321 BCE a whole consular army marched into a blind valley at the Caudine Forks, was trapped without a battle, and was released only after passing under the yoke, a ritual humiliation Livy describes with real pain.
  • Rome then repudiated the treaty on a technicality and handed back the officers who had sworn it. Livy is uncomfortable with the manoeuvre and reports it anyway, one of the places where his honesty beats his patriotism.
  • The flexible manipular legion, with its lines graded by age and wealth, grew out of mountain war. It would later meet and defeat the Macedonian phalanx.
  • At Sentinum in 295 BCE, Rome beat a coalition of Samnites, Etruscans, Umbrians and Gauls in the largest battle yet fought in Italy. By 290 the Samnites made peace.
  • Book 9 includes a famous digression asking what would have happened if Alexander the Great had turned west. Livy’s answer: Rome would have won.

Books 21–30 · The War with Hannibal

Ten books on seventeen years

The collection that made Livy’s name. The pace slows to about two years a book, the sources are good, and the material is the best story in ancient history: a general crosses the Alps with elephants, destroys three Roman armies in three years, and can’t finish the job, because Rome’s answer to catastrophe is to raise another army and refuse to discuss terms.

Book 21 · 219–218 BCE

Over the Alps

The most famous march in history, and what it cost.

  • The war opens with Hannibal’s siege of Saguntum in Spain, a Roman ally, and his decision to take the war to Italy overland.
  • The crossing of the Pyrenees, the Rhône and the Alps in late autumn cost him more men than any Roman army would take from him (see the figure below).
  • Livy’s route over the Alps doesn’t fit any real pass exactly. It is the famous case of his geography by inference.

Books 21–22 · 218–216 BCE

Three Defeats in Three Years

Three different tricks, each a lesson in how a Roman commander gets himself killed.

  • Trebia, 218: Hannibal provokes hungry men into crossing an icy river at dawn, then springs a force hidden in a watercourse.
  • Lake Trasimene, 217: the largest ambush in recorded history, laid along a lake shore in morning fog.
  • Cannae, 216: harder still. Hannibal wins a straight fight in the open, outnumbered nearly two to one, by letting his centre give way and closing his wings around the Roman army. It was the worst single day any European army has suffered.
  • Behind each defeat is the same institutional weakness: a consul with a one-year command, needing a victory before his term ends, facing a general with no deadline.

Books 23–30 · 216–201 BCE

Sixteen Years of Not Losing

Hannibal wins the battles and loses the map.

  • Fabius Maximus, “the Delayer,” refuses battle and shadows Hannibal, buying the time in which Rome can afford to grow a Scipio.
  • Rome retakes Capua and Syracuse, where Archimedes dies in the sack. Every Carthaginian success is a battle, and every Roman success is a place taken and held.
  • The young Scipio takes New Carthage in Spain and destroys Carthage’s Spanish empire. The Senate, learning slowly and expensively, now keeps good commanders in post for years.
  • Scipio invades Africa, and Hannibal is recalled and beaten at Zama in 202. Livy gives him a dignified exit: an interview with Scipio before the battle, and afterwards advice to Carthage to accept terms that strip it of its fleet, its empire and the right to make war.

Books 31–45 · Rome and the Mediterranean

Thirty-four years to one master

Rome finishes the Hannibalic war exhausted and immediately starts another. Within a generation it breaks Macedon twice and humbles the Seleucid empire, while annexing almost nothing and insisting it wants nothing. This is largely Polybius, translated and reshaped, and the best surviving account of how the Mediterranean got a single master.

Books 31–38 · 200–188 BCE

Macedon, Then Antioch

The legion beats the phalanx, and Rome declares the Greeks free.

  • At Cynoscephalae in 197, two armies collide in fog on a ridge. Half the Macedonian phalanx forms up and is unstoppable. A Roman tribune takes twenty maniples from the winning wing and hits it from behind. The ancient world’s decisive weapon stops being decisive.
  • At the Isthmian Games in 196, Flamininus has a herald proclaim the Greek cities free, with no garrisons and no tribute. Livy records the crowd’s disbelief.
  • Antiochus III comes into Greece claiming Rome’s “freedom” is a fraud. He is thrown out at Thermopylae in 191 and broken at Magnesia. The peace of Apamea in 188 confines his empire behind the Taurus mountains and imposes a huge indemnity.

Books 39–45 · 187–167 BCE

Pydna, and the Anxiety at Home

Victory abroad, and the first complaints about what victory brings.

  • The Third Macedonian War ends at Pydna in 168. Perseus’s phalanx advances until the ground breaks it up, and Aemilius Paullus feeds maniples into the gaps. The battle lasts about an hour.
  • Macedon is not released this time. It is abolished and divided into four republics forbidden to trade or intermarry with each other.
  • At home, the wealth starts arriving and so do complaints about luxury, foreign religion and the decay of the old discipline. In 186 the Senate suppresses the secret Bacchic cult across Italy, with thousands put to death. Livy tells it as moral contagion from the East. It reads today as a state discovering it can police belief.
  • Book 45 ends in 167 with Paullus’s triumph and the captive king walking behind the chariot. Everything Livy wrote after that is lost.

Reading Livy

The set pieces, and their afterlives

Most readers already know several of Livy’s episodes without knowing they are his. They sit in the first books and cite by book and section, which works in any translation.

EpisodeWhereWhat happens, and what it became
Lucretia1.57–59The king’s son assaults a nobleman’s wife. She names him, exacts an oath of vengeance and kills herself, and the monarchy falls. It became the founding text of tyrannicide, painted by Botticelli, Titian and Rembrandt.
Horatius at the bridge2.10Three men hold the Tiber bridge against an army while it is cut down behind them. Macaulay’s Lays of Ancient Rome put it in every Victorian schoolroom.
Mucius Scaevola2.12A captured assassin holds his right hand in a fire to show what Roman resolve is worth.
Coriolanus2.33–40An exiled general leads an enemy army against Rome and is turned back by his mother. Shakespeare’s play follows Plutarch, but the shape is here.
Cincinnatus3.26–29A farmer made dictator wins his war in sixteen days and returns to his plough. Washington’s resignation was read this way, and Cincinnati is named after the society that took the point.
Verginia3.44–48A magistrate schemes to have a free girl declared a slave. Her father kills her rather than let it happen, and the decemvirs fall.
The geese5.47A night assault on the Capitol is betrayed by the sacred geese, and the last defenders hold.
The Caudine Forks9.1–6An army trapped without a battle is sent under the yoke. It is the uncomfortable one, proof that Livy isn’t simply a patriot.

Six of the eight turn on someone accepting a private loss (a life, a hand, a daughter, an office) to make a public point about what Rome is. That is not how events usually happen. It is how a moral curriculum is built: these are the exempla a Roman education ran on. Almost certainly none happened as told, and precisely because they were built to be quoted, they survived being quoted.

Reading Livy

Where the text comes from

  • The annalists. Livy worked from earlier Roman histories, from Fabius Pictor to near-contemporaries like Valerius Antias. That tradition grew with each generation, the opposite of what honest evidence does, and was written by and for the families whose ancestors star in it. Livy distrusts Antias’s inflated casualty figures and says so, but accepts the structure around them.
  • Polybius. For the Hannibalic war and the East he had a far better source: a Greek hostage in Rome who knew the Scipios, walked the battlefields and interviewed participants. Where Livy follows Polybius, the geography becomes coherent and the analysis sharp. The reliability of any passage depends mostly on which source he is using.
  • The speeches. About a fifth of the text is direct speech, and none of it was recorded. Following Thucydides’s convention, the historian writes what the situation demanded. Read them as an essayist’s dramatisations of a decision, not as evidence.
  • The usual errors. Doublets (one battle told twice, decades apart), late-Republican institutions projected onto the fifth century, geography inferred rather than seen, and army sizes central Italy couldn’t have fed.

Reading Livy

What Livy became

No ancient historian has had more practical political effect. Machiavelli’s Discourses on the First Decade of Titus Livy (around 1517) is a chapter-by-chapter commentary on Books 1–10, reading Livy’s moral narrative as an engineering manual for durable states.

Machiavelli’s conclusion inverts Livy’s. Livy deplores the conflict between patricians and plebeians as disorder, while Machiavelli argues that the tumult was the source of Roman liberty and strength, and that the tribunate made Rome free. Two different political theories can be built from the same chapters, and both have been.

The least reliable part of Livy, the legendary first books, had the largest effect on later history. Lucretia, Brutus, Horatius, Cincinnatus and Verginia are almost certainly not historical people, and they supplied the working examples for two thousand years of argument about liberty and tyranny.

Terms

Glossary

Annals
History written year by year, with each year’s magistrates, wars and prodigies.
Patricians / plebeians
Rome’s hereditary aristocracy and the rest of the citizen body.
Secession
The plebeians’ strike: leaving the city and refusing military service.
Tribune of the plebs
An officer who could veto magistrates to protect plebeians.
Consul
One of two chief magistrates, elected for a single year.
Dictator
An emergency magistrate with supreme power for up to six months.
Maniple
A flexible sub-unit of the legion, the key to beating the phalanx.
Phalanx
The dense Macedonian pike formation: overwhelming from the front, fragile on broken ground.
Exemplum
A moral example from history, taught for imitation or avoidance.
Periochae
Short ancient summaries of Livy’s books, our main source for the lost ones.

Using it

How to read him

  1. Start with Hannibal (21–30)

    The best written, the most reliable, and it needs no context.

  2. Then Early Rome (1–5)

    The legends, read as national myth. Book 5 is the finest single book Livy wrote.

  3. Then the East (31–45)

    If the Hellenistic world interests you: Polybius with a Roman conscience.

  4. Rome and Italy (6–10) last

    The hardest going, and the structural answer to why the rest happened.

  5. Keep the moral frame in view

    Foreign luxury is always suspect, popular leaders are usually demagogues, and the Senate is usually right. Knowing the bias makes the narrative more useful, not less.

Read alongside

  • Polybius, The Histories: the same wars from the Greek side, with the constitutional analysis Livy never attempts.
  • Machiavelli, Discourses on Livy (c. 1517): the most consequential reading of Books 1–10.
  • The Story of Civilization, Volume III, summarised on this site: the Republic in the long view.
  • A History of Warfare, summarised on this site: the legion and the phalanx in the history of battle.
