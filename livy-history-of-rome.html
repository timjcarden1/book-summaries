The four surviving collections

The History
of Rome From the founding of the city to the conquest of the Mediterranean — 142 books written, 35 surviving, and the whole Roman self-image built out of them.

Livy spent forty years writing a history of Rome from its first day, and never held office, commanded a soldier or, so far as we know, left Italy. What he produced is not research in the modern sense: it is a national epic in prose, organised year by year, whose declared purpose is to show a reader what to imitate and what to avoid. It is also, for the first five centuries of Rome, very nearly the only continuous account we have — which is the permanent difficulty and the permanent fascination of reading it.

Orientation

The Book, and What Is Left of It

What Livy set out to do, and how much of it reached us.

Ab Urbe Condita Libri — "Books from the Founding of the City" — ran to 142 books and was published in instalments across Livy's working life, from about 29 BCE into the reign of Tiberius. It began with Aeneas and ended in 9 BCE. Roughly three-quarters of it is gone.

What Livy thought he was doing

The preface is short and unusually candid. Livy admits the early material is closer to poetry than to evidence, declines to argue about it, and states a purpose that is frankly moral: the reader should look at the record as at a monument, and take from it models to copy and warnings to avoid. The frame is one of decline — a hard, poor, disciplined Rome that conquered the world and was then corrupted by what it won.

"What chiefly makes the study of history wholesome and profitable is this, that you behold the lessons of every kind of experience set forth as on a conspicuous monument."Livy · Preface

These are the most famous and least reliable pages Livy wrote, and he knows it. The material is family tradition, priestly record, Greek speculation and patriotic invention, and out of it he assembles the founding stories every educated Roman afterwards had by heart: Romulus and Remus, the Sabine women, Horatius at the bridge, Mucius Scaevola's hand in the fire, Cincinnatus at the plough, the geese of the Capitol. Read as evidence they are nearly worthless; read as a portrait of what Rome wanted to be, they are the most valuable thing in the book.

Bks 2–4

The Republic Learns to Argue

509 – 406 BCE

With the kings gone, Livy's subject becomes a two-hundred-year quarrel between patricians and plebeians that never quite becomes a revolution. The plebs' weapon is not violence but secession: they walk out of the city and refuse to serve in the army, and since Rome is always at war, they are always needed back. Each walkout ends in a concession, and the concessions accumulate into a constitution.

The moral set-pieces, and what they are for Horatius holding the bridge alone; Mucius Scaevola burning off his own right hand to show Porsenna what Romans are; Cincinnatus taking the dictatorship, winning in sixteen days and returning to his four acres; Verginius killing his own daughter rather than let a magistrate take her. Each is almost certainly invented. Each dramatises one virtue — constantia, fortitudo, modestia, pudicitia — and each was told to Roman schoolboys for the next five hundred years. Livy is manufacturing a civic character, and he says in the preface that this is the point of history.
Bk 5

Veii, and Then the Gauls

406 – 390 BCE

Book 5 is the best-constructed of the five and turns on a single reversal. Rome takes Veii after a ten-year siege — a deliberate echo of Troy, complete with a tunnel instead of a horse — and doubles its territory. Camillus, the victor, is then exiled on a charge of mishandling the spoils. Within five years a Gallic army destroys the Roman force at the Allia and burns the city; only the Capitol holds, saved by the sacred geese. Camillus is recalled, drives the Gauls out, and talks the Romans out of abandoning the ruins for Veii.

Brennus threw his sword onto the scale with the gold and said the word Livy makes famous: vae victis — woe to the conquered. Rome spent the next three centuries making sure the phrase was said by Romans.Book 5 · the Gallic ransom

This is the collection people skip, and it contains the answer to the question the other three raise: how did Rome absorb defeats that would have finished any other state? The answer is worked out here, in a hundred years of unglamorous fighting against the Samnites — a confederation of hill peoples who beat Rome repeatedly and still lost, because Rome had found a way to turn conquered enemies into soldiers.

Bks 7–10

The Samnite Wars

343 – 290 BCE

Three wars over fifty years, fought in mountains that suited the enemy, and Livy does not hide that Rome lost a great many of the engagements. The central episode is the disaster at the Caudine Forks in 321, where an entire consular army marched into a blind valley, was trapped without a battle, and was released only after passing under the yoke — a ritual humiliation Livy describes at length and with real pain.

The collection closes at Sentinum in 295, where Rome faced a coalition of Samnites, Etruscans, Umbrians and Gauls and won the largest battle fought in Italy to that date. Livy gives it the full set-piece treatment, including the second devotio of a Decius Mus — a consul formally dedicating himself and the enemy to the gods of the underworld and riding into their line to die. Three years later the Samnites make peace, and Italy south of the Po is Rome's.

This is the collection that made Livy's name and still justifies it. The narrative pace slows to roughly two years a book, the sources are good, and the material is the best story in ancient history: a Carthaginian general crosses the Alps with an army and elephants, destroys three Roman armies in three years — the third in the worst single day any European army has suffered — and then cannot finish the job, because Rome's answer to catastrophe is to raise another army and decline to discuss terms.

Bks 21–22

Three Defeats in Three Years

218 – 216 BCE

Hannibal's first three victories are three different tricks, and Livy narrates each as a lesson in how a Roman commander gets himself killed. At the Trebia he provokes a crossing of an icy river at dawn by hungry men, then springs a force hidden in a watercourse. At Trasimene he sets the largest ambush in recorded history along a lake shore in morning fog. At Cannae he does something harder: he wins a straight fight, in the open, outnumbered nearly two to one.

What Rome did next Refused to ransom the survivors. Forbade the word "peace" to be spoken in public. Enrolled slaves, debtors and boys under seventeen. Reduced the property qualification for service. And formally thanked the defeated consul Varro, in Livy's most quoted sentence of the war, "because he had not despaired of the republic." Hannibal won the battle in an afternoon and then discovered he had no way to convert it into a victory.

Theme

Rome finishes the Hannibalic war exhausted and immediately declares another, on Philip V of Macedon. Within thirty-four years it has broken Macedon twice, humbled the Seleucid empire, dismantled both, and annexed almost none of it. Livy's account — largely Polybius, translated and reshaped — is the best surviving narrative of how the Mediterranean acquired a single master, and of how uneasy that master was about saying so.

Bks 31–38

Macedon, Then Antioch

200 – 188 BCE

The Second Macedonian War is decided at Cynoscephalae in 197, on a ridge in fog, when the two armies collide before either has deployed. Half the Macedonian phalanx forms up and is unstoppable; the other half is caught still marching. A Roman tribune takes twenty maniples out of the winning line, turns them round, and hits the successful phalanx from behind. It is the moment the ancient world's decisive weapon stops being decisive, and Livy — following Polybius — explains why at length.

In 196, at the Isthmian Games at Corinth, Flamininus has a herald proclaim the Greek cities free — no garrisons, no tribute, no governor. Livy records the crowd's disbelief and the birds falling out of the air at the noise. Two years later Rome is at war with Antiochus III, who has come into Greece at the invitation of the Aetolians on the grounds that Rome's freedom is a fraud. He is thrown out of Thermopylae in 191 and broken at Magnesia in 190, and the peace of Apamea in 188 confines the Seleucid empire behind the Taurus and imposes an indemnity so large it takes a generation to pay.

Bks 39–45

Pydna, and the Anxiety at Home

188 – 167 BCE

The last books run two stories in parallel. Abroad, the Third Macedonian War ends at Pydna in 168, where Perseus's phalanx advances irresistibly across a plain until the ground breaks up beneath it, gaps open, and Aemilius Paullus feeds maniples into them. Livy says Paullus later admitted he had never seen anything so terrifying as that phalanx coming on. The battle lasts about an hour.

At home, the mood is anything but triumphant. Livy's Roman reader in the age of Augustus is meant to notice that this is precisely when the wealth starts arriving, and precisely when the complaints about luxury, foreign religion and the decay of the old discipline begin.

Method

How to Read Him

What Livy does that a modern historian would not, and why he does it.
01

The speeches are composed, not reported

Nobody transcribed Camillus. The speeches follow the Greek historiographical convention: the author writes what the situation demanded, to reveal character and lay out the arguments on both sides. They are the most artful thing in Livy and the least evidentiary. Read them as an essayist's dramatisation of a decision.

02

The numbers inflate, especially early

Livy inherited annalists — Valerius Antias above all — who multiplied enemy dead freely, and he sometimes says so, reporting two figures and declining to choose. Where Polybius survives alongside him, the two differ by up to fifty per cent. Treat every casualty and army figure as an order of magnitude.

03

He is not a researcher

He worked from earlier literary histories rather than from archives, does not appear to have consulted the linen rolls or the priestly records directly, and never visited a battlefield he describes. His originality is in shaping and in prose, not in discovery. Tacitus, a working senator, is a different kind of writer entirely.

04

The moral frame does real work

The thesis — that Rome rose through discipline and poverty and is being destroyed by the wealth of its conquests — shapes what gets emphasis. Foreign luxury is always suspect; popular leaders are usually demagogues; the Senate is usually right. Knowing the bias makes the narrative more useful, not less.

05

And yet he reports against himself

He records the Caudine Forks repudiation, the massacre at New Carthage, the cynicism of the Greek settlement and the brutality in Epirus. Where his sources disagree he often says so. He is a partisan witness who does not suppress inconvenient testimony — which is precisely why he remains usable.

A practical reading order
  • Start with The War with Hannibal (Books 21–30). It is the best written, the most reliable, and it needs no prior context.
  • Then The Early History of Rome (Books 1–5) for the legends, read as national myth rather than as record. Book 5 is the finest single book Livy wrote.
  • Rome and the Mediterranean (Books 31–45) next, if the Hellenistic world interests you — it is Polybius with a Roman conscience attached.
  • Rome and Italy (Books 6–10) last. It is the hardest going and contains the structural answer to why any of the rest happened.
  • Read alongside: Polybius, Books 1–6, for the same events from the Greek side and for the constitutional analysis Livy never attempts.
  1. Rome's advantage was institutional, not military.It lost battles constantly. What no rival could match was a citizenship that turned defeated enemies into future soldiers, and a Senate that could raise another army after losing 50,000 men in a day.
  2. The annual magistracy was a weapon and a wound.It prevented tyranny for four centuries and handed Hannibal three victories. The war against him is where Rome quietly learns to keep good commanders in post — and where the road to the Caesars begins.
  3. Livy's early books are evidence about Livy's Rome, not early Rome.They tell you what the Augustan age believed about its ancestors, which is a real historical fact about a real period — just not the period being described.
  4. The moral decline thesis has a date in the text.Livy locates the turn in the 180s, at the moment of maximum success, when eastern money arrives. Whether or not the analysis holds, it is the same argument Sallust, Cato and later Gibbon all make.
  5. Empire arrived before anyone admitted wanting it.Rome declared Greece free in 196 and abolished Macedon in 167. Livy shows the whole sequence without ever quite resolving whether the reluctance was genuine.
  6. What survives shaped what Europe knew.Machiavelli's Discourses is a commentary on the first ten books. For fifteen hundred years, "Roman virtue" meant the specific stories in this specific text — which is a great deal of influence for a writer who never checked an archive.
