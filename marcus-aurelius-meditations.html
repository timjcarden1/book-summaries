Meditations

Ta eis heauton, “to himself”

“Soon you will have forgotten the world, and soon the world will have forgotten you.”

Book 7.21

Marcus Aurelius · written c. 170–180, in Greek

The whole book in one breath

This is the private notebook of the most powerful man on earth, and he isn’t explaining anything to anyone. Writing at night on campaign, in Greek, Marcus tells himself the same handful of things over and over, because he keeps failing to remember them. Separate what is up to you (your judgements, intentions and conduct) from what isn’t (everything else). The injury is in your judgement of an event, not the event. Other people behave badly out of ignorance, and they are your kin anyway. Everything passes, you will die soon, and so will everyone who remembers you, so act well now. Most readers’ consolation is not that Marcus had answers, but that a man with absolute power and thirty years of practice still struggled with anger, procrastination, vanity and the fear of death.

The life behind the notebook

An heir for twenty-three years, an emperor for nineteen

Marcus was born in Rome in 121 into a wealthy senatorial family. Hadrian noticed him young, nicknamed him Verissimus, “most truthful,” and in 138 arranged for his successor Antoninus Pius to adopt both Marcus and Lucius Verus. Marcus was seventeen, and he waited twenty-three years to rule, studying philosophy so intensely that his rhetoric tutor Fronto complained. Their surviving letters show a different Marcus: chatty, affectionate, fussing about his sore throat.

Becoming emperor in 161, he insisted the Senate make Lucius his equal co-emperor, the first joint rule in Roman history. Then almost everything went wrong at once: war with Parthia, a pandemic brought home by the army, a permanent crisis on the Danube, the revolt of his most trusted general, and the deaths of his wife and most of his children. He wrote this notebook in the last decade of it, mostly in camp, in Greek, the language of philosophy rather than government.

It was never meant to be read. It has no argument, no structure and no audience, and the title we use isn’t his. That is precisely what makes it useful.

If you remember nothing else

Seven things to keep

The discipline of assent

The injury is in the judgement

Between what happens and how you feel sits one step, your assent, and it is the only place you have any purchase.

The dichotomy of control

Separate what is yours, often

Your judgement, intention and conduct are yours. Your body, reputation, other people and whether the plan works are not. The discipline is applying it to today’s problem.

Action

Obligation survives the loss of control

That outcomes aren’t up to you doesn’t release you from acting well. This separates Stoicism from fatalism, and modern summaries drop it.

Other people

Difficult people are mistaken, not enemies

Treat wrongdoing as a failure of understanding. True or not, holding the thought makes anger hard to sustain.

Scale

Get the size of things right

Death, deep time, the view from above and the plain physical description of a prized object all shrink what fear or rank has inflated.

Time

Only the present can be lost

The past is spent and the future isn’t yours yet, so the present is all that is actually at stake.

Practice

He never finished

Book 12 tells him what Book 2 told him. There is no arrival. That a man ran the practice for a decade and still needed it is the book’s most reassuring fact.

Book 1

Debts and Lessons

A list of what he owes to each person who formed him, and the only book that isn’t an argument with himself.

  • Marcus goes through grandfather, parents, tutors, philosophers, his adoptive father and finally the gods, and names for each the specific quality he took from them.
  • The lessons are unglamorous and mostly about restraint: not to take sides at the races, not to correct people’s grammar in public, not to plead “I’m too busy” to dodge obligations, not to be taken in by wonder-workers.
  • The philosopher Rusticus told him his character needed work, steered him away from showy rhetoric, and lent him his own copy of Epictetus. Almost everything in the other eleven books descends from that loan.
  • Antoninus Pius gets the longest entry, a portrait of the ruler Marcus intended to be: steady, unmoved by flattery, willing to hear opposition, never rushed and never dithering, the same in private as in public.
  • The last entry thanks the gods for what went right: good family and teachers, never being told he lacked the means to help someone, not getting further in rhetoric and poetry. Many scholars think Book 1 was written separately, perhaps last.

The system

What He Takes from Stoicism

A practitioner’s kit, not an introduction to the school.

  • Stoicism was four centuries old, with a full physics, logic and ethics. Marcus uses little of the first two. Mostly through Epictetus, he takes a small set of operations he can run on himself when angry, frightened or reluctant to get out of bed.
  • The school had no social entry requirement. Its founders included a Cypriot merchant, and Marcus learned it from Epictetus, a man who had been a slave.
  • The core distinction is between what is up to us and what isn’t (see the figure below). The operational core is that emotions follow judgements, and judgements can be examined, which modern cognitive therapy rediscovered in the twentieth century.
  • Pierre Hadot’s reading has become standard: these are spiritual exercises, not doctrines. Almost every entry in Books 2–12 exercises one of three disciplines: of assent (judgements), of desire (accepting what happens), and of action (serving others justly).

Physics

Providence or Atoms

He never decides, and shows his ethics survive either answer.

  • Orthodox Stoicism held that the cosmos is one rational, living whole, and that accepting what happens is recognition, not resignation.
  • Marcus keeps raising the alternative: either providence, or Epicurean atoms colliding by chance with no plan. He states the choice in at least nine places and never settles it.
  • His conclusions hold either way. If there is providence, complaint is incoherent. If there are only atoms, nothing is owed to you, and your own mind is all the more worth governing. The ethics bear the load and the metaphysics is scaffolding. That is why the book works for readers who share none of his cosmology.
  • He leans hard on Heraclitus’s flux: everything is turning into something else, and the courts of Vespasian and Trajan, full of people marrying, quarrelling and scheming, are gone. That consoles, since whatever is unbearable will pass, and it deflates, since whatever you chase will pass too. The one thing exempt is how you behave now.

Book 11.18

Ten Things to Remember When Angry

The one numbered list in the book, and the closest it comes to instructions for a bad afternoon.

  1. We were made for one another, and I was set over them as a leader.
  2. Consider what these people are like at table and in bed, and what their opinions compel them to do.
  3. If they are right, don’t be vexed. If wrong, they act involuntarily, out of ignorance.
  4. You often do wrong yourself and are much like them.
  5. You can’t be sure they are doing wrong at all. Much is done for reasons you can’t see.
  6. Life is very short, and soon we will all be laid out dead.
  7. It isn’t their acts that disturb us, but our judgements of them. Take away the judgement and the anger goes.
  8. Anger and grief do us more harm than the things we are angry about.
  9. Kindness, if sincere, is unbeatable: correct them gently, and without a lecture.
  10. To expect bad people not to do wrong is madness.

Our paraphrase. Item 7 is the principle and the rest apply it. Only item 9 says what to do; the other nine are about how to see, which fits the whole method, since the intervention point is the judgement. Several items, like the Socratic claim in item 3, Marcus asserts rather than proves. He is assembling thoughts that, held together at the right moment, make anger harder to keep up.

Obsessions

What He Keeps Telling Himself

A man doesn’t write the same reminder for ten years unless he keeps needing it.

  • Death, constantly. Augustus and his court are “all dead, all buried.” Alexander and his mule-driver came to the same end. Don’t act as if you will live ten thousand years. Mortality is his scaling device: set against it, a courtier’s slight shrinks to nothing, and the present hour becomes the whole stock of life.
  • Other people. Book 2 opens by warning him that today he will meet meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest people. The part usually left out: they act from ignorance of good and bad, they are his kin through shared reason, and so they can’t injure him and he can’t hate them. Wrongdoing is treated like an arithmetic mistake. Easy to hold in the abstract, and nearly impossible when someone is being intolerable.
  • The obstacle. At 5.20, what stands in the way becomes the way. It isn’t a slogan about grit. An obstruction to one purpose is material for another, because the virtue in practice is responding well to what is in front of you.
  • Getting out of bed. At 5.1, at dawn, he argues with himself: he rises to do the work of a human being, so was he made to lie under the blankets and keep warm? “But this is more pleasant.” Then do you exist for pleasure? The ruler of Rome, at war, in his fifties, arguing with himself about the snooze button, in several books.

A guide to the twelve books

The books have no titles, themes or order, so the usual experience is remembering a line and being unable to find it. References are given as book.section, which works in any translation.

Context

Among the Stoics

WriterWho he wasHow he writes
SenecaCourtier, Nero’s tutor, one of Rome’s richest menPolished letters written for publication, shadowed by the gap between his wealth and his subject
EpictetusA former slave who became a teacherLecture notes taken by a student: direct, sometimes abrasive, addressed to a room
Marcus AureliusEmperorNotes to one reader, himself, with nobody to persuade or impress, which is why he admits he couldn’t get out of bed

Among the rival schools, the Epicureans advised withdrawing from public life, and the Stoics demanded participation: hold office, raise children, fight the war. That is why Stoicism became the philosophy of Rome’s governing class, and why the modern version as a private calming technique loses so much.

Context

The uncomfortable parts

Universal brotherhood, and slavery

Marcus writes that his city, as a man, is the world, and that all rational beings share one citizenship. He ruled a society in which a large share of people were legal property, and nothing in the twelve books treats that as a problem, though his own teacher Epictetus had been a slave. It is a real limit on how far the universalism reached.

The Christians

Christians were executed in his reign, notably at Lyon in 177. The single mention of them (11.3) is dismissive, and some scholars think it a later insertion. Whether he ordered or approved the killings is disputed.

Commodus

He chose his son as successor: the first biological succession in over eighty years, ending a run of adopted emperors. Commodus was a catastrophe. The notebook, written in exactly those years, says nothing about the choice, the plague, his children’s deaths or the revolt of Avidius Cassius. It records one activity within his life, not the life.

The case against Stoicism

The fortress is safe because it is empty

From Aristotle to Martha Nussbaum: some external things really are goods, and grief at a child’s death is an accurate perception of loss, not an error of reasoning. Stoicism buys invulnerability by shrinking what you may care about. Marcus himself writes of his teachers and children with a tenderness his doctrine doesn’t license.

Fate and responsibility

The Stoics were determinists who also held that our judgements are up to us. Chrysippus’s answer was the cylinder: a push starts it rolling, but its shape decides how. Critics note that the shape, your character, came from the same causal chain.

Indifferents that aren’t indifferent

If health and wealth are truly indifferent, there is no reason to prefer them. The Stoic “preferred indifferents” let the Stoic live like everyone else while holding a doctrine that would forbid it.

A philosophy that changes nothing

Locating all value in inner judgement supplies no lever against an unjust order, and fine consolation for living inside one. Hegel called it freedom in thought that leaves the world untouched. Nietzsche added that “live according to nature” reads your ideal into nature and then claims to derive it from there.

What none of this touches

The practical psychology survives: emotions follow judgements, judgements can be examined, and rehearsing a difficulty in advance blunts it. You can take the psychology and decline the cosmology, but the result is a set of techniques, not Stoicism.

Context

From nothing to everywhere

For most of its history the book was known to a few scholars. It was first printed in 1559, found an English readership in the nineteenth century, and became a mass-market book only in the last two decades, helped by Gregory Hays’s plain modern translation.

Its deepest afterlife is clinical. Albert Ellis credited Epictetus when he developed Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy in the 1950s, and Aaron Beck’s cognitive therapy made the same move: find the automatic thought, examine the belief behind it, test it. What carried over was the machinery. What didn’t was the point: Marcus examines his judgements because some are false and a life built on false judgements is not a good life, whatever it feels like.

Using it

How to read it

  1. Choose the translation carefully

    Gregory Hays (Modern Library, 2002) for a first read: plain and alive. Robin Hard (Oxford) or Martin Hammond (Penguin) for more literal versions with better notes. Avoid the free Victorian George Long translation.

  2. Don’t read it straight through

    It has no argument to follow, and two sittings produce fatigue. Read a few entries at a time.

  3. Sample Book 6 first

    The densest run of the famous entries.

  4. Then Book 4, slowly

    The one that repays rereading.

  5. Keep it by the bed

    Different entries surface at different points of life, as you would expect of a book organised around problems rather than topics.

Read alongside

  • Epictetus, Discourses and Handbook: the source of nearly everything Marcus practises.
  • Pierre Hadot, The Inner Citadel (1992): the reading of the book as spiritual exercises.
  • Seneca, Letters to Lucilius: the Stoic courtier’s version.
  • Fooled by Randomness, summarised on this site: Taleb’s modern use of Stoic dignity under bad luck.
