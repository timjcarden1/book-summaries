Marcus Aurelius · 121–180 CE

Written c. 170–180, in Greek

Never intended to be read

Meditations Twelve books of notes a Roman emperor wrote to himself, mostly on campaign, arguing with his own worst impulses.

There is no other book like this, and the reason is structural rather than literary: it is the private working notebook of the most powerful man on earth, and he is not explaining anything to anyone. He is telling himself, over and over, the same handful of things — because he keeps failing to remember them. The consolation most readers take from it is not that Marcus had answers, but that a man with absolute power, the best education available, and thirty years of practice was still struggling with irritation, procrastination, self-importance and the fear of death.

The man

The Life Behind the Notebook

Almost none of which appears in the book itself.

Marcus Annius Verus was born in Rome in 121 into a wealthy senatorial family of Spanish origin. His father died when he was small and he was raised by his grandfather and mother. The emperor Hadrian noticed him young and nicknamed him Verissimus — "most truthful," a pun on the family name — and in 138, needing to secure the succession two generations deep, arranged for Antoninus Pius to adopt both Marcus and Lucius Verus. Marcus was seventeen and had just been handed the empire, on a twenty-three-year delay.

Those twenty-three years as heir apparent are the least discussed and probably most formative part of his life. He served as consul, sat in the Senate, watched Antoninus govern, and — as Book 1 records at length — studied him. He also studied philosophy intensely enough that his rhetoric tutor Fronto wrote to him complaining about it. A substantial correspondence with Fronto survives and is worth knowing about, because it shows a completely different Marcus: chatty, affectionate, anxious about his health, gossiping about grape harvests. Whoever wrote the Meditations also wrote letters about a sore throat.

He became emperor in 161 and immediately did something no Roman had done: he insisted the Senate confirm Lucius Verus as his equal co-emperor, on the grounds that Hadrian had intended both. It was the first joint principate in Roman history and it worked, more or less, until Verus died in 169. What followed was nineteen years in which almost everything went wrong at once — a war in the east, a pandemic brought home by the returning army, a permanent crisis on the Danube, the revolt of his most trusted general, and the deaths of his wife and most of his children.

Book 1

Debts and Lessons

Seventeen people, and exactly what he owes each of them.

The first book is unlike the other eleven and is, for my money, the most quietly extraordinary thing in the whole text. It is a list. Marcus goes through the people who formed him — grandfather, parents, tutors, philosophers, his adoptive father, and finally the gods — and for each one names the specific quality he took from them. No self-praise, no narrative, no complaints: just an audit of where his character came from, itemised.

The entries get more precise as they go, and they are frequently unglamorous. From his tutor: not to take sides at the races, and to do his own work. From Alexander the grammarian: not to correct people's grammar in public, but to work the right expression into your reply. From Alexander the Platonist: not to say "I'm too busy" more often than necessary, or use it to dodge obligations. From Fronto, of all people, he says he learned what envy and duplicity look like in a ruler. From Diognetus he got scepticism about miracle-workers and charmers, and the habit of sleeping on a plain board.

The philosophical debts are the ones with consequences. Rusticus told him his character needed work, steered him away from writing poetry and showy rhetoric, taught him to read carefully rather than be satisfied with a general sense — and lent him his own copy of the Discourses of Epictetus. Almost everything in the remaining eleven books descends from that loan. Apollonius gave him independence and steadiness under pain and loss. Sextus gave him the model of a household run without anger.

The two long entries

Antoninus Pius, his adoptive father, gets the longest section in the book by a wide margin, and it reads as a portrait of the kind of ruler Marcus intends to be. What he singles out is almost entirely temperamental rather than political: that Antoninus worked steadily and finished what he started; that he was unmoved by flattery; that he would listen to anyone who had something useful to contribute, even in opposition; that he looked after his body without fuss, "not as one in love with life, nor for appearance"; that he had few needs and could go without; that he never rushed and never dithered; that he was the same in private as in public. Marcus ends by telling himself to behave, in every situation, as Antoninus would have behaved.

The last entry, 1.17, is addressed to the gods and is a list of the things he did not have to suffer. That he had good grandparents, parents, sister, teachers and friends. That he never wronged any of them, though he had the temperament to. That he did not grow up too fast, and remained under his mother's roof. That when he wanted to help someone he was never told he lacked the means. That his children were neither stupid nor deformed. That he did not get further in rhetoric and poetry, which might have absorbed him. And — a line that has generated centuries of speculation given what Roman gossip said about her — that his wife was "so obedient, so affectionate, so unaffected."

Orientation

A Private Notebook That Should Not Exist

Who wrote it, when, and how improbable its survival was.

Marcus Aurelius ruled from 161 to 180, through a plague that may have killed a tenth of the empire, a decade of war on the Danube, and a revolt by his most trusted general. He wrote these notes in Greek — the language of philosophy, not of government — in camp, at night, for no reader. The title we use is not his; the book has no argument, no structure, and no audience. That is precisely what makes it useful.

Behind the notes

The System He Is Drawing On

Marcus never explains Stoicism. He assumes it, and uses one corner of it.

The Meditations are the notes of a practitioner, not an introduction. Stoicism was already four centuries old and had a full physics, logic and ethics; Marcus uses almost none of the first two. What he has taken, mostly through Epictetus, is a small set of practical operations he can run on himself when he is angry, frightened, or reluctant to get out of bed.

The background picture

Providence or Atoms

The question he raises a dozen times and deliberately never settles.

Orthodox Stoicism had a complete physics. The cosmos is a single living, rational being; everything that happens follows from its nature; the divine reason — the logos — pervades matter, and a human mind is a fragment of it. Fate is not a constraint imposed from outside but the unfolding of one thing you are part of. Accepting what happens is therefore not resignation but recognition.

Marcus holds this, mostly. What is remarkable, and what separates him from a textbook Stoic, is that he keeps raising the alternative and refusing to close it off. Again and again he sets out a disjunction: either providence or atoms. Either the universe is an ordered whole governed by reason, or it is Epicurean — a chance concourse of particles, with no plan, no providence and no meaning beyond what a mind makes. He states this at 4.3, at 6.24, at 7.32, at 8.17, at 9.28, at 9.39, at 10.6, at 11.18 and at 12.14, among others. He never decides.

What he does instead is more interesting than deciding would have been. He argues that his practical conclusions survive either answer. If there is providence, then what happens to you is part of an order that includes you, and complaint is incoherent. If there are only atoms, then nothing is owed to you by anything, no one is coming, and the only thing you have is your own mind — which is therefore all the more worth governing well. Either way: attend to your own conduct, accept what you cannot control, treat other people decently, and stop expecting the universe to explain itself. The ethics are load-bearing; the metaphysics is scaffolding, and he knows it.

Flux

The other half of the physics that Marcus actually uses is the doctrine of universal change, inherited from Heraclitus and worked hard. Everything is in the process of turning into something else. Your body is matter borrowed and shortly to be returned; the empire is a phase; the people you envy are already dissolving. He puts it in a dozen forms — that time is a river of passing events, that the present moment is a point between two eternities, that Vespasian's court and Trajan's court are gone and were exactly like this one, full of people marrying and quarrelling and suspecting each other and wishing others dead.

Two uses follow. The first is consolation: if everything is temporary then so is whatever is currently unbearable. The second is deflation, and it is the one he leans on harder: if everything is temporary then so is whatever you are chasing, and the reputation you are protecting will not outlast the people who hold it. There is a real bleakness in this and it would be dishonest to soften it — long stretches of the middle books are a man telling himself, repeatedly, that nothing matters very much. What keeps it from nihilism is the one thing he exempts: how you behave, right now, is not diminished by any of it.

"Soon you will have forgotten the world, and soon the world will have forgotten you."Book 7.21

Book 11.18

Ten Things to Remember When Angry

The single most practical passage in the book, set out as he wrote it.

Most of the Meditations are scattered notes, but at 11.18 Marcus does something he almost never does: he writes out a numbered list. It is a protocol for dealing with anger at other people, and it is worth having in full, because it is the closest thing in the book to a set of instructions you could actually follow on a bad afternoon.

Two features of the list are worth pausing on. The first is that only one of the ten — item 9 — is about what to do; the other nine are about how to see. This is consistent with the whole method: the intervention point is the judgement, not the behaviour, and if you get the judgement right the behaviour looks after itself. The second is that several of the items are arguments Marcus does not quite believe and knows he does not quite believe. Item 3, that wrongdoers act involuntarily, is a Socratic doctrine that most people find implausible on first hearing and that Marcus asserts rather than defends. He is not proving anything here. He is assembling a set of thoughts that, held together at the right moment, will make anger harder to sustain.

The exercises

What He Actually Does

A small number of moves, run again and again.

Pierre Hadot's reading — that these are spiritual exercises rather than doctrines — has become the standard one, and it makes the repetition legible. Marcus is not developing a position. He is rehearsing a handful of manoeuvres so that they will be available when he needs them, in the way a soldier drills.

Marcus among the Stoics

The three Roman Stoics whose work survives are startlingly different as writers, and knowing which is which saves a lot of confusion. Seneca is a courtier and a stylist: his letters are polished, epigrammatic, written for publication, and shadowed by the awkward fact that he was Nero's tutor and one of the richest men in Rome while writing about the unimportance of wealth. Epictetus is a teacher and was born a slave: the Discourses are lecture notes taken by a student, and the tone is direct, sometimes abrasive, addressed to a room. Marcus is neither — he is writing to one person, himself, with no audience to persuade and no reputation at stake, and that is why the book reads as it does.

And among the schools

It is also worth knowing what Stoicism was competing against, because Marcus's positions are often responses to live alternatives rather than statements of the obvious. The Hellenistic schools were all answering one question — how should a person live, given that the world is not arranged for your benefit? — and they gave genuinely different answers.

The substance

What He Keeps Telling Himself

Six or seven ideas, returned to for a decade.

The Meditations have no plot and no development, but they do have obsessions, and the obsessions are countable. A handful of themes account for most of the book, and Marcus circles them with a persistence that is either monotonous or moving depending on how you read it. My own view is that the repetition is the most informative thing about the text: a man does not write the same reminder to himself for ten years unless he keeps needing it.

Death, constantly

No other theme comes close. Marcus reminds himself that he will die, that everyone he knows will die, that everyone who might remember him will die, and that the people who might remember those people will die too. He lists emperors and their courts — Augustus, his wife, his daughter, his descendants, his advisers, "all dead, all buried" — and notes that they had seemed permanent at the time. He observes that Alexander the Great and his mule-driver came to the same end. He tells himself not to behave "as though you were going to live ten thousand years."

The purpose of this is not morbidity, and reading it as gloom misses the mechanism. Marcus is using mortality as a scaling device. Almost everything that provokes anxiety — a slight from a courtier, a delayed dispatch, a question of precedence — becomes negligible when set against a span in which the whole Roman world is a brief episode. He is trying to be permanently unimpressed by his own circumstances, and death is the most reliable instrument he has for that. There is also a second, quieter use: if this is the only life, then the present hour is the whole of the available stock, and wasting it on resentment is expensive in a way that can actually be felt.

Other people

The second theme is the one modern readers find most immediately usable, because it is so unmistakably the complaint of someone who spends his days in meetings. The famous entry that opens Book 2 tells him what to expect on waking: the people he deals with today will be meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest and surly. What follows, though, is the part usually left out of the quotation. They are like this, he says, because they cannot tell good from bad — and since he has understood that goodness is beautiful and badness ugly, and since they are his kin, not by blood but by sharing in the same reason, he cannot be injured by them and he cannot hate them.

This is a much stranger and more demanding thought than the tolerance it is usually reduced to. It is not "people are difficult, so be patient." It is a claim that wrongdoing is a species of ignorance — the old Socratic position — and that therefore anger at a wrongdoer is as misplaced as anger at someone who has made an arithmetical error. Whether that is true is genuinely arguable; Marcus does not argue it, he assumes it. What he does instead is rehearse the consequence over and over, in dozens of variations, because the doctrine is easy to hold in the abstract and nearly impossible to hold at the moment somebody is being intolerable to you.

"It can ruin your life only if it ruins your character. Otherwise it cannot harm you — inside or out."Book 4.8

The present, and the obstacle

Two shorter but heavily worked seams. On the present: Marcus observes that whatever length of life a person has, they can only ever lose the present moment, since the past is already gone and the future is not theirs to lose. From this he draws the odd but consoling conclusion that the longest and shortest lives come to the same thing at the point of loss. On obstacles: the line at 5.20 — the impediment to action advances action, what stands in the way becomes the way — has been flattened by modern quotation into a slogan about grit, which is not quite what it says. His point is narrower and better: an obstruction to one purpose is raw material for another, because the virtue you are actually practising is not "getting this done" but "responding well to what is in front of me," and an obstacle supplies as much occasion for that as a clear road does.

And the reluctance to get up in the morning

The single most humanising entry in the book is at 5.1, and it deserves quoting at something like length because it is where the distance between an emperor and a reader collapses entirely. At dawn, when he does not want to get out of bed, he is to tell himself: I am rising to do the work of a human being. Why should I complain, if I am going out to do what I was born for and what I was brought into the world to do? Or was I made for this — to lie under the blankets and keep myself warm? And then, a few lines later, the note of exasperation with himself: but this is more pleasant. Do you exist, then, for pleasure — and not at all for action and exertion?

He is the ruler of the Roman empire, at war, in his fifties, and he is arguing with himself about the snooze button. The entry is undated but it is not a one-off: variations on it appear in at least four books. Whatever else the Meditations are, they are evidence that the internal experience of being a person has changed remarkably little in eighteen hundred years, and that neither power nor philosophy makes the morning easier.

Against

The Uncomfortable Parts

What the book does not say, and what its author did.

The Meditations are usually read as a self-contained moral document, and read that way they are close to unimpeachable. Set beside the life of the man who wrote them, they raise questions the text itself never touches — and a reader who takes the book seriously should know where those questions are rather than discover them later and feel misled.

The largest is the one that hangs over all ancient ethics. Marcus writes movingly that his city, so far as he is a man, is the world; that all rational beings share one citizenship; that what does not harm the community does not harm its members. He ruled a society in which perhaps a fifth of the population were legally property, and there is nothing in the twelve books suggesting he considered this a problem. Epictetus, his own principal source, had been a slave. The Stoic doctrine of universal brotherhood was, for Marcus, entirely compatible with the institution — as it was for almost everyone in antiquity. This is not a reason to dismiss the book, but it is a real limit on how far its universalism actually reached, and it should temper the common claim that Stoicism was ahead of its time on human equality.

Second: Christians were executed during his reign, most notoriously at Lyons in 177, and the single reference to them in the Meditations (11.3) is dismissive — he praises the soul that is ready to leave the body through its own judgement rather than "in sheer opposition, like the Christians." Whether Marcus ordered or approved those executions is genuinely disputed by historians; the machinery of provincial governors did not need imperial instruction. Some scholars also think the phrase is a later interpolation. Either way, the man writing about tolerance and kinship was presiding over a state that killed people for a religious refusal.

Third, and most damaging to the romantic reading: he chose his son Commodus as successor. It was the first biological succession in eighty-four years, breaking a run of adoptions that had produced four capable emperors, and Commodus was a catastrophe — vain, murderous, and eventually strangled in his bath. The Meditations, written across exactly the years in which that decision was being made and confirmed, contain no reflection on it at all. There is likewise nothing about the plague that devastated his empire, nothing about the deaths of most of his children, and nothing about the revolt of Avidius Cassius. The notebook is not a record of his life. It is a record of one activity within it, and it is silent about almost everything a biographer would want.

The honest summary This is an excellent book about how to manage your own mind, written by a man who was probably a better-than-average ruler and certainly not a moral revolutionary. It tells you nothing about whether he was right to hold power, and it was never trying to. Treating it as a testimonial for its author — rather than as a set of exercises that can be assessed on their own merits — is the commonest mistake made about it.
Afterlife

From Nothing to Everywhere

Eighteen centuries in which the book was mostly ignored, and two decades in which it was not.

One of the odder facts about the Meditations is how recent its fame is. For most of its existence it was a curiosity known to a few scholars. It has been a mass-market book for roughly twenty years. The version of Marcus that circulates today — quotable, motivational, compatible with a morning routine — is a very late arrival, and it is worth knowing which parts of it come from the text and which from the packaging.

The line into modern psychotherapy

The most substantive afterlife of Stoicism is not literary but clinical. When Albert Ellis developed Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy in the 1950s, he credited Epictetus directly and quoted him constantly: men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of them. Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy, developed independently in the 1960s, arrived at a structurally identical move. The core technique of both — identify the automatic thought, examine the belief that produced it, test whether it survives inspection — is the discipline of assent, rediscovered.

What carried across is the cognitive machinery. What did not is everything that gave the machinery a point. Marcus does not examine his judgements in order to feel better; he examines them because he thinks some of them are false, and because a person whose judgements are false is not living well regardless of how he feels. The Stoic claim is that virtue is the only good, which is a claim about value, not about mood. Strip that out and you are left with a technique for managing distress — genuinely useful, demonstrably effective, and not what the book is about.

Practical

How to Read It

Translation, method, and the modern misreadings.

More than with almost any other classic, the translation decides whether this book works for you. Marcus's Greek is compressed, unliterary and often ungrammatical — he was writing notes, not prose — and translators have to decide whether to smooth it or preserve it. The difference between versions is not stylistic garnish; it changes what the book seems to be.

Gregory Hays (Modern Library, 2002) is the version most people should start with. It is punchy, contemporary and unafraid of short sentences, and it makes Marcus sound like someone thinking rather than someone declaiming. Purists object that it takes liberties; it does, and it is still the one that gets read to the end. Robin Hard (Oxford World's Classics) and Martin Hammond (Penguin) are both more literal, both excellent, and both come with substantially better notes — Hard's introduction by Christopher Gill is the best short account of the philosophy attached to any popular edition. Avoid the free George Long translation of 1862 that circulates everywhere online: it is out of copyright, which is why it is everywhere, and its Victorian diction makes an unfussy book sound pompous.

As for method: do not read it straight through. The book has no argument to follow and reading it cover to cover in two sittings produces fatigue and a false sense of monotony. It was written in fragments over years and it works best consumed the same way — a few entries at a time, ideally when you actually need them. Many readers keep it as a bedside or morning book for years and report that different entries surface as relevant at different points of life, which is what you would expect of a text organised around problems rather than around topics.

  1. The injury is in the judgement, not the event.Between what happens and how you feel about it there is a step — the assent — and that step is the only place in the whole chain where you have any purchase. Almost every exercise in the book is aimed at widening it.
  2. Separate what is yours from what is not, ruthlessly and often.Your judgement, your intention, your conduct. Not your body, your reputation, other people, or whether the plan works. The discipline is in applying it to the specific thing bothering you today, not in agreeing with it in general.
  3. Obligation survives the loss of control.That outcomes are not up to you does not release you from acting well. This is the hinge that separates Stoicism from fatalism, and the one modern summaries most often drop.
  4. Other people are difficult because they are mistaken, not because they are enemies.Marcus asks himself to treat wrongdoing as a failure of understanding. Whether or not that is true, holding it makes anger structurally harder to sustain.
  5. Scale is a tool.Death, deep time, the view from above, the plain physical description of a prized object — four ways of getting the size of a thing right when your position or your fear has distorted it.
  6. Only the present can be lost.The past is spent and the future is not yet yours, so nothing else is actually at stake. This is the entry that does the most work at three in the morning.
  7. He never finished, and that is the point.Book 12 is telling him what Book 2 told him. There is no arrival, no graduation, no state of being fixed. The practice is the thing, and the evidence that a man could run it for a decade and still need it is the most reassuring fact in the book.
