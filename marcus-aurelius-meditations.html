Meditations

Ta eis heauton, “to himself”

“Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar, that thou art not dyed with this dye; for such things happen.”

Book 6.30 · George Long’s translation

Marcus Aurelius · written c. 170–180, in Greek

The whole book in one breath

This is the private notebook of the most powerful man on earth, written in Greek, largely in army camps on the Danube, in the last decade of his life. It explains nothing to anyone. In 487 entries, some a single line and a few of them two pages long, Marcus tells himself the same handful of things again and again because he keeps forgetting them: only his own judgements, aims and acts are his; things cannot touch the mind, only his opinions about them can; people who wrong him do it from ignorance, and they are his kin anyway; everything changes, he will soon be dead and forgotten, and so the one thing worth doing is to act justly now. Book 1 is a list of what he owed to the people who formed him. Books 2 to 12 are exercises, not arguments. Most readers take comfort not from any answer Marcus found but from the fact that a man with absolute power and decades of practice was still struggling, at the end, with anger, sloth, vanity and the fear of death.

The life behind the notebook

An heir for twenty-three years, an emperor for nineteen

A boy Hadrian noticed

Marcus was born in Rome on 26 April 121 as Marcus Annius Verus, into a rich senatorial family whose roots lay in southern Spain. His father died when he was about three, and he was brought up by his grandfather, Annius Verus, three times consul and prefect of the city, and by his mother, Domitia Lucilla, heiress to a brickmaking fortune. Both open Book 1 of the Meditations. He was taught at home rather than at school (1.4). At around twelve, according to the ancient biography in the Historia Augusta, he took up the philosopher’s rough cloak and wanted to sleep on the ground until his mother talked him into a couch spread with skins, which matches his “plank bed and skin” at 1.6. The emperor Hadrian took an interest in the boy, made him a Salian priest at eight and, punning on Verus, called him Verissimus, “most truthful.”

The long apprenticeship

In 138 Hadrian’s chosen heir, Lucius Aelius, died. Hadrian adopted the senator we know as Antoninus Pius on condition that Antoninus in turn adopt two boys: Marcus, then sixteen, and Aelius’s seven-year-old son, the future Lucius Verus. Marcus was betrothed to Antoninus’s daughter Faustina, married her in 145 and spent the next twenty-three years at his adoptive father’s side, consul at eighteen and learning the empire’s business from a man who ruled it for twenty-three years, largely in peace. His Latin rhetoric tutor was Marcus Cornelius Fronto, whose letters with him survive and show a warm, joking, sometimes anxious young man. In his mid-twenties he turned from rhetoric to philosophy, and it was the Stoic Junius Rusticus, later prefect of Rome, who lent him the lecture notes of Epictetus that shaped everything he wrote (1.7).

Emperor, 161

Antoninus died at his villa at Lorium on 7 March 161, having given the officer of the guard that evening the watchword aequanimitas, equanimity. Marcus at once had the senate make Lucius his equal, the first time Rome had two emperors ruling together. That August Faustina bore twins, one of them Commodus. Then almost everything went wrong. Parthia invaded Armenia; the war lasted until 166, and the returning army brought back a plague, probably smallpox, that ran through the empire for years and killed millions. Germanic and Sarmatian peoples pressed on the Danube. Lucius died in early 169, and the same year Marcus lost his seven-year-old son Annius. In 170 the Marcomanni and Quadi broke through, besieged Aquileia and burned a town in northern Italy, the first invasion of Italy in almost three centuries.

The hard decade

Marcus spent most of the 170s with the armies: at Carnuntum, near modern Vienna, in 171–173, fighting the Quadi, Marcomanni and the Sarmatian Iazyges. In 175 Avidius Cassius, his most successful general, proclaimed himself emperor in the East on a report that Marcus was dead, and was killed by his own officers after about three months. Marcus toured the East, and Faustina died on the journey, in a village in Cappadocia, that winter. In 176 he was initiated into the mysteries at Eleusis and endowed chairs of philosophy at Athens for the four main schools. In 177 he made his teenage son Commodus co-emperor; that summer Christians were tortured and killed at Lyon. He went north again in 178 and died on campaign on 17 March 180, probably at Vindobona or Sirmium, aged fifty-eight. Of the fourteen or so children Faustina bore him, only Commodus and four or five daughters outlived him.

The historian Cassius Dio, who lived through what followed, wrote that Marcus “did not meet with the good fortune that he deserved,” and that with his death the history of Rome “descends from a kingdom of gold to one of iron and rust.” The notebook was written in these years. It was never meant to be read.

The work

A notebook with no title, no plan and one reader

What survives is twelve “books” of entries in Greek, the language of philosophy for an educated Roman, although his letters to Fronto are in Latin. The Greeks called such things hypomnemata, personal notes kept for rereading, and that is what these are: reminders, arguments with himself, definitions, lists, quotations copied out for later. Nobody is addressed except “thou,” and “thou” is Marcus. The title under which the book reached us, Ta eis heauton, “the things to himself,” comes from the manuscripts and is probably not his.

  • Where it was written. Between Books 1 and 2 one manuscript tradition carries the note “Among the Quadi at the Granua,” a river in modern Slovakia, and between Books 2 and 3 “This in Carnuntum,” the army base on the Danube. Editors disagree whether each note closes the book before it or heads the one after, but together they put at least the early books in the camps of the early 170s.
  • Book 1 is different. It is continuous, finished and addressed to no one, a list of debts rather than an exercise. Many scholars since the Austrian editor Heinrich Schenkl have thought it was written last and set at the front as a kind of dedication.
  • The rest is fragments. The entries run from five words to more than nine hundred; the median, in George Long’s translation, is 64. Some are polished aphorisms, some half-finished thoughts, and a few are unreadable in the manuscripts.
  • He copied as well as wrote. Book 7 contains a run of quotations from Plato, Euripides and others (7.35–7.51), and Book 11 ends with sayings of Epictetus and Socrates (11.22–39). Parts of the book are a commonplace book.
  • The numbering is modern. The division into twelve books is in the manuscripts; the numbered entries come from Thomas Gataker’s edition of 1652. References here are to book and entry in Long’s numbering, which almost every translation follows.

If you remember nothing else

Twelve things to keep

Judgement · 4.7, 8.47

The injury is in the opinion

“Take away thy opinion, and then there is taken away the complaint, ‘I have been harmed.’” Between what happens and what you feel sits a judgement, and it is the one place you have any purchase.

Control · 6.41, 12.3

Only three things are yours

Your judgement, your aim and your act. Body, reputation, other people and results belong to the world. Most misery comes from treating the second list as if it were the first.

Action · 5.20, 6.50

Act anyway, with a reservation

That results are not yours does not excuse you from trying. Aim “conditionally,” and when blocked, turn the obstacle into material for another virtue.

Other people · 2.1, 7.22

Wrongdoers are kin who are mistaken

They act from ignorance of good and bad, and they share your reason. So they cannot really harm you, and you have no ground to hate them.

Anger · 11.18

Gentleness is the strong position

Anger is a weakness, like pain; mildness “possesses strength, nerves, and courage.” And anger usually costs more than the thing you are angry about.

Perspective · 6.13, 9.30

Get the size of things right

Describe a prized thing plainly (a purple robe is sheep’s wool dyed in shellfish blood), or look down on it from above. Both shrink what rank or appetite inflates.

Time · 2.14, 3.10

Only the present can be lost

The past is gone and the future not yet yours, so the longest life and the shortest lose exactly the same thing: this moment.

Death · 4.17, 9.3

Death is near and natural

“Do not act as if thou wert going to live ten thousand years.” Dying is a work of nature like teething or growing grey, and there is no point fearing it.

Fame · 4.3, 4.19

Praise is noise from the dying

The people who might remember you will die soon too, and then the people who remember them. Posthumous fame is a relay of forgetting.

Duty · 5.1, 6.54

You exist for the common work

“That which is not good for the swarm, neither is it good for the bee.” Getting out of bed is joining the work that plants, ants and bees already do.

Acceptance · 4.23, 10.6

You are a part of a whole

Whether the universe is run by providence or is atoms colliding, a part has no business resenting the whole. He argues it both ways and lands in the same place.

Practice · 5.9, 8.1

He never finished

“It is plain that thou art far from philosophy.” Book 12 repeats what Book 2 said. Return to philosophy not as a schoolmaster but as a salve for sore eyes.

The Stoic kit

A practitioner’s tools, not a textbook

Stoicism was nearly five centuries old when Marcus wrote. It was founded at Athens around 300 BCE by Zeno, a merchant from Citium in Cyprus, taught in the Painted Stoa that gave it its name, and systematised by Chrysippus into three parts: logic, physics and ethics. Marcus tells himself to apply all three to “every impression on the soul” (8.13), but he wrote no treatise on any of them. What he takes, mostly through Epictetus, is a set of operations he can run on himself when he is angry, frightened, bored or reluctant to get up.

The pieces

  • The ruling part. A person is “a little flesh and breath, and the ruling part” (2.2; again at 12.3). The ruling part, the hegemonikon, is reason, and it is a fragment of the reason that runs the universe: the daemon “which Zeus hath given to every man for his guardian and guide, a portion of himself” (5.27).
  • Impressions and assent. Events arrive as impressions. The mind can accept or refuse the judgement that comes with them. “Things themselves touch not the soul” (5.19). His plainest example: “I see that my child is sick. I do see; but that he is in danger, I do not see” (8.49). Add nothing to the first report.
  • Good, bad and indifferent. Only virtue is good and only vice is bad. Death, life, honour, pain and pleasure “equally happen to good men and bad,” so they are neither (2.11). Stoics called them indifferents, while still allowing that some, like health, are rationally preferred; Marcus keeps the idea when he says that in “things indifferent I attempt to ascertain the value of each” (3.11).
  • Acting with a reservation. A Stoic aims at outcomes “conditionally” (6.50; 8.41): he tries to win, but he is not wrecked if he loses, because what was his was the trying. This is what makes obstacles usable: “the mind converts and changes every hindrance to its activity into an aid” (5.20).
  • The city of the world. If reason is common to all people, so is law, so all are fellow citizens, “the world is in a manner a state” (4.4). “My city and country, so far as I am Antoninus, is Rome, but so far as I am a man, it is the world” (6.44).
  • The whole. The universe is “one living being, having one substance and one soul” (4.40), everything woven together “and the bond is holy” (7.9). Whatever happens to a part is good for the whole, and so, rightly seen, for the part.

Three disciplines

The French scholar Pierre Hadot, in The Inner Citadel (1992), showed that almost every entry in Books 2–12 exercises one of three disciplines, which Marcus took from Epictetus and quotes in Epictetus’s own words at 11.37. Marcus sometimes packs all three into one sentence, as at 7.54 and 9.6.

The twelve books

Book by book

The books have no titles and no subjects, and the same thoughts recur in all of them, so the usual experience is remembering a line and being unable to find it. What follows goes through each book in order, with the entries that carry its weight. Titles are ours; quotations are from George Long’s translation of 1862.

Book 1 · 17 entries

Debts

A list of what he owed to each person who formed him, ending with the gods: the one book that is not an argument with himself.

Each entry begins “From…” with no verb, so translators supply “I learned” or “I observed”; Long warns that Marcus is not claiming to have acquired every virtue he admired. The list runs from family through tutors to philosophers, then to his adoptive father and finally the gods, and what it praises is almost entirely restraint.

What the list values

  • Almost every lesson is a refusal: of display, of partisanship, of excuses, of superstition, of fine writing. Rusticus taught him “not to walk about in the house in my outdoor dress” and to write letters as plainly as the one Rusticus wrote from Sinuessa to his mother (1.7).
  • The tutor’s lessons read like a charter for a man who would one day sit in the imperial box: no side at the chariot races, no favourite type of gladiator (1.5).
  • Fronto, the teacher whose letters show the most affection, gets one sentence, and it is about tyranny: he taught Marcus “what envy and duplicity and hypocrisy are in a tyrant,” and that Roman patricians “are rather deficient in paternal affection” (1.11). It is the only hint in the book of what court life under Hadrian had looked like.
  • From Severus comes the book’s one political programme: “a polity in which there is the same law for all,” with “equal rights and equal freedom of speech,” and “a kingly government which respects most of all the freedom of the governed.” With it comes a list of heroes of the senatorial opposition to earlier emperors: Thrasea, Helvidius, Cato, Dion, Brutus (1.14).
  • Claudius Maximus “never showed amazement and surprise, and was never in a hurry, and never put off doing a thing” (1.15). Several items describe the kind of man Marcus kept failing to be.

The portrait of Antoninus Pius (1.16)

  • The emperor who adopted him gets nearly nine hundred words: mild, immovable once he had deliberated, indifferent to honours, willing to hear anyone “who had anything to propose for the common weal.”
  • He “considered himself no more than any other citizen,” released his friends from the duty of dining with him or escorting him, and did not tire of them.
  • He stopped applause and flattery at once, watched the treasury, economised on shows and public buildings, and “patiently” endured the blame that economy brought.
  • He was not superstitious, not a flatterer of the crowd, not curious about food, clothes or “the beauty of his slaves,” and he looked after his health so well that he seldom needed a doctor.
  • Homely details break in: his clothes came from his villa at Lorium, after headaches he went straight back to work, and “we know how he behaved to the toll-collector at Tusculum who asked his pardon,” a story now lost.
  • The last line borrows Xenophon’s praise of Socrates: he could both abstain from and enjoy the things most people can neither give up nor enjoy in moderation.

What he thanks the gods for (1.17)

  • Good grandparents, parents, a sister, teachers, friends; a ruler and father who showed him “it is possible for a man to live in a palace without wanting either guards or embroidered dresses, or torches and statues.”
  • That he was not brought up longer with “my grandfather’s concubine,” that he kept his youth chaste and “never touched either Benedicta or Theodotus,” and that when he did fall in love he was cured.
  • That although often out of temper with Rusticus, he never did anything he had to regret; that his mother, though fated to die young, spent her last years with him.
  • “Such a brother,” meaning Lucius Verus, whose character roused him “to vigilance over myself”; “such a wife, so obedient, and so affectionate, and so simple”; children “not stupid nor deformed in body.”
  • Remedies shown in dreams, against spitting blood and giddiness; and that he never wasted time on writers of history, syllogisms or astronomy.
  • An admission slips into the thanks: he still falls short of living by nature “through my own fault, and through not observing the admonitions of the gods.”

Book 2 · 17 entries · “Among the Quadi”

Begin the morning

The shortest book, and in miniature the whole method: rehearse the day’s annoyances, remember death, throw away the books, keep the god within clean.

The morning exercise

  • The book opens with its most famous entry: “Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busybody, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial” (2.1). The point is not pessimism but inoculation: expect it, and it loses its power to surprise.
  • The reason he gives is usually dropped. Such people act “by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil,” and they share his reason and “portion of the divinity,” so he “can neither be injured by any of them … nor can I be angry with my kinsman, nor hate him.”
  • “For we are made for co-operation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth.” To be vexed and turn away is itself to act against them.

No more time

  • “Throw away thy books” (2.2), “cast away the thirst after books” (2.3): reading had become a way of putting off living.
  • “Thou art an old man; no longer let this be a slave, no longer be pulled by the strings like a puppet” (2.2). He was probably about fifty.
  • “Remember how long thou hast been putting off these things” (2.4). A limit is fixed to his time, and if he does not use it to clear his mind, “it will go and thou wilt go, and it will never return.”
  • Do “every act of thy life as if it were the last” (2.5), and think “as a Roman and a man” with dignity, affection, freedom and justice.
  • People who are frantically busy without an aim are triflers too (2.7); what makes a man unhappy is not failing to read other minds but failing to watch his own (2.8).

Gods or no gods, and the one life

  • Death is nothing to fear whether there are gods or not (2.11): if they exist they will not harm him, and if not, “what is it to me to live in a universe devoid of gods or devoid of providence?” He then insists they do exist and care, and argues that since death, pain, honour and dishonour fall equally on good and bad, they cannot be goods or evils.
  • Death is “an operation of nature; and if any one is afraid of an operation of nature, he is a child” (2.12).
  • “No man loses any other life than this which he now lives, nor lives any other than this which he now loses” (2.14). However long you live, even thirty thousand years, you lose only the present moment, the only thing you have.
  • From the Cynic Monimus, a motto that recurs through the book: “all is opinion” (2.15).
  • An aside from Aristotle’s successor Theophrastus: wrongs done from desire are worse than wrongs done from anger, because anger at least begins in pain (2.10).

Five ways to harm yourself (2.16)

The soul does violence to itself when it resents what happens and becomes “a tumor on the universe”; when it turns against a person in anger; when pleasure or pain overpowers it; when it acts a part and speaks insincerely; and when it does anything without an aim.

“Of human life the time is a point, and the substance is in a flux, and the perception dull … and after fame is oblivion. What then is that which is able to conduct a man? One thing, and only one, philosophy.”2.17, the book’s last entry, followed by the note “This in Carnuntum”

Book 3 · 16 entries · “At Carnuntum”

Make haste

Longer entries on urgency, on the beauty of things that are not meant to be beautiful, and on a mind with nothing to hide.

Before the mind goes

  • The book opens with a fear more specific than death: that the understanding may fail first (3.1). Breathing and appetite survive dotage; the power to judge, to do one’s duty and to decide whether to leave life does not. “We must make haste.”
  • “Neither wilt thou read thy own memoirs, nor the acts of the ancient Romans and Hellenes, and the selections from books which thou wast reserving for thy old age” (3.14). He may mean these very notes.

The beauty of by-products (3.2)

  • Bread splits in the oven against the baker’s intention, and the cracks are attractive; figs gape when ripe; olives near rotting have “a peculiar beauty”; so do the bending ears of corn, the lion’s eyebrows and the foam at a boar’s mouth.
  • Someone who has come to understand nature will see real animals’ jaws with as much pleasure as painted ones, and find “a certain maturity and comeliness” in an old man or woman. It is the book’s one passage of pure delight.

Everyone dies (3.3)

  • Hippocrates cured many and died of disease; astrologers foretold deaths and died; Alexander, Pompey and Caesar destroyed cities and armies and then left life themselves.
  • Heraclitus, who theorised that the world would end in fire, died of dropsy “smeared all over with mud” (the ancient cure was a coat of dung); “lice destroyed Democritus; and other lice killed Socrates,” meaning his accusers.
  • “Thou hast embarked, thou hast made the voyage, thou art come to shore; get out.” If there is another life, there are gods there too; if nothing, then no more pain and pleasure.

The transparent mind

  • Think only thoughts you could state at once if someone asked, “What hast thou now in thy thoughts?” (3.4). Such a man is “a priest and minister of the gods … a fighter in the noblest fight.”
  • Let the god within be guardian of one who is “manly and of ripe age, and engaged in matter political, and a Roman, and a ruler,” standing at his post ready to be recalled (3.5).
  • If you find anything better than justice, truth, temperance and courage, turn to it with all your soul; you will not (3.6). Never value anything that requires you to break a promise, hate, curse, or “desire anything which needs walls and curtains” (3.7).
  • Receiving impressions is common to cattle; being pulled by desire is common to beasts and to “a Phalaris and a Nero”; even the scoundrel has intelligence. What belongs to the good man alone is contentment with his lot and a clean god within (3.16).

Tools

  • The method of physical definition (3.11): define anything that confronts you “in its substance, in its nudity,” name its parts and what it will dissolve into, and ask what virtue it calls for. Man is “a citizen of the highest city, of which all other cities are like families.”
  • Keep your principles ready “as physicians have always their instruments and knives ready” (3.13).
  • Life is a point, the earth a nook, and posthumous fame is carried by “a succession of poor human beings, who will very soon die” (3.10).

“A man then must stand erect, not be kept erect by others.”3.5

Book 4 · 51 entries

The retreat within

The entries grow shorter and more numerous. The book is built around one long meditation on retreat and a sustained assault on the fear of being forgotten.

The inner retreat (4.3)

  • “Men seek retreats for themselves, houses in the country, sea-shores, and mountains; and thou too art wont to desire such things very much.” But you can retire into yourself whenever you choose, and nowhere is quieter.
  • The retreat is not escape but review: brief first principles that “cleanse the soul completely, and … send thee back free from all discontent with the things to which thou returnest.”
  • Then a drill in four rounds, each ending “and be quiet at last.” Angry at people? They exist for one another, and they do wrong involuntarily. Unhappy with your lot? “Either there is providence or atoms.” Troubled by the body? The mind stands apart from it. Wanting fame? “For the whole earth is a point.”
  • Two thoughts to keep closest: “things do not touch the soul,” and everything you see changes and will soon not exist. “The universe is transformation: life is opinion.”

The world as a city

  • A chain of “if this is so” (4.4): if mind is common, so is reason; so is law; so we are fellow citizens; so “the world is in a manner a state.”
  • Change your mind whenever someone shows you are wrong, but only from being persuaded that it is just or useful, “not because it appears pleasant or brings reputation” (4.12).
  • “Everything harmonizes with me, which is harmonious to thee, O Universe” (4.23). Athenians call their city “Dear city of Cecrops”; “wilt not thou say, Dear city of Zeus?”
  • Democritus advised doing few things. Better: do what is necessary, and ask of each act and each thought, “Is this one of the unnecessary things?” (4.24).

Harm, praise and death

  • “Take away thy opinion, and then there is taken away the complaint, ‘I have been harmed’” (4.7). What does not make a man worse cannot make his life worse (4.8).
  • “Within ten days thou wilt seem a god to those to whom thou art now a beast and an ape, if thou wilt return to thy principles” (4.16).
  • “Do not act as if thou wert going to live ten thousand years. Death hangs over thee. While thou livest, while it is in thy power, be good” (4.17).
  • Anything beautiful is beautiful in itself: “Is such a thing as an emerald made worse than it was, if it is not praised?” (4.20).
  • Where do souls go, if they survive? Into the air, as bodies go into the earth, where they change and make room for new ones, just as the animals we eat are “in a manner buried” in us (4.21).
  • “Many grains of frankincense on the same altar: one falls before, another falls after; but it makes no difference” (4.15).

The dead and the forgotten

  • Picture the reign of Vespasian: people “marrying, bringing up children, sick, dying, warring, feasting,” flattering, plotting, “desiring consulship, kingly power.” All gone. Then Trajan’s reign: the same (4.32).
  • Names once famous are now antique words: Camillus, Caeso, Volesus, Leonnatus (Haines reads Dentatus, an early Roman hero), then Scipio and Cato, then Augustus, “then also Hadrianus and Antoninus” (4.33), his own adoptive grandfather and father.
  • Physicians who frowned over the sick, astrologers, philosophers of immortality, conquerors, tyrants, whole cities (Helice, Pompeii, Herculaneum): all dead (4.48). “What was yesterday a little mucus, to-morrow will be a mummy or ashes.”
  • So end your journey “as an olive falls off when it is ripe, blessing nature who produced it, and thanking the tree on which it grew.”
  • The long-lived gained nothing: “Cadicianus, Fabius, Julianus, Lepidus” carried many to their graves and were then carried out (4.50). Nobody now knows who they were.
  • “Thou art a little soul bearing about a corpse, as Epictetus used to say” (4.41).

The promontory (4.49)

“Be like the promontory against which the waves continually break, but it stands firm and tames the fury of the water around it.” Do not say “Unhappy am I because this has happened to me,” but “happy am I, though this has happened to me,” because you came through it unbroken. The principle to apply to every vexation: “not that this is a misfortune, but that to bear it nobly is good fortune.”

“The universe is transformation: life is opinion.”4.3

Book 5 · 36 entries

The work of a human being

The emperor argues with himself about getting up, then about the harder work: doing good without keeping score, and turning obstacles into material.

Getting out of bed (5.1)

  • “In the morning when thou risest unwillingly, let this thought be present: I am rising to the work of a human being.” Was he made “to lie in the bed-clothes and keep myself warm? But this is more pleasant.” Then was he made for pleasure, and not for action?
  • Little plants, little birds, ants, spiders and bees all work at their share of the universe. Sleep is necessary, but nature set limits to it, as to food, and he overshoots them while stopping short in his work.
  • The craftsman who loves his art forgets to wash and eat; “thou valuest thy own nature less than the turner values the turning art, or the dancer the dancing art.” He returns to the same argument at 8.12.

No excuses

  • “Men cannot admire the sharpness of thy wits”: granted. But sincerity, dignity, endurance, contentment, kindness and frankness need no talent, and he still falls short “voluntarily” (5.5).
  • Do not return to philosophy as to a schoolmaster, but as people with sore eyes return to their sponge and egg (5.9). And “be content if the greater part of what thou doest is consistent with man’s nature.”
  • “It is hardly possible to endure even the most agreeable” of those who live with him, “to say nothing of a man being hardly able to endure himself” (5.10).
  • “Whose soul have I now, that of a child, or of a young man, or of a feeble woman, or of a tyrant, or of a domestic animal, or of a wild beast?” (5.11).

Doing good like a vine (5.6)

  • Three kinds of benefactor: one enters the favour in his accounts; one does not say so but thinks of the other as his debtor; a third “does not even know what he has done, but he is like a vine which has produced grapes, and seeks for nothing more.”
  • Like a horse after a race or a bee after its honey, the good man “does not call out for others to come and see, but he goes on to another act.” He answers his own objection: to be social is not to want the credit.

What nature prescribes (5.8)

A doctor prescribes riding or cold baths; the nature of the universe prescribes “disease, or mutilation, or loss.” Both are unpleasant and both are for health: in the second case, the health of the whole, which would be “mutilated” if any link in the chain of causes were cut. So accept what happens, because it was fitted to you like a squared stone in a wall, and because it serves the whole.

The obstacle and the way

  • “The soul is dyed by the thoughts” (5.16). Dye it with this one: where a man can live, he can live well. “But he must live in a palace; well then, he can also live well in a palace.”
  • “Nothing happens to any man which he is not formed by nature to bear” (5.18). “Things themselves touch not the soul, not in the least degree” (5.19).
  • People who obstruct him become, to that extent, as indifferent as sun, wind or a wild beast. They can stop his action but not his disposition, which acts “conditionally”: “the mind converts and changes every hindrance to its activity into an aid … and that which is an obstacle on the road helps us on this road” (5.20). Gregory Hays’s modern translation renders the thought “what stands in the way becomes the way.”
  • “That which does no harm to the state, does no harm to the citizen” (5.22).

Small and large

  • “Art thou angry with him whose armpits stink? … What good will this anger do thee?” (5.28). If a man is at fault, use your reason to rouse his and show him.
  • On suicide, the Stoic open door: “The house is smoky, and I quit it” (5.29). Until then he stays, and is free.
  • How has he behaved to gods, parents, brothers, children, teachers, nurses, friends and slaves (5.31)? He can recall how much he has endured, and that his service is nearly done.
  • “Soon, very soon, thou wilt be ashes, or a skeleton, and either a name or not even a name” (5.33). Meanwhile the things most prized in life are “like little dogs biting one another.”
  • A last glimpse of public life (5.36): “When thou art calling out on the Rostra, hast thou forgotten, man, what these things are?”

  • “It is no longer in thy power to have lived the whole of thy life … like a philosopher; but both to many others and to thyself it is plain that thou art far from philosophy” (8.1). So stop worrying how he seems, and live the rest as nature wants.

  • Of any thing: what is it in itself, what is it made of, what causes it, what is it doing in the world, how long will it last? (8.11).

Injustice is impiety

Opens with the book’s most sustained argument, contains its bleakest cry, and ends with its most practical advice on ingratitude.

The argument (9.1)

  • Nature made rational beings for one another, so “he who acts unjustly acts impiously.” The lie is impious too, because nature is truth.
  • Even the unintentional liar offends nature, having neglected the powers that would have let him tell true from false.
  • So is the man who pursues pleasure as good and avoids pain as evil, because he must end by accusing nature of rewarding the bad and punishing the good; and fear of pain will make him fear things that must happen, and love of pleasure will make him unjust.
  • Pain and pleasure, death and life, honour and dishonour: nature uses them indifferently, and so should we.

Tired of people

  • Better to leave life without tasting lying, hypocrisy, luxury and pride; next best, to leave when sick of them. The corruption of the mind is “a pestilence, much more, indeed, than any such corruption and change of this atmosphere” (9.2). It is the book’s only glance at the plague.
  • Wait for death as for a child to come out of the womb (9.3). The cheap comfort is to think whom you are leaving: people whose principles you do not share. “Come quick, O death, lest perchance I, too, should forget myself.”
  • “He often acts unjustly who does not do a certain thing; not only he who does a certain thing” (9.5).
  • “To-day I have got out of all trouble, or rather I have cast out all trouble, for it was not outside, but within and in my opinions” (9.13).

Like to like

  • Earth seeks earth and fire seeks fire; bees swarm, cattle herd; men form states, friendships, families, treaties. Only rational beings have forgotten this pull, “but still, though men strive to avoid [this union], they are caught and held by it” (9.9).
  • Any act with no social purpose “is of the nature of a mutiny,” like a man standing apart in an assembly (9.23).
  • Childhood, youth and manhood each ended; each change was a death, and none was frightening. Nor was the change from his life under his grandfather to life under his mother, then under his father (9.21).

No Republic

“How worthless are all these poor people who are engaged in matters political, and, as they suppose, are playing the philosopher!” (9.29). Do what nature requires now, “nor yet expect Plato’s Republic: but be content if the smallest thing goes on well.” The reason is political realism: “who can change men’s opinions? and without a change of opinions what else is there than the slavery of men who groan while they pretend to obey?” Alexander, Philip and Demetrius of Phalerum may judge whether they understood nature, but “if they acted like tragedy heroes, no one has condemned me to imitate them.”

Prayer, sickness, ingratitude

  • Pray differently (9.40). Not “How shall I be released from this?” but “How shall I not desire to be released?”; not “How shall I not lose my little son?” but “How shall I not be afraid to lose him?”
  • Epicurus again (9.41): in illness he talked philosophy, not symptoms, and gave the doctors no chance to look solemn. Do the same.
  • When someone is shameless, ask whether a world without shameless people is possible. It is not; this is one of them (9.42). Nature gave mildness as the antidote to the stupid, and every wrongdoer can be taught.
  • If someone proves ungrateful, blame yourself for trusting him or for giving in expectation of a return. Do you want payment for a kindness, “just as if the eye demanded a recompense for seeing, or the feet for walking”?

Three acts are the whole play

A summing-up that reads like a last book: the old reminders restated plainly, a rare doubt about the gods’ justice, and an ending on leaving the stage.

Now, not later

  • Everything he is trying to reach by a roundabout road he can have now, if he forgets the past, trusts the future to providence, and directs the present by piety and justice (12.1).
  • Fear not that you must some time stop living, but “never to have begun to live according to nature.” Then you will stop being “a stranger in thy native land.”
  • You are body, breath and mind; only the mind is properly yours. Strip off everything else, past and future included, and become like Empedocles’ sphere, “All round and in its joyous rest reposing” (12.3).
  • Practise even what you despair of: the left hand, useless for most things, holds the bridle better than the right because it is used to it (12.6).
  • Be a pancratiast, not a gladiator: the gladiator can drop his sword and be killed, but the fighter whose weapon is his hand always has it (12.9).

Other people’s opinion

  • “Every man loves himself more than all the rest of men, but yet sets less value on his own opinion of himself than on the opinion of others” (12.4). None of us could bear a single day of saying every thought aloud.
  • “If it is not right, do not do it: if it is not true, do not say it” (12.17).
  • How do I know this act was wrong? And if it was, how do I know he has not already condemned himself? To want the bad not to do wrong is to want a fig tree without juice (12.16).

The one doubt (12.5)

How can the gods, having ordered everything well, have allowed the best men, the ones closest to them, to be “completely extinguished” at death? He answers his own question: if it were just for them to survive, the gods would have arranged it, and since in this very inquiry “thou art disputing with the Deity,” he must trust the gods’ justice. It is the nearest the notebook comes to wanting an afterlife.

Threads through the books

Four things he kept returning to

Because the notebook has no plan, its real structure is repetition. These four threads run through all twelve books.

Physics

Providence or atoms

Orthodox Stoicism held that the universe is one rational, living whole run by providence, so that accepting what happens is recognition rather than resignation. Marcus believed this, and says so. But again and again he sets beside it the rival picture of the Epicureans: a universe of atoms colliding by chance, with no plan and no one in charge. He never settles the question, and he does not need to, because his conclusions survive either answer. If there is providence, complaint is incoherent. If there are only atoms, nothing is owed to you, and the one ordered thing in the storm, your own mind, is all the more worth governing (12.14). The ethics carry the weight; the physics is scaffolding. This is why the book still works for readers who share none of his cosmology.

Change as the law of things

  • From Heraclitus he takes the flux: “the death of earth is to become water, and the death of water is to become air, and the death of air is to become fire, and reversely” (4.46). Time is “a river made up of the events which happen” (4.43).
  • Nature “loves nothing so much as to change the things which are and to make new things like them” (4.36); it moulds a horse out of its substance as if out of wax, breaks it up and makes a tree (7.23).
  • Nothing comes from nothing or goes to nothing (4.4, 5.13): the parts of him will be reduced into parts of the universe “and so on forever.” The universe has no rubbish heap outside itself, so it remakes its own decay into new things (8.50).
  • This consoles, because whatever is unbearable will pass, and it deflates, because whatever you want will pass too. The one thing not carried away is how you behave now.

The constant

Twelve ways to face death

Death is on almost every page. It is not morbidity but a measuring rod: set against death, a courtier’s slight shrinks to nothing and the present hour becomes the whole stock of life. Marcus tries every argument he knows, Stoic, Epicurean and Platonic, and he does not seem to think any one of them enough.

What he never says is that death is good in itself, or that he looks forward to an afterlife. The furthest he goes is the puzzled question at 12.5 and the refusal, there and at 11.3, to decide between extinction, dispersal and survival.

Oblivion

The names he forgot

His favourite weapon against vanity is a list of names. The famous dead of Rome, the courts of earlier emperors, his own family and teachers, and people so obscure that nobody now knows who they were: all of them are offered as proof that everyone is forgotten. The irony is the book’s own survival. Most of the names he chose as examples of oblivion are now known only because he wrote them down, and some not even then.

Absences

What the notebook leaves out

The reign was one of the most eventful in Roman history, and the notebook passes over nearly all of it. That is the clearest evidence of what the book is: a record of one activity within a life, the daily work on his own judgements, rather than a record of the life. But the silences are not total, and where the events leak through they leak through as exercises.

His children are the notable leak. Stoic doctrine told him a child’s death was not an evil, and he quotes the hardest version of that doctrine from Epictetus. But he also prays, at 9.40, not to lose his little son, before correcting the prayer, and at 10.35 he catches himself wishing his children to live and files the wish under a diseased eye. Marcus lost at least half of his children, several of them before he began writing. The doctrine is on the page; so is the effort it cost.

Context, critics and use

Whom else he uses

  • Socrates is named in eleven entries, more than anyone else: as the model of a soul content to be just (7.66), the man who could abstain and enjoy (1.16), who called popular opinions bogeys (11.23).
  • Heraclitus supplies the flux and the fire (4.46), and the sleepers who are also workers (6.42).
  • Plato is quoted for the view from above (7.48) and, from the Apology, for staying at one’s post (7.44–45); his Republic is the ideal not to wait for (9.29).
  • Epicurus, the rival school’s founder, is quoted with approval on pain (7.64) and on talking philosophy in sickness (9.41). The Epicurean atoms are the alternative he keeps open.
  • The poets: Homer’s leaves (10.34), Euripides on the earth loving the shower (10.21), and lines and half-lines from others, often only a few words long. Tragedy and comedy get a short history of their uses at 11.6.

The political difference between the schools matters for reading him. Epicureans advised withdrawal from public life. Stoics demanded the opposite: hold office, raise children, fight the war, “a Roman, and a ruler” at his post (3.5). That is why Stoicism became the philosophy of Rome’s governing class, and why the modern version, a private technique for calm, loses so much of what Marcus meant.

Checked against what we now know

What held up

Marcus makes no forecasts in the ordinary sense, but the book is full of claims about the mind, the world and memory that can be checked. Our verdicts:

Held

Feelings follow judgements 4.7, 8.47, 11.18

The founding premise of cognitive therapy. Aaron Beck and his colleagues traced the philosophical origins of their method to the Stoics in 1979, and Albert Ellis credited Epictetus. Challenging the judgement to change the feeling is now one of the best-tested treatments in psychology.

Partly

You will soon be forgotten 4.6, 7.21, 8.25

Wrong about himself: he is read eighteen and a half centuries later. Right about almost everyone else he names. Cadicianus, Fabius, Julianus and Lepidus (4.50), Philistion, Phoebus and Origanion (6.47) survive only as names in his list, and nobody knows who they were.

Held

Nothing comes from nothing 4.4, 5.13, 8.50

Matter is not destroyed but reworked, and the body is rebuilt constantly from food and air (10.7). Chemistry confirmed the conservation of mass in the eighteenth century and physiology the constant turnover of the body’s material.

Missed

The universe is one living being, renewed by fire 4.40, 5.13, 10.7

The Stoic physics behind the ethics, a world-soul and periodic conflagration, has no place in modern cosmology. The ethics turned out to be detachable from it, which is Marcus’s own “either way” argument.

Partly

Forty years show you everything 7.49, 11.1

True of ambition, flattery, court intrigue and grief, which the book describes in terms any modern office would recognise. False of knowledge and technology: the world of his readers would have been unimaginable to him, starting with the printing press that made his notebook a bestseller.

Partly

People do wrong from ignorance 2.1, 7.63, 11.18

Research on perpetrators, notably Roy Baumeister’s Evil (1997), finds that most people who do harm believe themselves justified or provoked, as Marcus says. That wrongdoing is always “involuntary” is harder to defend.

Held

Anger harms the angry 11.18

His eighth rule says anger costs more than its cause. Studies of chronic anger and hostility link them with heart disease; a 2009 meta-analysis by Yoichi Chida and Andrew Steptoe found a modest but real association with future coronary disease.

Missed

Christians die from mere obstinacy 11.3

If the phrase is his, he misread the movement completely. Within a century and a half of his death the empire had a Christian emperor, and within two centuries Christianity was its official religion.

Held

Rule cannot change minds by decree 9.29

“Without a change of opinions what else is there than the slavery of men who groan while they pretend to obey?” A fair summary of how imposed reform tends to fare, from Rome to the twentieth century, and a check on his own power that few rulers wrote down.

Disputes

Where it’s argued with

Diary of a sad man, or exercise book?

Nineteenth-century readers such as Matthew Arnold (1863) and Ernest Renan (1882) heard a melancholy soul, lonely on his throne, resigned rather than hopeful. Pierre Hadot (1992) answered that the bleak passages, sex as friction, purple as shellfish blood, life as a bag of blood and smell, are a deliberate technique, the “physical definition” of 3.11, performed to loosen desire. On Hadot’s reading they tell us about the method, not the mood. Most scholars now take a middle view: the exercises are technique, but the choice of what to exercise against is revealing.

Was Book 1 written last?

Heinrich Schenkl, the book’s early twentieth-century editor, argued that Book 1, finished and orderly unlike the rest, was composed late and set at the front; C. R. Haines accepted this in his Loeb edition of 1916. It cannot be proved, and some think Book 1 was simply written as a separate piece at any time.

Opium

Thomas W. Africa’s article “The Opium Addiction of Marcus Aurelius” (1961) noted that Galen, the emperor’s physician, prescribed him a daily theriac containing poppy juice, and that when Galen removed the poppy Marcus could not sleep. Africa read the book’s cosmic visions as drug effects. Pierre Hadot replied that the view from above and the vastness of time are standard exercises found in Plato, Cicero and Seneca, and that the prescription shows no sign of addiction. The thesis now has few defenders.

Was he a Stoic at all?

In a 1982 essay John Rist pointed to the constant “providence or atoms,” the doubt about survival after death (12.5) and the pessimism, and asked whether Marcus was a Stoic at all. Christopher Gill (2013) answers that the disjunctions are an old argumentative device that proves his ethics hold even on the rival’s assumptions, and that the core is orthodox.

The Christians

The single mention (11.3) is grammatically awkward, and Haines (1916) called it “pretty certainly a gloss.” The persecutions are real: Justin Martyr was condemned in Rome around 165 by the city prefect, who was Marcus’s own teacher Junius Rusticus, and the martyrs of Lyon died in 177. How far Marcus directed either is disputed. John Stuart Mill (1859) made him the classic case of a good ruler doing wrong through certainty: if Marcus could persecute, anyone could.

Commodus

From Nerva in 96, emperors had adopted their successors; Marcus chose his son, and Commodus was a disaster. Machiavelli (in his Discourses on Livy, around 1517) and Gibbon (1776) made the choice the end of Rome’s best age. The biographer Anthony Birley (1966, revised 1987) and most modern historians answer that none of the adoptive emperors had a surviving son; passing over Commodus would have made him a rallying point for civil war. The notebook, written in those very years, says nothing either way.

One law for all, and slavery

Marcus praises “a polity in which there is the same law for all” (1.14) and calls every rational being his kin and fellow citizen (4.4). He ruled a society in which millions were legal property, and nothing in the twelve books treats that as a problem, though the teacher he revered, Epictetus, had been a slave. Slaves appear in the book as property he should treat well (5.31) and as possessions the vulgar admire (6.14). The universalism is sincere and it has edges.

The self-help Marcus

Since the 2010s the book has become a bestseller, promoted as a manual for resilience and productivity. Hadot’s own point cuts against the most stripped-down versions: the exercises were meant to serve justice and the common good (the discipline of action) and to rest on a view of the whole (the discipline of desire). A reading that keeps only the calm keeps one discipline of three.

Transmission

How it survived

For most of its history the book was nearly lost. It survived through a handful of copies in Byzantium, reached print in the Reformation, and became a mass-market book only in the last twenty years.

Its deepest modern afterlife is clinical. Albert Ellis, founding what became rational emotive behaviour therapy in the 1950s, credited Epictetus, and Aaron Beck’s cognitive therapy made the same move: find the automatic thought, examine the belief behind it, test it. What carried over was the machinery. What did not was the purpose: Marcus examines his judgements because some are false, and a life built on false judgements is not a good life, whatever it feels like.

Versions

One sentence, four translators

The Greek is terse, often ungrammatical, sometimes corrupt, and every translation is an interpretation. Here is 7.21 as it has come into English over three centuries.

  • What the Victorians left out. At 6.13, where Marcus describes sex as clinically as he describes wine and purple, Long simply omits the clause. Haines translates it. Check for gaps if you read a nineteenth-century version.
  • Thou or you. Every public-domain version uses “thou,” which suits the self-address but distances it. Modern versions (Staniforth 1964, Hays 2002, Martin Hammond 2006, Robin Hard 2011, Robin Waterfield 2021) use “you.”
  • Why this page quotes Long. His is the complete, proofread text we could check every quotation against. He is literal, faithful and old-fashioned; for a first read, a modern translation is easier.

Terms

Glossary

Ruling part (hegemonikon)
The reasoning, choosing centre of a person; the only part that is truly “thine” (12.3).
Daemon
The divine spark within, “a portion of” Zeus given as guardian (5.27). Keeping it clean is the aim of the whole book.
Logos
Reason, both the reason in each person and the rational order that runs the universe.
Impression (phantasia)
The way an event presents itself to the mind, before judgement. “Wipe out the imagination” means erase the added judgement (7.29).
Assent
Accepting an impression as true. Under the discipline of assent the mind “assents to nothing false or uncertain” (8.7).
Impulse
The movement toward action; the discipline of action directs it to “social acts.”
Desire and aversion
Wanting and shunning; Stoics restrict both to what is in our power (8.7, 11.37).
Choice (prohairesis)
The faculty of will; “no man can rob us of our free will” (11.36).
Passions
Fear, grief, lust, anger: disturbances based on false judgements. Freedom from them (apatheia) is the Stoic ideal (8.48).
Indifferents
Everything neither virtue nor vice: health, wealth, fame, death. They “happen equally to good men and bad” (2.11).
Preferred indifferents
Indifferents it is rational to choose, like health, though they are not goods; hence “in things indifferent I attempt to ascertain the value of each” (3.11).
Appropriate act
What a role or relation calls for: the duties of a son, a friend, an emperor.
Right act (katorthoma)
An appropriate act done from a perfect disposition; Marcus says the word signifies that such acts “proceed by the right road” (5.14).
Reservation
Aiming at an outcome “conditionally,” so that failure is not a harm (6.50, 8.41).
Physical definition
Describing a thing in bare physical terms to strip off its prestige (3.11, 6.13).
View from above
Imagining the earth from a height to see human affairs at their scale (7.48, 9.30, 12.24).
Cosmopolis
The world as one city of all rational beings, “the most ancient city and polity” (2.16, 4.4).
Sympathy
The interconnection of all parts of the universe: “all things are implicated with one another” (6.38).
Conflagration
The Stoic doctrine that the universe is periodically consumed by fire and reborn; Marcus mentions it only as one possibility (10.7).
Eudaimonia
Happiness, which Marcus glosses by its etymology as “a good daemon” (7.17).
Smooth flow
The Stoic image of the good life as a stream running easily; “a life which flows in quiet” (2.5), “an equable flow of happiness” (5.34).
Hypomnemata
Personal notes for rereading, the genre the Meditations belong to.
Aequanimitas
Equanimity, the watchword Antoninus Pius gave on his deathbed; Marcus defines it as “the voluntary acceptance of the things which are assigned to thee by the common nature” (10.8).

Using it

How to read it

  1. Pick a modern translation first

    Gregory Hays (2002) is the plainest and most alive; Martin Hammond (Penguin, 2006) and Robin Hard (Oxford, 2011) are closer to the Greek with better notes; Robin Waterfield’s annotated edition (2021) explains almost every line. Long and Haines are free and faithful, but read them second.

  2. Start with Book 2

    Seventeen entries, the whole method in miniature, and the morning exercise that opens it.

  3. Then Book 1, as a portrait

    Read 1.16 on Antoninus Pius slowly: it is the person Marcus was trying to become.

  4. Sample, don’t march

    The book has no argument to follow and two long sittings produce fatigue. A few entries at a time, and reread.

  5. Use the threads

    When a line strikes you, find its cousins: the tables above list where he returns to providence, death, fame and anger.

  6. Keep it near the bed

    Different entries surface at different points of life, as you would expect of a book organised around problems rather than topics.

Read alongside

  • Epictetus, Discourses and Handbook: the source of nearly everything Marcus practises.
  • Pierre Hadot, The Inner Citadel (1992; English 1998): the book that explains the three disciplines.
  • Anthony Birley, Marcus Aurelius: A Biography (1966, revised 1987): the life and the wars.
  • Seneca, Letters to Lucilius: the Stoic courtier’s version, written for readers.
  • Livy, The History of Rome, summarised on this site: the Republic whose heroes Marcus lists as forgotten.
  • Taleb, Fooled by Randomness, summarised on this site: a modern use of Stoic dignity under bad luck.
