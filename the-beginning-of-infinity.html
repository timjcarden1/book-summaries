Deutsch’s two principles, the book in eight words

The Beginning of Infinity

Explanations That Transform the World · David Deutsch · 2011

The whole book in one breath

For most of history, knowledge barely grew. Then, with the scientific revolution and the Enlightenment, it began to grow without limit. Deutsch argues this happened because people stopped looking to authority and started seeking good explanations: theories that are hard to vary, open to criticism, and able to reach far beyond the problems they were invented for. Knowledge doesn’t come from observation or induction. It comes from bold conjecture corrected by criticism, the method Karl Popper described, and the same logic runs through evolution, science, art and politics. People, as universal explainers, are therefore cosmically significant, not chemical scum on an ordinary planet. Every evil is a problem we don’t yet know how to solve, and societies flourish when they protect the error-correction that makes progress possible. The growth of knowledge has no natural end. We are always at the beginning of infinity.

The author

A founder of quantum computing, writing about everything

David Deutsch, born in Haifa in 1953, is a physicist at Oxford. His 1985 paper describing a universal quantum computer helped found the field of quantum computation, and he is the best-known defender of the Everett “many-worlds” reading of quantum theory. His first book, The Fabric of Reality (1997), argued that four theories (quantum physics, evolution, computation and Popper’s epistemology) together form a single worldview.

The Beginning of Infinity extends that project from physics to civilisation. Its chapters range over science, mathematics, artificial intelligence, aesthetics, voting systems and the history of ideas. They share one theme: how knowledge is created, and why its growth need never stop.

The argument

Knowledge is explanation, and explanation has no ceiling

  • Knowledge comes from conjecture, not observation. We guess explanations and correct them by criticism and experiment. Nothing is “derived” from the senses.
  • Good explanations are hard to vary. Every part does work, so the explanation can’t be tweaked to fit any outcome.
  • Good explanations have reach. They solve problems far beyond the ones they were created for.
  • Everything not forbidden by the laws of nature is achievable, given the right knowledge.
  • So people matter cosmically, and the societies that protect criticism and error-correction are the ones that make progress.

If you remember nothing else

Twelve ideas that carry the book

Ch. 1

Seek explanations that are hard to vary

A myth can be rewritten to fit anything. A good explanation breaks if you change any part, which is why it can be tested and trusted.

Ch. 1

Explanations have reach

Theories built for one problem turn out to explain vastly more. The physics of falling apples explains distant galaxies.

Ch. 1–2

We never simply observe

All observation is interpreted through theories. Empiricism and induction are myths about how knowledge grows.

Ch. 3

People are significant

Earth isn’t a life-support system built for us, and we aren’t cosmically insignificant. Knowledge-creators can transform their environment on any scale.

Ch. 4

Knowledge is created in two ways

Genes (by evolution) and ideas (by creativity) both grow knowledge through variation and selection. Nothing else can put knowledge into the world.

Ch. 5

Abstractions are real

Numbers, laws and knowledge are part of reality because the best explanations of physical events need them.

Ch. 6

Systems can jump to universality

Alphabets, positional numbers, computers and people each crossed a threshold where a small set of rules suddenly covers everything.

Ch. 7

Real AI must be creative

Computation is universal, so artificial general intelligence is possible. But it needs a theory of creativity, not better tricks for passing tests.

Ch. 9

The future can’t be prophesied

Predicting future knowledge would mean having it now. Pessimistic prophecies fail because they can’t count solutions not yet found.

Ch. 13

Judge institutions by error-correction

Good political systems let you remove bad rulers and policies without violence. That matters more than perfectly aggregating preferences.

Ch. 15–16

Static societies suppress criticism

For most of history, cultures preserved themselves by disabling criticism. Dynamic societies, which let ideas be criticised, are rare and recent.

Ch. 17

Sustainability is the wrong ideal

Static “sustainable” societies fail when problems outrun their knowledge. The only sustainable thing is the growth of knowledge itself.

The book, in order

Eighteen chapters

The book is not formally divided into parts. Its chapters move from how explanations work, through universality and infinity, to optimism, politics, culture and creativity. The rail groups them by theme.

Chapter 1

The Reach of Explanations

What we know is mostly explanation, and good explanations are hard to vary.

  • Most of our knowledge of reality, such as the cores of stars or the history of the Earth, concerns things no one has seen. It is explanation, not observation.
  • Empiricism (knowledge from the senses) and inductivism (generalising from instances) are wrong. Theories are conjectured first, then tested and criticised, as Popper argued.
  • The mark of a good explanation is that it is hard to vary while still accounting for what it explains (see the figure below).
  • Good explanations have reach. They explain far more than their authors intended, which is why knowledge can grow without bound.
  • The Enlightenment was, at root, the rejection of authority as the source of knowledge and the adoption of a tradition of criticism.

Chapter 2

Closer to Reality

Science reaches reality through theory-laden interpretation, not raw perception.

  • Even looking through a telescope depends on layers of theory about light, optics, instruments and our own senses. There is no theory-free seeing.
  • That doesn’t make knowledge subjective. Theories that survive criticism bring us closer to how things are, even of things we can never directly sense.

Chapter 3

The Spark

Against the idea that humans are insignificant, and against Earth as a spaceship made for us.

  • The Principle of Mediocrity says there is nothing special about humans. Deutsch cites Stephen Hawking’s description of humanity as chemical scum on a moderate-sized planet, and disagrees.
  • The Spaceship Earth idea says the biosphere is a benign life-support system we must not disturb. In fact almost all of the environment, including most of Earth for most of history, is lethal to unaided humans. What keeps us alive is knowledge.
  • Anything that creates explanatory knowledge is cosmically significant. In principle it can affect the fate of stars, which unthinking matter cannot.

Chapter 4

Creation

Where knowledge comes from: variation and selection, in genes and in minds.

  • Adaptations like eyes embody knowledge. The question is how that knowledge got there.
  • Creationism and Lamarckism are bad explanations. They restate the appearance of design without explaining how the knowledge was created. Neo-Darwinism explains it: random variation, selected by survival.
  • Deutsch also treats the “fine-tuning” of physical constants and anthropic arguments, holding them to the same standard of good explanation.

Chapter 5

The Reality of Abstractions

Abstract things are real because good explanations need them.

  • Reductionism says everything is best explained at the lowest level (particles). Deutsch argues that high-level explanations are often the true ones.
  • His example is a chain of dominoes set up as a computer testing whether 641 is prime. Why does a particular domino never fall? The real explanation is not the physics of dominoes but the fact that 641 is prime.
  • So numbers, laws and knowledge are real, though they are not physical objects.

Chapter 6

The Jump to Universality

A small change in a system can suddenly let it do everything in its domain.

  • Early writing used a symbol per word. An alphabet, with a few dozen letters, can write any word, including ones not yet coined.
  • Roman numerals needed new symbols as numbers grew. Positional notation can write any number with ten digits (see the figure below).
  • Computers made the same jump. Babbage imagined it, and Turing proved that one machine can compute anything computable. So did the genetic code, and so did people, who are universal explainers.
  • Universality often arrives almost by accident, as a by-product of making a system more regular.

Chapter 7

Artificial Creativity

Artificial general intelligence is possible, and the field has been looking in the wrong place.

  • Because computation is universal, any process a brain performs can in principle be run on a computer. So AGI is possible.
  • But AI research has made little progress on creativity, because there is no theory of how it works. Programs that fool judges, like early chatbots, show only that the Turing test can be gamed.
  • A true AGI would be a person, a creator of explanations, and would deserve the rights of one.

Chapter 8

A Window on Infinity

Infinity is strange but real, and understanding it teaches humility about intuition.

  • Deutsch’s Infinity Hotel, his version of Hilbert’s, is always full yet can always take more guests (see the figure below).
  • Infinities defy intuition: there are as many even numbers as numbers. Deutsch uses them to show how reason can go where intuition fails.
  • Mathematical proofs are physical processes, so our knowledge even of mathematics depends on the laws of physics.

Chapter 9

Optimism

Every evil is a problem we don’t yet know how to solve.

  • The principle of optimism holds that all evils are caused by insufficient knowledge. This isn’t a mood or a prediction. It is a claim about what is possible under the laws of physics.
  • Prophecy is impossible where knowledge will grow, because to predict future discoveries you would need to have made them already. Malthus and later prophets of doom failed for exactly that reason.
  • Blind pessimism is no safer than blind optimism. The precautionary principle, which says do nothing not known to be safe, would have stopped every advance, and it leaves us defenceless against dangers we haven’t imagined.

Chapter 10

A Dream of Socrates

A dialogue about knowledge, set in ancient Athens.

  • Socrates dreams of a conversation with the god Hermes about how anyone can know anything, and why the oracle called him wisest.
  • Hermes steers him toward fallibilism: knowledge comes from conjecture and criticism, not from revelation or the senses.
  • The dialogue contrasts Athens, briefly a dynamic, critical society, with static Sparta.

Chapter 11

The Multiverse

Quantum theory, taken literally, describes many parallel histories.

  • Deutsch defends the Everett interpretation: the equations of quantum theory describe a multiverse of histories that mostly go their separate ways but can interfere.
  • He introduces the idea of fungibility: identical instances of an object are not merely alike but have no individual identity, which is how branching works.
  • Rejecting the multiverse, he argues, means refusing to explain what the equations plainly describe.

Chapter 12

A Physicist’s History of Bad Philosophy

Some philosophy is worse than false: it stops knowledge from growing.

  • Bad philosophy doesn’t just err. It closes off inquiry. Examples include instrumentalism (theories are only prediction tools), positivism, behaviourism and much of postmodernism.
  • In physics, the instrumentalist “just calculate” attitude to quantum theory discouraged the search for explanation for decades.

Chapter 13

Choices

No voting rule is perfect, so judge institutions by how well they correct mistakes.

  • Social-choice theory proves uncomfortable results. Arrow’s theorem shows no voting rule satisfies all reasonable conditions. The Balinski–Young theorem shows that every fair way of apportioning seats produces paradoxes (see the figure below).
  • Deutsch concludes that the goal of political institutions isn’t to aggregate preferences perfectly. It is to allow bad policies and leaders to be removed without violence, which is Popper’s criterion.
  • On that basis he favours plurality voting systems that produce clear winners who can be clearly blamed and replaced.

Chapter 14

Why Are Flowers Beautiful?

Some beauty is objective, and flowers show why.

  • Flowers evolved to attract insects, yet humans, who were never their target, find them beautiful.
  • Deutsch’s explanation: signals between different species can’t rely on shared, arbitrary tastes. They have to be hard to fake, which pushes them toward objective beauty that any sufficiently sophisticated mind can recognise.
  • Aesthetics, like science, can therefore involve real knowledge that can grow.

Chapter 15

The Evolution of Culture

Ideas replicate, and some survive by disabling the criticism that would kill them.

  • Memes, ideas passed from mind to mind, evolve like genes.
  • Rational memes survive because they are useful or true and can withstand criticism. Anti-rational memes survive by suppressing criticism of themselves.
  • Static societies, almost all societies in history, are held in place by anti-rational memes and change so slowly that no one notices. Dynamic societies are rare and recent.

Chapter 16

The Evolution of Creativity

Why did creativity evolve at all, if societies suppressed innovation?

  • A puzzle: human creativity is enormously powerful, yet for most of our history it produced almost no innovation.
  • Deutsch’s answer is that creativity evolved to copy memes faithfully. To reproduce a behaviour you must guess the meaning behind it, and that takes creativity. Innovation was a side-effect.

Chapter 17

Unsustainable

Against sustainability as an ideal, and against explaining history by geography.

  • Easter Island is usually told as a parable of ecological collapse, as in Jared Diamond’s Collapse. Deutsch reads it as the failure of a static society that couldn’t create the knowledge to solve its problems.
  • He rejects Diamond’s Guns, Germs, and Steel account as the main explanation of history: environments may shape early progress, but what matters is whether a society adopts a tradition of criticism.
  • A “sustainable” society in the sense of unchanging is only sustainable until the next problem. The only sustainable thing is the growth of knowledge.
  • On climate change he argues for creating knowledge and wealth to adapt and to solve it, not for halting progress.

Chapter 18

The Beginning

We are, and will always be, at the beginning of infinity.

  • The book’s threads come together: explanation, universality, fallibilism, optimism and the dynamic society.
  • Because every solution creates new and better problems, there is no end state, no final theory and no finished civilisation. There is only unbounded growth, if we keep the traditions of criticism alive.

Deutsch vs the default view

The book argues against a set of assumptions many educated readers hold without noticing.

Context

Where it has been argued with

Sweep and tone

Reviewers widely admired the ambition and many found the book exhilarating. Some philosophers thought its dismissals of empiricism, induction and “bad philosophy” too sweeping, and its confidence grandiose.

Many worlds

The Everett interpretation remains one option among several. Many physicists reject it or regard the question as open, so Chapter 11 presents a contested view with more certainty than the field has.

Can optimism be tested?

Critics ask whether “all evils are caused by insufficient knowledge” is a claim that could ever be shown false. Writers on existential risk, such as Nick Bostrom, stress catastrophes a civilisation might not survive to learn from.

Geography and history

Defenders of Jared Diamond reply that environment shapes the opportunities for creativity without denying it. The two views may be less opposed than Chapter 17 suggests. See both summaries on this site.

AI since 2011

Large language models have reopened Chapter 7’s questions. Deutsch has argued in interviews that they are not creative in his sense, and others disagree.

Terms of art

Glossary

Good explanation
One that is hard to vary while still accounting for what it purports to explain.
Reach
An explanation’s power to solve problems beyond those it was created for.
Fallibilism
The view that no knowledge is certain and all of it can be improved.
Empiricism
The mistaken view that knowledge is derived from sense experience.
Instrumentalism
The view that theories are only tools for prediction, not explanations of reality.
Principle of Mediocrity
The claim that humans are unremarkable in the cosmos. Deutsch rejects it.
Spaceship Earth
The idea of the biosphere as a life-support system designed for us. Deutsch rejects that too.
Universality
The ability of a system to handle every case in its domain, as alphabets, positional numbers, computers and people do.
Principle of optimism
All evils are caused by insufficient knowledge.
Static society
One that preserves itself by suppressing criticism and changes too slowly for anyone to notice.
Dynamic society
One sustained by criticism and rapid change, like the post-Enlightenment West.
Rational / anti-rational meme
Ideas that survive by withstanding criticism, versus ideas that survive by disabling it.
Popper’s criterion
Good government is government you can remove without violence.
Fungibility
The complete interchangeability of identical instances, central to Deutsch’s multiverse.

Chapters build on one another loosely. If you want the core:

    Good explanations and reach: the foundation of everything after.

  2. Chapters 3 and 6

    Why people matter, and the jump to universality.

  3. Chapter 9

    Optimism and the impossibility of prophecy.

  4. Chapters 13 and 15

    Politics as error-correction, and static versus dynamic societies.

  5. Chapters 17–18

    Sustainability, Easter Island and the argument with Diamond.

Read alongside

  • Karl Popper, Objective Knowledge, summarised on this site: the epistemology underneath.
  • David Deutsch, The Fabric of Reality (1997): the physics and computation side of the same worldview.
  • Jared Diamond, Guns, Germs, and Steel, summarised on this site: the account Chapter 17 contests.
  • Matt Ridley, The Rational Optimist (2010), and Steven Pinker, Enlightenment Now (2018): optimism argued from the data.
