Problems are inevitable.Problems are soluble.

The two sentences Deutsch proposes carving in stone, Chapter 3

The Beginning of Infinity

Explanations That Transform the World · David Deutsch · 2011

The whole book in one breath

Rapid, lasting progress has happened only once in human history. It began around the time of the scientific revolution and is still under way. Deutsch’s explanation is that people stopped looking to authority for knowledge and started seeking good explanations: accounts that are hard to vary, open to criticism, and able to reach far beyond the problems they were invented to solve. Knowledge doesn’t flow in through the senses. It is guessed and then corrected, as Karl Popper argued, and the same pattern of variation and selection runs through biological evolution, science, morality, art and politics. Everything the laws of physics permit can be done by anyone who knows how. That makes people, the only known creators of explanations, cosmically significant. It means every evil comes from not knowing enough, and societies last when they protect the traditions of criticism that correct errors. From there the book goes on to universal computers, Hilbert’s infinite hotel, the quantum multiverse, voting paradoxes, the beauty of flowers, how culture evolves, Easter Island and climate change. The growth of knowledge has no natural end, so we are always at its beginning.

The author

A founder of quantum computing, writing about everything

David Deutsch was born in Haifa in 1953 and grew up in London. He studied at Cambridge and took his doctorate at Oxford under the cosmologist Dennis Sciama, whose argument about anthropic reasoning reappears in Chapter 4. He is a visiting professor of physics at Oxford, attached to its Centre for Quantum Computation, and has worked largely outside the usual academic round of teaching and administration.

His 1985 paper, “Quantum theory, the Church–Turing principle and the universal quantum computer”, described a computer that runs on quantum physics and could simulate any physical system. It helped found the field of quantum computation. He is also the best-known living defender of Hugh Everett’s “many-worlds” reading of quantum theory, which he takes to be what the theory plainly says rather than an interpretation of it. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 2008. In 2023 he shared the Breakthrough Prize in Fundamental Physics with Charles Bennett, Gilles Brassard and Peter Shor for the foundations of quantum information.

His first book, The Fabric of Reality (1997), argued that four theories form one worldview: quantum physics, the theory of computation, Darwinian evolution and Popper’s theory of knowledge. The Beginning of Infinity (Allen Lane, 2011) carries that worldview from physics into history, politics, morality and art. Its thread is a single question: how is knowledge created, and why need its growth never stop? Karl Popper is the book’s intellectual father. Deutsch calls him “the twentieth century’s foremost proponent of the British Enlightenment”, and Chapter 10 admits that its theory of knowledge is “largely that of the twentieth-century philosopher Karl Popper, together with some addenda of my own”.

The book has an unusual device. Most chapters end with the chapter’s own glossary, a list of the “meanings of ‘the beginning of infinity’ encountered in this chapter”, and a summary. This page reports those lists where the book gives them.

The argument

Knowledge is explanation, and explanation has no ceiling

  • Progress has happened once. “Progress that is both rapid enough to be noticed and stable enough to continue over many generations has been achieved only once in the history of our species.” It began with the scientific revolution and the Enlightenment, and it covers science, technology, political institutions, moral values and art.
  • Knowledge is conjectured, not derived. Nothing is read off the senses or generalised from repeated instances. Theories are guesses, and experience is used to choose between guesses already made.
  • Good explanations are hard to vary. Every part does work. A myth can be adjusted to fit anything, which is why it explains nothing.
  • Good explanations have reach. They solve problems far beyond the ones they were created for, and that is why knowledge can grow without bound.
  • Everything not forbidden by the laws of nature is achievable, given the right knowledge. So people, the only known creators of explanations, are the most significant phenomena in the universe, not “chemical scum”.
  • Many systems jump to universality. Alphabets, positional numerals, computers, the genetic code and human minds each crossed a threshold where a small set of rules suddenly covers a whole domain.
  • The future can’t be prophesied. Future knowledge can’t be known in advance. Prophecies that ignore it are biased towards pessimism, from Malthus to modern doom-sayers.
  • Institutions should be judged by error-correction. Following Popper, a good political system is one that lets people remove bad rulers and policies without violence. Perfectly representing preferences is logically impossible anyway.
  • Societies are static or dynamic. Almost every culture in history survived by disabling its members’ creativity. Ours is the first long-lived exception, and it is still in an unstable transition.
  • Only progress is sustainable. Static societies fail when a problem outruns their knowledge. The safe course is more knowledge and more wealth, not less change.

All evils are caused by insufficient knowledge.The principle of optimism, Chapter 9

If you remember nothing else

Twelve ideas that carry the book

Ch. 1

Seek explanations that are hard to vary

Persephone’s myth could explain any weather. The axis-tilt theory of the seasons breaks if you change any part, which is why it could be tested and why it is knowledge.

Ch. 1

Explanations have reach

A theory devised to explain the sun’s height in the sky also predicts reversed seasons in the southern hemisphere and six-month days near the poles, places its authors had never seen.

Ch. 1–2

All observation is theory-laden

Instruments bring us closer to reality even as they put more machinery between us and it. What makes an observation is an explanation of the evidence, not the act of looking.

Ch. 3

People are significant

The biosphere would kill us, and the universe is mostly cold, dark and empty. But almost any place in it could support knowledge-creation, and knowledge can reshape matter on any scale.

Ch. 4

Knowledge has two known sources

Biological evolution and human creativity both build knowledge by variation and selection. Creationism, spontaneous generation and Lamarckism all deny that knowledge has to be created.

Ch. 5

Abstractions are real

Why does a domino in a computer made of dominoes never fall? “Because 641 is prime.” The best explanation is often at a high level of emergence.

Ch. 6

Error-correction is the beginning of infinity

Only digital systems, which correct small errors at every step, can make the jump to universality. That is why there are universal computers and no universal analogue ones.

Ch. 7

Imitating thought is not thinking

Computation is universal, so artificial general intelligence is possible. It needs a theory of creativity, not ever better tricks for passing the Turing test.

Ch. 9

Prediction is not prophecy

No one in 1900 thought the internet unlikely. They didn’t conceive of it at all. Forecasts that ignore future knowledge are prophecy, and prophecy leans pessimistic.

Ch. 13

Judge institutions by error-correction

Arrow’s theorem shows there is no coherent “will of the people”. What matters is how easily bad rulers and policies can be removed without violence.

Ch. 15–16

Creativity evolved to preserve, not invent

Static societies suppress innovation, yet creativity evolved inside them. It evolved to copy memes, because copying a meaning means guessing it.

Ch. 17

Sustainability is the disease

“In the pessimistic conception, that distinctive ability of people is a disease for which sustainability is the cure. In the optimistic one, sustainability is the disease and people are the cure.”

How the book is built

Eighteen chapters, one phrase with many meanings

The book has no formal parts. It opens with how explanations work (Chapters 1–5), turns to universality and infinity (6–8), then to optimism, knowledge and politics (9–13), and closes with beauty, culture, creativity and the future (14–18). Chapters 10 and 12 are partly dialogues, and Chapter 11 is a piece of science fiction that turns into real physics.

The title phrase does a lot of work. Deutsch’s idea is that many different things mark the moment when open-ended progress becomes possible, and that these are facets of one attribute of reality. Here are the meanings he lists at the end of each chapter.

Chapter 1

The Reach of Explanations

What we know is mostly explanation of things no one has seen, and the mark of a good explanation is that it is hard to vary.

“Behind it all is surely an idea so simple, so beautiful, that when we grasp it — in a decade, a century, or a millennium — we will all say to each other, how could it have been otherwise?”John Archibald Wheeler (1986), the chapter’s motto

Stars: what the eye sees and what we know

  • To the eye, the night sky is a few thousand dots. The astronomer’s account is of suns 150 million kilometres away and further, powered by transmuting hydrogen into helium, converting millions of tonnes of mass into energy every second.
  • Transmutation was first shown in a laboratory in 1901, by Frederick Soddy and Ernest Rutherford. Alchemists never managed it. “Base metals can be transmuted into gold by stars, and by intelligent beings who understand the processes that power stars, but by nothing else in the universe.”
  • The universe is full of violent phenomena. Several supernovae a second go off within range of the best telescopes, and we owe most of our elements to them. In March 2008 a gamma-ray burst 7.5 billion light years away, brighter than a million supernovae, was visible to the naked eye for a few seconds. Quasars shine with the power of a trillion suns.
  • “I do not know which is more awesome: the phenomena themselves or the fact that we know so much about them.” Our eyes detect only light that has entered them. Everything else we know about stars, we know “only from theory”.

Against empiricism and induction

  • Empiricism says knowledge is derived from the senses: John Locke’s mind as “white paper” in 1689, or the “Book of Nature” waiting to be read. Deutsch: “scientific theories are not ‘derived’ from anything … They are guesses — bold conjectures.” The role of experience is to choose between theories that have already been guessed.
  • Empiricism was historically useful. It rejected the authority of holy books, priests and academics, and was optimistic compared with medieval fatalism. But it could not explain how anything gets from the senses into a theory. “No one would expect to deduce the geography of Mars from a map of Earth.”
  • Inductivism says we generalise from repeated experience: the sun has always risen, so it will rise again, under a “principle of induction” that the future will resemble the past. Two misconceptions sink it. First, most theoretical knowledge is about reality, not experience, and no one has experienced a billion years. Second, the future is often unlike the past.
  • People who flew for the first time didn’t induce flight from experience: “they discovered good explanatory theories about flying, and then they flew — in that order.” The first nuclear fission explosion in 1945 was predicted, not extrapolated. From orbit the sun rises every ninety minutes, or never. To say the future will “resemble” the past in obeying the same laws is empty until you know which laws.
  • Even noticing a repetition needs a theory: a cloudy day, a mirror or a video can make the same thing look different or different things look the same.
  • Sherlock Holmes said, “It is a capital mistake to theorize before one has data.” Deutsch: “But that was itself a capital mistake.” All observation is theory-laden, a term he takes from Popper. We even experience our nerve signals as located out there, as a blue sky or a headache.

Authority, justification and fallibilism

  • Empiricism set up two false authorities, sensory experience and “derivation”, and inherited an ideal of knowledge as justified true belief. That turns “how do we know …?” into “by what authority do we claim …?”. Deutsch calls this justificationism. It converts the quest for truth into a quest for certainty, which is a feeling, or for endorsement, which is a social status.
  • Fallibilism is the recognition that there are no authoritative sources of knowledge and no reliable means of justifying ideas. “It is fallibilism, not mere rejection of authority, that is essential for the initiation of unlimited knowledge growth — the beginning of infinity.”
  • For most of history there was no progress, although human brains were anatomically the same as ours, and discoveries like fire, clothing, stone tools and bronze were rare. The scientific revolution and Enlightenment were a rebellion against authority about knowledge. The Royal Society’s motto, adopted after its founding in 1660, was Nullius in verba, “Take no one’s word for it”. Rebellion alone wasn’t enough. What mattered was a tradition of criticism.

Testability is not enough

  • Popper’s criterion of demarcation says scientific theories are testable. Rutherford and Soddy’s radium, appearing in sealed uranium, refuted the idea that transmutation was impossible, while the theory of four elements (earth, air, fire, water) was untestable. Galileo called experiments cimenti, trials by ordeal.
  • But testability doesn’t decide. The prophet who says the sun will come out next Tuesday, the gambler who says “this is my lucky night”, and a flint-knapper’s rules of thumb are all testable, and none is science.
  • Prediction isn’t the purpose of science either. A conjuring trick shows why: you can predict that the cups will turn out empty without having any idea how the trick works. Instrumentalism, the view that theories are only prediction tools, mistakes the means for the end. So does the habit, in statistics, of saying a factor “explains” some percentage of an effect.
  • There is no purely predictive theory. Every rule of thumb relies on background explanations about when it applies. If the conjuror swapped the balls for lighted candles, “That’s not conjuring, it’s actual magic” would be the only prediction left to a pure instrumentalist.
  • A problem exists when ideas conflict. A child with no expectations finds a conjuring trick uninteresting. Popper, in Realism and the Aim of Science (1983): science means meeting a problem, seeing its beauty and falling in love with it, “till death do ye part — unless you should meet another and even more fascinating problem”.

Persephone and the axis tilt

  • The ancient Greek myth: Hades, god of the underworld, kidnapped Persephone, goddess of spring. Her mother Demeter, goddess of the earth and agriculture, negotiated her release, but a magic seed compelled Persephone to return each year, and Demeter’s grief brings winter. A Norse version has Freyr, god of spring, at war with the forces of cold and darkness.
  • The myth is testable: it predicts winter everywhere at once, and the southern hemisphere refutes it. But a defender could vary it at will. Persephone might escape each year and raid the underworld, carrying warm spring air that rises and brings summer. The details were chosen for cultural and artistic reasons, not to account for the seasons. “If an explanation could easily explain anything in the given field, then it actually explains nothing.”
  • The axis-tilt theory is different. The earth’s axis is tilted, so each hemisphere in turn gets more direct sunlight. Every part does work: the tilt, the geometry, the heating. It predicts seasons out of phase between the hemispheres. “If the axis-tilt theory had been refuted, its defenders would have had nowhere to go.”
  • So the Greeks’ mistake was not failing to send expeditions south. With bad explanations testing is useless; they might as well have “stayed at home, and tested the easily testable theory that winter can be staved off by yodelling”. Most false theories can be “rejected out of hand without any experiment, simply for being bad explanations”. Occam’s razor misses the point, since “Demeter did it” is very simple.
  • The asymmetry that drives progress: “inventing falsehoods is easy, and therefore they are easy to vary once found; discovering good explanations is hard, but the harder they are to find, the harder they are to vary once found.”

Reach

  • Aristarchus of Samos, who argued in the third century BCE that the earth moves round the sun, knew a world of Europe, North Africa and parts of Asia. From the axis-tilt theory he could have predicted that north of the Shetlands a day would last six months, that south of Ethiopia there would be no seasons, and that further south still the seasons would be reversed.
  • Suppose you’d rather not know, and amend the theory to say that the tilt works only where we have looked. That turns an explanation into a rule of thumb and denies the cause even at home. The theory “is not yours to modify. It has an autonomous meaning.”
  • Reach is “the ability of some of them to solve problems beyond those that they were created to solve”. The tilt theory was originally about the sun’s height in the sky. It went on to explain out-of-phase seasons, the tropics and the midnight sun. Reach is not a principle of induction: it is discovered after the fact, as part of the explanation.
  • We know gravity works the same on Mars because our only viable explanation of gravity, general relativity, is universal. We don’t know Mars’s map, because nothing explains why its geography should copy Earth’s.
  • A gold nugget found anywhere in the universe came either from a supernova or from an intelligent being who had an explanation. That observation is where Chapter 3 begins.

The chapter’s own terms

Explanation, reach, creativity (“the capacity to create new explanations”), empiricism, theory-laden, inductivism, induction (“the non-existent process”), realism, relativism, instrumentalism, justificationism, fallibilism, background knowledge, rule of thumb, problem (“A problem exists when a conflict between ideas is experienced”), good and bad explanation (hard or easy to vary “while still accounting for what it purports to account for”), the Enlightenment, mini-enlightenment, rational, the West.

Chapter 2

Closer to Reality

Instruments bring us closer to reality even as they put more machinery between us and it. Observation is the explaining of evidence.

  • As a graduate student, Deutsch spent time on an astronomy project that used the Palomar Sky Survey: 1,874 photographic negatives on glass, examined under microscopes, cataloguing the positions of galaxies in clusters such as Coma with cross-hairs. The work relied on rules of thumb, like judging a galaxy by its fuzzy edge, and could be checked against higher-resolution photographs. The project was testing whether dark matter exists, and it succeeded.
  • Galaxies are “mind-bogglingly huge”. But “Feeling insignificant because the universe is large has exactly the same logic as feeling inadequate for not being a cow. Or a herd of cows. The universe is not there to overwhelm us; it is our home, and our resource. The bigger the better.”
  • Thomas Edison said invention was “one per cent inspiration and ninety-nine per cent perspiration”. Deutsch thinks he was misreading his own experience. Computers play chess by mindless exhaustive search. Humans play creatively, and even the routine parts of research can be creative and fun: “A trial that fails is still fun.”
  • He once asked whether a blob was a galaxy or a star. The answer: “Neither … That’s just a defect in the photographic emulsion.” He had overestimated its mass by some fifty powers of ten. The lesson is about what an error in experimental science is: “a mistake about the cause of something”.
  • Every new instrument puts more distance between the astronomer and the star: telescope, camera, glass plate, copying camera, delivery truck, microscope. Astronomers rarely look at the sky. Jocelyn Bell discovered pulsars by studying an ink line drawn by a radio telescope. Yet each step brings us closer to reality. Telescopes with tracking and adaptive optics remove the twinkling of stars, which was only ever a parochial error of seeing through air.
  • “Progress requires the application of ever more knowledge in advance of our observations.” Electron microscopes show dead, stained, frozen cells, and still tell us what living ones are like. Instruments are “like conjuring tricks in reverse”: they fool our senses into seeing what is really there. Iron ore and sand get rearranged into telescopes, and “when astronomers look at them, they see stars.”

Chapter 3

The Spark

Two fashionable ideas are wrong: that humans are cosmically insignificant, and that the earth is a spaceship built to keep us alive.

Two misconceptions

  • Ancient explanations were anthropocentric. Euclid’s geometry was the exception, and astronomers were also astrologers. Science since then has become impersonal: particles, forces and spacetime; no vital principle in biology; thoughts as aggregates of brain processes.
  • From that shift came the Principle of Mediocrity: there is nothing significant about humans. Stephen Hawking put it memorably: we are “just a chemical scum on the surface of a typical planet that’s in orbit round a typical star on the outskirts of a typical galaxy”.
  • The second idea is Spaceship Earth: the biosphere as a life-support system with a finite capacity, like a generation ship we are only passengers on.
  • Both look like truisms, and both oppose arrogance: Mediocrity the pre-Enlightenment arrogance of thinking we are central, Spaceship Earth the Enlightenment arrogance of thinking we are in control. Both are false. Deutsch proposes their negations: “People are significant in the cosmic scheme of things; and The Earth’s biosphere is incapable of supporting human life.”
  • The real error is parochialism, “mistaking appearance for reality, or local regularities for universal laws”. Seasons being in phase across the known world was parochial, not anthropocentric.

A typical place in the universe

  • Our surroundings are highly untypical. About 80 per cent of matter is dark matter. Ordinary matter mostly glows, and even people emit infra-red. Most of it is plasma. The cosmic background radiation is at 2.7 kelvin, and nothing known in nature is colder than about 1 kelvin, except in laboratories that have reached below a billionth of a kelvin. Physicists’ refrigerators may be the coldest places in the universe.
  • Divide space into cubes the size of the solar system. A typical one is intergalactic: pitch black, too far for even a supernova of the nearest star to be visible, at 2.7 kelvin, where everything but helium freezes, and with less than one atom per cubic metre, “a million times sparser than … between the stars”. It holds hydrogen and helium and no chemistry. “Cold, dark and empty.”
  • Intergalactic space would kill an unprotected human in seconds. Primeval Oxfordshire would do it in hours, and a winter night in Oxford can still kill. The life support in Oxfordshire is clothes, houses, farms, hospitals, the electricity grid and sewers, all built by people.

The biosphere is not a life-support system

  • The biosphere is “a death trap for humans rather than a life-support system”. The Great Rift Valley, where our species evolved, is barely more hospitable. Populations live at the edge of disaster, because they grow until their disadvantages balance their growth. About 99.9 per cent of all species that have lived are extinct. Our species narrowly escaped extinction at least once, and our cousin species were wiped out by the supposed life-support system.
  • The first people to live at Oxford’s latitude, possibly Neanderthals, brought knowledge with them: tools, weapons, fire and clothing. It was cultural, not genetic. Deutsch says he himself would die in the primeval Rift Valley. “There are very few fossils of old people.”
  • So “we have never been merely its passengers, nor … its stewards, nor even its maintenance crew: we are its designers and builders.” Humans are also the only species that tries to limit its effect on its descendants and on other species.
  • The Principle of Mediocrity is paradoxical. It singles out anthropocentrism, which is itself anthropocentric, and relies on value-laden words like “arrogance”, “just scum” and “mediocrity”. The ancients’ mistake was not arrogance but parochial error: “their whole problem was that they were not arrogant enough.”

Haldane, Dawkins and the limits of brains

  • Richard Dawkins has argued that our senses evolved to find fruit and avoid predators, not to tell the colours of stars, and that our brains may be similarly parochial. J. B. S. Haldane: “the universe is not only queerer than we suppose, but queerer than we can suppose.” That implies a biological limit to science.
  • Deutsch notes that Mediocrity and this “Haldane–Dawkins” idea share a picture of a “tiny, human-friendly bubble” of comprehensibility. Dawkins himself, in Unweaving the Rainbow (1998), prefers “an orderly universe, one indifferent to human preoccupations, in which everything has an explanation”. An inexplicable universe would be indistinguishable from magic.
  • Birds that fly between islands exploit the universal laws of aerodynamics and optics, but imperfectly. They fail a few kilometres up. Humans, with cultural knowledge that evolves thousands of times faster, went to the moon because Newton’s theories showed what was needed.

Every putative physical transformation … is either — impossible because it is forbidden by the laws of nature; or — achievable, given the right knowledge.The chapter’s key dichotomy

  • The argument: if some transformation were impossible for a reason other than a law of nature, that would be a testable regularity with an explanation, and so a law of nature after all. Haldane–Dawkins therefore doesn’t apply to people.
  • People are “entities that can create explanatory knowledge”. Martin Rees has suggested that “Just as a chimpanzee can’t understand quantum theory, it could be there are aspects of reality that are beyond the capacity of our brains.” Deutsch: “But that cannot be so.” A qualitative inability to understand is an appeal to the supernatural. Einstein: “My pencil and I are more clever than I.”
  • The genome, in Dawkins’s phrase, is “a coded description of ancestral environments”. Primates need vitamin C from their food, yet over a tonne of vitamin C appears in Oxfordshire every week, because people synthesise it. A moon colony is possible with present-day technology: sunlight, recycling, oxygen from the metal oxides in moon rock, rare elements from Earth or asteroid mining.
  • People are universal constructors, “factories for transforming anything into anything that the laws of nature allow”. Losing a limb, or altering one’s genes, doesn’t make anyone less a person: “it is only if you lose your brain” that you would be. Apes cracking nuts have culture, but no explanatory knowledge, so for them the Haldane–Dawkins limit is real.

Problems are inevitable, problems are soluble

  • Open-ended knowledge creation needs only matter, energy and evidence. Evidence deluges the earth’s surface: every roof is struck by evidence that would be worth a Nobel prize to whoever could read it.
  • Problems never end. There is “about one chance in a thousand” per century of a comet or asteroid killing a large part of humanity, and “a typical child born in the United States today is more likely to die as a result of an astronomical event than a car accident”. Colonies in space would be a hedge. Hawking, in the Daily Telegraph in October 2001: “I don’t think the human race will survive the next thousand years, unless we spread into space … But I’m an optimist. We will reach out to the stars.”
  • “An unproblematic state is a state without creative thought. Its other name is death.” There is no ultimate explanation, and “no utopia is possible, but only because our values and our objectives can continue to improve indefinitely.”
  • Deutsch proposes carving two sentences in stone: PROBLEMS ARE INEVITABLE and PROBLEMS ARE SOLUBLE. “Soluble” means the right knowledge would solve them, not that we already have it.
  • This is where he places two Enlightenments, following the historian Roy Porter. The Continental Enlightenment, archetypally the French Revolution of 1789 and the Reign of Terror, “understood that problems are soluble but not that they are inevitable”, and so became utopian. The British Enlightenment understood both. Condorcet, though French, was “British” in this sense, and Popper was the twentieth century’s foremost proponent of it. When Deutsch says “the Enlightenment”, he means the British one.

A spark in the dark

  • Even the empty intergalactic cube holds over a billion tonnes of ionised hydrogen. A knowledge-bearing civilisation could trawl millions of tonnes a year, fuse it for “orders of magnitude more than the combined power consumption of everyone on Earth today”, and, with a mirror the width of the cube, map every star, planet and moon in the nearest galaxies to a resolution of about ten kilometres. “Knowledge-friendliness is the rule … Death is the rule for those who do not [have knowledge].”
  • That is why people are significant. Predicting what a star will do requires a theory of people, since people might stop it exploding: “astrophysics is incomplete without a theory of people”. Only knowledge-driven transformations, like cooling something to a millionth of a degree, happen that way. A brain can contain an accurate working model of a quasar jet.
  • The Arecibo champagne. Near the SETI radio telescope at Arecibo in Puerto Rico, suppose a bottle of champagne sits in a fridge, to be opened if aliens are detected. Predicting the cork requires knowing about extraterrestrials light years away. The telescope ignores tonnes of people nearby but is sensitive to radio engineers across the galaxy. So “humans, people and knowledge are not only objectively significant: they are by far the most significant phenomena in nature.”
  • A lunar colony would be the moon reorganising itself in response to knowledge, “a beginning of infinity”. The cold, dark universe is like an explosive awaiting a spark. “If we want to, we could be that spark.”

The chapter’s own terms

Person, anthropocentric, fundamental or significant phenomenon, the Principle of Mediocrity, parochialism, Spaceship Earth, constructor, universal constructor.

Chapter 4

Creation

Knowledge in genes and knowledge in minds are both created by variation and selection. Every rival account denies that creation happens.

Two kinds of knowledge, one logic

  • The knowledge in human brains and the knowledge in biological adaptations both come from evolution in the broad sense: variation and selection. Human knowledge grows by conjecture and criticism, biological knowledge by mutation and natural selection.
  • Good adaptations, like good explanations, are hard to vary while still doing their job. Knowledge, in the book’s definition, is information which, once physically embodied in a suitable environment, tends to cause itself to remain so.
  • The differences matter too. Biological knowledge is non-explanatory and has limited reach. Mutations are random with respect to what would be useful, while human conjectures are purposeful.

Creationism

  • “The gods did it” is a bad explanation, because it could explain anything. Creationism faces a dilemma. Either the designer was “just there”, which explains nothing, or the designer is explained, in which case it is merely unseen, not supernatural, like an extraterrestrial engineer.
  • Life shows bad design. The vertebrate retina has its wiring in front of the light-sensitive cells, which is why we have a blind spot. The eyes of squids have the same basic design without the flaw. Darwin, in The Origin of Species: “how utterly inexplicable is it that organs bearing the plain stamp of inutility … should so frequently occur.” Primates carry a gene for making vitamin C that no longer works.
  • The theological problem of evil has the same shape as the Haldane–Dawkins argument of Chapter 3. Both place an unexplained limit on what can be understood.
  • Deutsch predicts that in future “any child will be capable of designing … a better, more complex, more beautiful, and also far more moral biosphere than the Earth’s, within a video game”. Religions will then stop claiming the design of the biosphere, just as they no longer bother to claim thunder.

Spontaneous generation

  • For centuries people believed that mice arose from rags and maggots from meat. The theory retreated to micro-organisms and was refuted by Louis Pasteur in 1859, the year The Origin of Species appeared.
  • The experiment shouldn’t have been needed. The theory was a bad explanation: how does the knowledge of how to build a mouse get into rags? St Augustine of Hippo (354–430) proposed that “seeds” of each species were distributed everywhere, so Noah’s Ark need not have carried them all, which is a form of creationism. Crystals and rainbows form again and again without any transfer of information, but they contain no knowledge. Mice do.

The argument from design

  • The argument is old. The historian Xenophon recalled Socrates telling a sceptic called Aristodemus that the eyes are “shuttered with eyelids”, the brows fringed against sweat, the mouth set close to the eyes and nose, and the body’s outlets “directed hindwards, as far away from the senses as possible”: “can you doubt whether they are products of chance or design?”
  • William Paley’s Natural Theology (1802), written before Darwin was born, sharpened it. A stone found on a heath needs no explanation, but a watch does, because “its several parts are framed and put together for a purpose”. Change any part and it stops keeping time. The sun is useful for telling the time too, but it isn’t adapted to that. The knowledge is in us and our sundials.
  • Paley was right that the appearance of design needs explaining. His mistake was to think there cannot be design without a designer. Darwin’s discovery of design without a designer is, for Deutsch, “one of the greatest discoveries in the history of science”. And Paley’s own argument rules out an ultimate designer, since the designer would show far more appearance of design than a watch. As Deutsch puts it, none of us can choose what our ideas imply.

Lamarckism and neo-Darwinism

  • In Darwin’s day “evolution” meant any gradual improvement. Erasmus Darwin (1731–1802), Charles’s grandfather, already held a theory of it. Deutsch thinks “evolution by natural selection” is better called evolution by variation and selection.
  • Jean-Baptiste Lamarck held that improvements acquired in a lifetime are inherited: giraffes stretch their necks and pass on longer ones, organs develop with use and waste with disuse, and life has a built-in tendency towards complexity. The tendency is a fudge, a form of spontaneous generation.
  • The deeper flaw: a muscle that strengthens with use does so because genes already contain that knowledge. A tiger living where its stripes stand out does not grow different fur, because nothing in the tiger “knows” what stripes are for. Lamarck’s error has the same logic as inductivism: it expects knowledge to come from the environment.
  • Neo-Darwinism says the genes that spread are the ones best at spreading, not the ones that benefit the species. The nesting thought experiment: suppose the best time for a bird species to nest is the beginning of April. A mutant that nests at the end of March gets the best sites and spreads, even though its chicks fare worse. Nesting gets ever earlier and the population falls. “An optimally adapted species may in this way evolve into one that is less ‘well off’ by any measure.”
  • “Survival of the fittest”, Herbert Spencer’s phrase that Darwin took up, is misleading, and not all evolution is progress. The peacock’s tail is the classic case. Richard Dawkins’s The Selfish Gene got the logic right, with one caution: genes don’t spread for their own welfare either. Organisms are their tools, and the benefits organisms get are a matter of reach. Mules stay alive though they are sterile.
  • A replicator is “anything that contributes causally to its own copying”: genes, jokes, and Dawkins’s memes (rhyming with “dreams”), which come in groups called memeplexes, such as languages, scientific theories, religions and the skill of playing classical music. The real replicator is abstract: the knowledge itself, which could be copied into a computer or a seed bank.

What would refute Darwinism?

  • Haldane is said to have named rabbit fossils in the Cambrian as a refutation. Deutsch finds this misleading: it would refute a piece of history, perhaps showing a fast-evolving isolated continent, not the theory.
  • The theory would be refuted by an organism whose mutations were only favourable, by complex adaptations appearing with no precursors, or by an adaptation with no history of selection, such as an animal that decided when to hibernate by consulting internet weather forecasts.

Fine-tuning and anthropic reasoning

  • The constants of physics seem fine-tuned for life. Brandon Carter pointed out in 1974 that if the strength of the interaction between charged particles were a few per cent smaller, there would be no planets. If it were a few per cent greater, no star would ever explode, and the universe would hold only hydrogen and helium. The philosopher Antony Flew, a long-time atheist, was persuaded by such arguments that there must be a designer.
  • Deutsch: an unsolved problem in physics is “no more evidence for a supernatural explanation than the existence of an unsolved crime is evidence that a ghost committed it”. We don’t even know that life needs planets or chemistry. Robert Forward’s science-fiction story Dragon’s Egg has life evolving on the surface of a neutron star. Either way, fine-tuning is a legitimate scientific problem.
  • If only one set of laws exists, we need an explanation of why they are as they are, which brings back Paley’s problem. If many universes exist with different laws, the anthropic explanation says we inevitably find ourselves in one that allows astrophysicists. The “weak anthropic principle” is not really a principle, “it is just logic”.
  • Dennis Sciama’s argument. Suppose theorists calculate that a constant must lie between 137 and 138 for astrophysicists to evolve, with the best odds at 137.5. If we then measured it at exactly 137.5, that would statistically refute the anthropic explanation, just as a dart that lands in the bull’s-eye is unlikely to hit its exact centre.
  • With many constants the prediction becomes stranger. Call a value “near the edge” if it is within 10 per cent of either end of its range. Then for many constants the anthropic explanation predicts that at least one will be near the edge: we should find ourselves in a universe that only just allows astrophysicists (see the table).
  • Anthropic arguments also have Fermi’s problem, “Where are they?”, and the choice between varying the constants and varying the laws, which Deutsch calls a parochial distinction. If, as Max Tegmark suggests, all logically possible laws are instantiated, we should be exactly on the edge. And, by an argument in the style of Feynman, most observers would be a sphere of order around a brain amid chaos, killed a picosecond later, “an extreme version of the gambler’s hunches”. There are also universes containing nothing but a single bison, in various poses, lasting 42 seconds.
  • So anthropic reasoning is never the whole explanation. The fine-tuning will be explained, Deutsch guesses, by something Wheeler would recognise: “the problem has been not that the world is so complex that we cannot understand why it looks as it does, but it is that it is so simple that we cannot yet understand it.”

Creation denial

  • All the bad explanations of the biosphere are linked, and all are easy to vary: anthropic selection leans on spontaneous generation, which leans on creationism. All of them fail to explain how knowledge is created. “Ironically, the theory that underrates creation most of all is creationism.” A creator who made the universe at the moment Einstein finished relativity would deny the only real creation that happened: Einstein’s.
  • Scientific discovery is “unpredictable creation ex nihilo. So does biological evolution. No other process does.” Hence: “Creationism is really creation denial — and so are all those other false explanations.”

The chapter’s own terms

Evolution (Darwinian), “creation of knowledge through alternating variation and selection”; replicator; neo-Darwinism; meme; memeplex; spontaneous generation; Lamarckism; fine-tuning; anthropic explanation. The summary’s key line: Lamarckism is the counterpart of inductivism.

Chapter 5

The Reality of Abstractions

Explanations at any level of emergence can be fundamental, and abstract things such as numbers, morality and causation are real.

Emergence

  • Hold out your arm and you cannot feel the force of gravity, because according to general relativity there isn’t one. The only force acting is your arm’s push away from the straightest path through curved spacetime. The rule: something is real if it figures in our best explanation. If the best explanation denies it, stop assuming it.
  • No supercomputer running for the age of the universe could predict the motion of every molecule in a boiling kettle. Yet we can predict when it will boil from a few quantities, such as the mass of water and the power of the element. That “resolution into explicability at a higher, quasi-autonomous level is known as emergence”.
  • Emergent phenomena are a tiny minority. Where each bubble forms remains unpredictable.
  • Reductionism says explanation always runs from lower levels to higher ones. Deutsch quotes his own The Fabric of Reality: a copper atom at the tip of the nose of Winston Churchill’s statue in Parliament Square is there because Churchill served as prime minister nearby, because his ideas and leadership helped win the Second World War, because such people are honoured with statues, and because bronze contains copper. There is no reason there should exist any lower-level explanation.
  • The second law of thermodynamics, that a scrambled egg never unscrambles itself, is a high-level law. It isn’t known whether it can be derived from the laws of atoms.
  • Reductionism carries a moral overtone, as though higher levels were less real. Instrumentalism rejects all explanation, the Principle of Mediocrity rejects explanations that involve people, and holism is the mirror image of reductionism. All four judge theories on grounds other than whether they are good explanations. High-level laws such as the universality of computation may even constrain the low-level ones, which might one day solve the fine-tuning problem.
  • “Emergence is another beginning of infinity: all knowledge-creation depends on, and physically consists of, emergent phenomena.”

Theories that die in our place

  • Kepler denied the celestial spheres, Newton used infinitesimals and forces, and Einstein denies both the force of gravity and time flowing at one rate for everyone. An instrumentalist might say the story zigzags: no force, then force, then no force. It doesn’t. Newton was right that the same laws govern heaven and earth, that mass differs from weight, and that gravity depends on mass rather than density, and the curvature caused by the sun does vary roughly as the inverse square.
  • Einstein: “There could be no fairer destiny for any physical theory than that it should point the way to a more comprehensive theory in which it lives on as a limiting case.” Newton’s theory is fine for bridges, slightly inadequate for satellite navigation, and hopeless for pulsars, quasars and the universe as a whole.
  • Progress can be gradual because it happens in scientists’ minds before it happens in the world. Popper: “We can let our theories die in our place.” Intermediate explanations don’t need to work, which is how ideas escape parochialism in a way biological evolution cannot.

The domino computer

  • A chess program that beats you is an abstraction. It could be instantiated in silicon, neurons or radio waves, and it, not the silicon atoms, beat you.
  • The thought experiment is Douglas Hofstadter’s, from I Am a Strange Loop (2007), which Deutsch quotes at length. A network of spring-loaded dominoes is set up as a computer that tests whether a number is prime. The input is a stretch of exactly that many dominoes, and a designated output domino falls only if a divisor is found. Hofstadter sets the input to 641 and trips the on-switch.
  • An observer who doesn’t know what the network is for notices one domino that never falls and asks why. Hofstadter contrasts two answers (see the figure). “The point of this example is that 641’s primality is the best explanation, perhaps even the only explanation, for why certain dominoes did fall and certain others did not fall.”
  • Deutsch agrees, with a small correction: the physics is essential too, because it explains why primality is relevant to that arrangement of dominoes. The example refutes reductionism about abstractions, since the theory of prime numbers is not part of physics.
  • Then, “Hofstadter goes on to disown his own argument and to embrace reductionism.” Following the philosopher Daniel Dennett, he concludes that the “I” is an illusion, because “physical law alone would suffice to determine [its] behaviour”. Deutsch replies that physical laws can’t push anything either. They only explain and predict. By the same argument, atoms couldn’t push atoms. Causation itself is an abstraction: David Hume showed we never perceive it, and the fundamental laws are time-reversible and say nothing about cause.

Numbers, geometry and morality

  • Our knowledge of infinite sets is an example of reach. In a kettle, an abstraction approximates a physical process. In a computer testing primes, the physical process approximates an abstraction, one that never goes wrong and has unlimited memory.
  • Plato thought geometry was about perfect entities no one ever experiences, and so must come from a supernatural source. Deutsch says it comes from conjecture, like everything else. Almost all rejected theories, in every field, are rejected as bad explanations without being tested. Experience supplies problems in philosophy just as it does in science.
  • Morality. Hume’s point that “you can’t derive an ought from an is” has been taken to mean morality is arbitrary. But “you can’t derive a factual theory from an is either.” Moral and factual explanations are connected. A nineteenth-century American slave who wrote a bestseller would not logically refute the claim that some people are intended by Providence to be slaves, but would ruin the explanation behind it. The beliefs of 9/11 conspiracy theorists “bear the imprint of moral wrongness just as clearly as a fossil”.
  • Jacob Bronowski argued that science itself entails values: truth, openness, tolerance, integrity. Deutsch: morally right values are connected with true factual theories. Moral philosophy is about what to do next, or what sort of life to want, a question that would face even the last person on earth. Utilitarianism resembles empiricism: a liberating focus with little truth in it. “There is an objective difference between right and wrong.”
  • Beauty (Chapter 14), right and wrong, primality and infinite sets exist objectively but not physically. So do the laws of physics and causation. A person, too, is an abstraction, and the ability to understand explanations is what leads to universality in the next chapter.

The chapter’s own terms

Levels of emergence, natural numbers, reductionism, holism, moral philosophy (“Addresses the problem of what sort of life to want”). Summary: reductionism and holism are both mistakes; explanations at any level can be fundamental; abstract entities are real and can cause physical phenomena; causation is itself an abstraction.

Chapter 6

The Jump to Universality

In many systems, a small change made for a parochial purpose suddenly makes the system universal. It happens only in digital systems, which correct errors.

Writing and numbers

  • The earliest writing used pictograms, such as a sun or a tree. A rule that lets a word be spelled with pictograms of words that sound like its parts (“treason” as tree + sun) brings in words nobody added: that is reach. A variant that uses only the initial sounds of words makes the system universal, able to write any word, including words not yet coined. The earliest alphabets probably evolved this way.
  • An alphabet’s rules contain more knowledge than a list of words: how words sound, how to inflect them, even how a foreigner might learn to speak. Yet the jump seems to have happened rarely, perhaps only once, among the Phoenicians’ predecessors. Phoenician script had no vowels. The Greeks added them.
  • This is what Deutsch calls the jump to universality: a small change for a parochial purpose that makes a system universal. The innovators almost never meant to do it.
  • Tally marks, one per goat, are universal for counting but unwieldy: selling 20 of 40 goats means 20 deletions. Grouping marks gave Roman numerals, whose symbols were not quite the modern ones. Rules for combining them let Romans calculate without counting. VII plus VIII becomes VIIVIII, then VVIIIII, then XV: “an abstract truth has been discovered, and proved … without anyone having counted”. It was “the Roman-numeral system using us to do arithmetic”, which Deutsch rejects calling slavery: “By that argument, it is slavery to escape from slavery.”
  • But the Roman system never jumped. Its highest symbol stood for a thousand, so numbers from two thousand up began with strings of thousand-symbols: tally marks again. Adding a bigger symbol always leaves a biggest one.
  • Positional notation, with ten digits and a zero as placeholder, originated in India, perhaps as late as the ninth century. Arab scholars embraced it at about that time, yet it was not generally used in the Arab world until a thousand years later. A few European scholars took it from the Arabs in the tenth century, hence the misnomer “Arabic numerals”, but everyday use took centuries more. The Babylonians had a positional system in base 60 by about 1900 BCE, with 59 digits each written in a Roman-like way, but without a zero, trailing zeros or a decimal point.
  • Archimedes, in the third century BCE, wanted to refute the truism that the grains of sand on a beach could not be numbered. Starting from a Greek system whose largest symbol was the myriad (10,000), he built a notation using powers of a myriad myriad, but capped the exponent, so it stopped at 10800,000,000. He extended it again and capped it again, somewhere above 10 to the power 8×1016. Each cap was arbitrary; without it he would have had a universal system. A few years later Apollonius fell short in the same way. “It is as though everyone in the ancient world was avoiding universality on purpose.” Laplace, writing of the Indian system: “We shall appreciate the grandeur of this achievement when we remember that it escaped the genius of Archimedes and Apollonius, two of the greatest minds produced by antiquity.” Deutsch’s guess: appreciating universality requires valuing abstract knowledge, which is unnatural in a static society. The Enlightenment reversed this, finding arbitrary exceptions (aristocrat and commoner, master and slave, man and woman) problematic.

Printing and computers

  • Movable type was invented in China in the eleventh century and had little effect, because Chinese writing needs thousands of characters. Gutenberg’s alphabetic type in the fifteenth century set off an avalanche. Before a jump, you build special-purpose objects. After it, you program a universal one. The Jacquard loom of 1801 was programmed with punched cards.
  • The jump in computing “should have happened in the 1820s”. Charles Babbage designed a Difference Engine, with cogs that click into ten positions, to automate mathematical tables then compiled by clerks called “computers”. He was a poor organiser, spent his own fortune and the British government’s, and never built it. In 1991 a team led by Doron Swade at London’s Science Museum built one to the tolerances of the period, and it worked.
  • Brook Taylor had shown in 1710 that analytic functions can be approximated by repeated addition and multiplication, so the engine was universal for analytic functions. Babbage’s next design, the Analytical Engine, could punch its own cards and choose which card to read next. That was the jump to universality: a universal classical computer and universal simulator. Ada, Countess of Lovelace, saw that it could handle algebra, chess, music and images. It is “one of the tragic might-have-beens of history”; with the electrical relays of telegraphy, computing might have arrived a century early.
  • Lovelace also wrote: “The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform.” Turing called this “Lady Lovelace’s objection”. The philosopher John Searle says the brain-as-computer is just the latest metaphor, after gears, hydraulic pipes, steam engines and telephone exchanges. Deutsch: “A steam engine is not a universal simulator. But a computer is.”
  • Alan Turing’s 1936 theory was about the nature of mathematical proof. In the Second World War, Britain’s Colossus broke codes, America’s ENIAC calculated artillery tables, and Konrad Zuse’s relay computers in Germany were destroyed by Allied bombing. Colossus was dismantled. ENIAC went on to weather forecasting and the hydrogen bomb. The microprocessor arrived around 1970, from several companies independently. Human clerks, the Analytical Engine, the wartime machines and today’s supercomputers all have the same repertoire.

Error-correction is the beginning of infinity.Chapter 6

  • There is no universal analogue computer. Analogue systems, like a slide rule or a goat tally kept as a length of string, accumulate errors. A digital system that measures string in whole inches with a tolerance of a tenth of an inch removes the error at each step. Babbage’s cogs click into place. Speech is digital too: we build words from a finite set of sounds, and people can distinguish only about seven levels of loudness, which is why musical notation uses about seven dynamic markings.
  • Life. Roughly four billion years ago, in oceans stirred by volcanoes, meteors and the tides of a closer moon, some molecule catalysed the formation of its own constituents: “not alive, but … the first hint of life”. Replicators formed groups, a rudimentary organism, and eventually RNA and a genetic code with bases as its alphabet. “The process of copying a genome is called a living organism.”
  • The code then stopped evolving while organisms kept evolving. Its reach is astonishing: evolved for single-celled organisms, it now specifies running, flying, teeth, immune systems and brains that explain quasars. For over a billion years life was only bacteria. “Reach always has an explanation. But this time … the explanation is not yet known.”
  • Is DNA a universal constructor? Organisms build with calcium phosphate for bones and magnetite for pigeons’ navigation, and biotechnology can make hydrogen or extract uranium from seawater. Deutsch guesses the code has “some lesser, and not yet understood, universality”. In 1994 Leonard Adleman used DNA to compute, “arguably the fastest computer in the world” at the time. Computational universality had been latent in DNA for billions of years.
  • The most significant jump is people, who are universal explainers. “Only people can rely on themselves into the unbounded future.”

The chapter’s own terms

The jump to universality: “The tendency of gradually improving systems to undergo a sudden large increase in functionality, becoming universal in some domain.” Summary: in the past, innovators who brought about such jumps had rarely been seeking them; since the Enlightenment they have been. Jumps happen only in digital systems.

Chapter 7

Artificial Creativity

Artificial general intelligence is possible because computation is universal. The field has made no progress because no one knows how creativity works.

The Turing test and its failures

  • Alan Turing, the father of computing (Babbage being its grandfather), asked in his 1950 paper “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” whether a machine can think, and proposed a test: can a judge, conversing by teleprinter, tell it from a person?
  • In 1964 Joseph Weizenbaum wrote Eliza, a program that played a psychotherapist by matching keywords to templates. Told “I hate my job”, it would ask “What do you hate most about your job?” People were fooled, and Weizenbaum warned against such anthropomorphism in Computer Power and Human Reason (1976).
  • In 1983 Douglas Hofstadter was hoaxed by graduate students who pretended to be a program. Asked “What are ears?”, the “program” said “Ears are auditory organs found on animals.” The replies grew bolder until jokes aimed at Hofstadter himself gave the game away. Hofstadter, in Metamagical Themas (1985): “In retrospect, I am quite amazed at how much genuine intelligence I was willing to accept as somehow having been implanted in the program.” Nineteen years after Eliza, no program was better than Eliza, and the chance that a template program keeps sounding human falls exponentially with each utterance.
  • Hugh Loebner endowed a prize for passing the Turing test in 1990, with a lesser prize each year for the closest entry. In 2008 the lesser prize went to Elbot. When the judge wrote “I spose it is a little harsh”, Elbot read “spose” as “spouse” and replied with a stock joke: “If we robots ever had marriages they would be eternal: ‘Till rust do us part’.” When the judge picked up Elbot’s joke about oil, Elbot ignored it and swapped “career” for “occupation” in a stock sentence.
  • “This is how much success the quest for ‘machines that think’ had achieved in the fifty-eight years following Turing’s paper: nil.” Specialised “AI” has done well, but none of it looks like machines that think. And lack of computing power is no excuse. Nobody has a program that would pass the test but take a year per reply.

Qualia and the test of understanding

  • General intelligence belongs to a constellation of puzzles about the mind, with consciousness, free will and meaning. A typical one is qualia (singular quale, rhyming with “baa-lay”), the subjective aspect of sensations.
  • The thought experiment: you are a biochemist born without working blue receptors, and you find a cure. You can predict that you will call the new colour “blue”, and that a clear daytime sky will look similar. What you cannot predict, and nor can anyone else, is what blue will look like. Qualia are neither describable nor predictable, which Deutsch finds exciting evidence of a discovery still to be made.
  • Daniel Dennett draws the opposite conclusion: qualia don’t exist, and our memories of experiencing them are mistaken. Some philosophers have remarked that his Consciousness Explained would be better called Consciousness Denied. Deutsch agrees. Denial is a bad explanation, because anything could be denied that way. “One day, we shall. Problems are soluble.”
  • Self-awareness, as in recognising oneself in a mirror, is not part of general intelligence. A simple pattern-recognition program could do it. Nor are tool use, signalling or emotional responses. His rule of thumb: “if it can already be programmed, it has nothing to do with intelligence in Turing’s sense.” And his test for any claim to have explained consciousness: “if you can’t program it, you haven’t understood it.”

Why the Turing test is the wrong test

  • Turing hoped the functionality could be achieved before it was explained. It rarely works that way. His test, like empiricism, seeks a purely behavioural criterion. But the judge’s real task is Paley’s: to explain who created the knowledge in the utterances. If the programmer did, it is not an AI. If the program did, it is.
  • The same question arises with people: conjurors, politicians or examination candidates fed answers through hidden earpieces, or a patient consenting to surgery who may not understand the words. We test by asking the question another way.
  • If a programmer explained how a program creates knowledge, including jokes, we should know it was an AI even before seeing any output, so the test would be unnecessary. The very same joke can be evidence of thinking or of not thinking, depending on the best explanation of how the program works.
  • “My guess is that every AI is a person: a general-purpose explainer.” AI will come by a jump to universality, not by degrees. Imitating humans is not a form of universality. “Becoming better at pretending to think is not the same as coming closer to being able to think.”
  • The doctrine that the two are the same is behaviourism, “instrumentalism applied to psychology”. Adding tricks to a chatbot is the analogue of Lamarckism: all the knowledge was created earlier and elsewhere, by the programmer.

Artificial evolution

  • Consider a robotics student building a walking robot. The creative work goes into the design and into a language of subroutines (veer, step, stop) adapted to walking. Then an evolutionary algorithm varies a simple program in that language and keeps the best walker, thousands of times over. The robot walks, and the thesis claims evolution was implemented on a computer.
  • Deutsch doubts any knowledge was created by the “evolution”. The obvious explanation is the programmer’s creativity: the language encodes truths about geometry and mechanics, and was designed with the goal in mind. Such projects typically stop improving once they reach their target.
  • His proposed test: “eliminate the graduate student”. Take a robot already in use that happens to walk, replace its program with random numbers, use only the kinds of error processors really make as mutations, and run the simulation. “If the robot ever walks better than it did originally, then I am mistaken. If it continues to improve after that, then I am very much mistaken.”
  • For that to work, the language would have to evolve along with the adaptations, as happened before the genetic code settled. We don’t know why the DNA code, evolved for bacteria, has enough reach to describe dinosaurs and humans, and we can’t explain qualia. “So long as we cannot explain them, how can we expect to simulate them in a computer program?” When we do understand them, implementing them “will then be no great effort”.

The chapter’s own terms

Quale, “the subjective aspect of a sensation”; behaviourism, “instrumentalism applied to psychology”. Summary: artificial general intelligence has made no progress because at its heart is an unsolved philosophical problem, how creativity works. Once that is solved, programming it will not be difficult.

Chapter 8

A Window on Infinity

Infinity makes sense in mathematics and physics, and its strangest property is the book’s title: every member of an infinite sequence is close to its beginning.

The ancient aversion to infinity

  • Mathematicians have worked consistently with infinity for centuries. Many of its properties are counter-intuitive, but so are many facts about finite things. What Dawkins calls “the argument from personal incredulity” is no argument. Newton and Leibniz’s calculus analysed continuous change as infinitely many infinitesimal changes.
  • The unlimited growth of knowledge depends on other infinities: laws of nature that let finite symbols describe all of space and time, and people, who are universal explainers and so universal constructors. Deutsch treats a “potential” infinity (progress will be unbounded) and a “realised” one (infinite progress will be made) as interchangeable here.
  • Finitism says only finite abstract entities exist. Is there a largest natural number? If yes, the rule for making a larger one is contradicted. If no, there are infinitely many. So finitists must deny the law of the excluded middle. Finitism is “instrumentalism applied to mathematics”, and it assumes a privileged access to finite things that doesn’t exist: “All observation is theory-laden. All abstract theorizing is theory-laden too.”
  • “The reach of explanations cannot be limited by fiat.” To set bounds on where reason is the proper arbiter is to believe in unreason or the supernatural.
  • Georg Cantor, in the nineteenth century, founded the modern study of infinity on the principle that abstract entities may be defined in any way that is unambiguous and consistent. John Conway called that principle “the mathematicians’ liberation movement”. Cantor met vitriolic opposition from mathematicians, scientists, philosophers and theologians, who saw work on infinity as trespassing on God’s prerogatives. In the mid-twentieth century Ludwig Wittgenstein still called it “meaningless”, and later said the same of all philosophy, including his own.

Infinity Hotel

  • An infinite set is one that can be put in one-to-one correspondence with part of itself, as the numbers 1, 2, 3 … can be matched with 2, 3, 4 ….
  • The mathematician David Hilbert devised a thought experiment to show which intuitions infinity forces us to drop: a hotel with infinitely many rooms, numbered 1, 2, 3 … with no last room. Every room number is infinitely far below infinity.
  • When the hotel is full and a new guest arrives, the management asks every guest to move to the room numbered one higher. Room 1 comes free. “At Infinity Hotel, it is never necessary to make a reservation.”
  • Rooms cost a dollar a night, yet each gets several hundred dollars’ worth of luxury: the rent from rooms 1–1000 pays for room 1, the rent from 1001–2000 pays for room 2, and the management makes a profit.
  • When an infinitely long train arrives, everyone moves from room n to room 2n, leaving all the odd rooms free (see the figure). Even infinitely many infinitely long trains can be accommodated.

Uncountable infinities

  • In the 1870s Cantor proved that not all infinities are equal. The continuum, the number of points in a line (the same as in all of space), is a larger infinity than the natural numbers.
  • His diagonal argument, in Deutsch’s version: imagine a pack of cards one centimetre thick, with one card for every real number between 0 and 1. Suppose the cards were dealt out, one per room. Build a new number whose first digit differs from the first digit of room 1’s card, whose second digit differs from the second digit of room 2’s, and so on. That card can’t be in any room. Contradiction.
  • Infinities that can be matched with the natural numbers are “countable”, though no one can count to infinity. Larger ones are uncountable, and there are uncountably many orders of infinity.
  • The set of all possible reassignments of guests to rooms is uncountable too. Public-address announcements are finite strings of characters, which are countable. So almost every logically possible reshuffling of the guests can never be ordered.

The puppy, the regress and the beginning of infinity

  • Waste disposal. Every guest passes their rubbish to the next room within a minute, any bag received within the next half-minute, then a quarter-minute, and so on. After two minutes all the rubbish is gone, although each guest did only finitely many things. It is nowhere. In physics that “nowhere” is called a singularity.
  • The staff are guests too, and can’t delegate all their work to staff in higher-numbered rooms, or the hotel would grind to a halt. That fallacy is an infinite regress. The heckler who says the earth sits on a turtle which sits on another turtle (“it’s turtles from there on down”) offers a bad explanation, not because it leaves something unexplained, but because what it leaves unexplained is the same thing it set out to explain. A designer of the biosphere designed by another designer is the same regress.
  • One day a puppy climbs into a rubbish bag, and within two minutes it too is nowhere. Reversing the moves fails: nothing comes back, because the rooms above return nothing either. If a puppy did arrive at room 1, nothing would explain why a puppy and not a kitten. That is a “naked singularity”. As Hawking put it, “Television sets could come out [of a naked singularity].” The Big Bang may have been a benign singularity, one governed by a law.
  • The low-numbered rooms are the most desirable, yet every guest is in a room unusually close to the beginning. “The clichéd politician’s promise to favour everyone can be honoured in Infinity Hotel.” “Every room is at the beginning of infinity.” So too with the growth of knowledge: “we are only just scratching the surface, and shall never be doing anything else.”

No typical room: bad news for anthropic arguments

  • Ideas like typical, average, rare and common don’t apply to infinite sets. Arranged as 1, 2, 4, 3, 6, 8, 5, 10, 12 …, the odd numbers look like a third of all numbers.
  • The management gives every millionth room Deutsch’s earlier book, The Fabric of Reality, and every other room The Beginning of Infinity. Your chance of the older book looks like one in a million. Then everyone moves so that one book’s recipients are in odd rooms and the other’s in even ones. Now it looks like one in two. There is no such thing as the probability here.
  • For an infinite set of universes with different values of a constant, relabelling them can make astrophysicists the rule or the exception. “Scientific explanations cannot possibly depend on how we choose to label the entities referred to in the theory.” So anthropic reasoning alone can’t predict anything.
  • Lee Smolin’s “evolutionary cosmology”, in which black holes spawn universes with slightly altered laws, doesn’t escape. Infinitely many universes bring back the counting problem. An infinite past, as Frank Tipler pointed out, means the ensemble reached equilibrium “an infinite time ago”, so the evolution never happened. A finite start leaves the fine-tuning of the first universe unexplained.
  • Lyra’s device. A girl born in universe 1 holds a button that moves her from universe to universe in a fixed order, one minute each, or on a second setting one minute, then half a minute, a quarter, and so on, visiting all of them in two minutes. Her instruments show running averages. If physics allowed visits in only one order, averages and proportions would have a meaning, a measure. None of the anthropic theories provides one. Quantum theory does (Chapter 11).
  • Zeno of Elea argued that Achilles can never overtake a tortoise, since he must first reach where it was, by which time it has moved on, and so on for ever. Zeno confused the mathematical notion called “infinity” with a physical one. “Only the laws of physics determine what is finite in nature.” A needle moving one centimetre passes through uncountably many points, but “Physics deals in distances, not numbers of points.”

Proof is a physical process

  • The same mistake: Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) held that Euclidean geometry is known a priori to be true of real space, so the angles of a real triangle must add to 180 degrees. Carl Friedrich Gauss soon measured a large triangle and found no deviation. Einstein’s theory, later confirmed, says space is curved. Near the earth the angles of a large triangle can add to 180.0000002 degrees, which satellite navigation systems must allow for.
  • In 1900 David Hilbert challenged mathematicians to formalise proof, with finite rules, axioms and steps. Turing’s theory showed proofs and computations are the same thing, and that almost all mathematical functions are non-computable, since functions are uncountable and programs countable. Kurt Gödel had found by another route that almost all mathematical truths are unprovable, and almost all statements undecidable.
  • “The laws of physics provide us with only a narrow window through which we can look out on the world of abstractions.” The truth of an undecidable statement can’t be just a way of describing some physical object, so it is a strong argument that abstractions exist objectively.
  • Under other laws of physics, other things would be provable. Infinity Hotel’s management could settle the twin-prime conjecture, that there are infinitely many primes differing by 2, such as 5 and 7, in five minutes, with three announcements. Hilbert ridiculed infinite proofs, making Zeno’s mistake. Which operations count as simple (not, and, or) depends on physics. Quantum computation has the same computable functions as Turing’s but “drives a coach and horses through the classical notion of a ‘simple’ … operation”.
  • “Whether a mathematical proposition is true or not is indeed independent of physics. But the proof of such a proposition is a matter of physics only.” So proof theory can’t be a branch of mathematics: “Proof theory is a science: specifically, it is computer science.” The search for a perfectly secure foundation for mathematics was a form of justificationism. The object of mathematics is to understand abstract entities, and proof is one of its tools.
  • The laws of physics seem fine-tuned for computation. They share one set of elementary operations and one distinction between finite and infinite, and a universal computer can compute their predictions. Eugene Wigner called this “the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences”. The claim that reality is a program running on a Great Simulator is “a chimera. An infinite regress”, since we could never know the physics of the simulator’s hardware.
  • Most mathematical questions are uninteresting. P ≠ NP is unproved, yet almost all researchers are sure it is true, because there are good explanations for expecting it. We may one day count the grains of sand on earth, but never the number in Archimedes’ time. Deutsch conjectures: “if the question is interesting, then the problem is soluble.” The biologist Peter Medawar called science “the art of the soluble”.

The chapter’s own terms

One-to-one correspondence; infinite (mathematical and physical); countably infinite; measure; singularity, “a situation in which something physical becomes unboundedly large, while remaining everywhere finite”; multiverse; infinite regress; computation, “a physical process that instantiates the properties of some abstract entity”; proof. Physics uses at most the first one or two levels of infinity.

Chapter 9

Optimism

The future of civilisation is not a game of chance. All evils are caused by insufficient knowledge, so the rational stance towards the unknowable is optimism.

“The possibilities that lie in the future are infinite. When I say ‘It is our duty to remain optimists,’ this includes not only the openness of the future but also that which all of us contribute to it by everything we do … Thus it is our duty, not to prophesy evil but, rather, to fight for a better world.”Karl Popper, The Myth of the Framework (1994), the chapter’s motto

Russian roulette and prophecy

  • The astronomer Martin Rees suspects civilisation was lucky to survive the twentieth century. In Our Final Century (2003) he gave it only a 50 per cent chance of surviving the twenty-first: biological weapons in the hands of terrorists or individuals, genetically modified organisms escaping a laboratory, intelligent robots and nanotechnology, perhaps even particle accelerators destabilising the vacuum. We need be unlucky only once, he argued, as in Russian roulette.
  • Deutsch: roulette is pure chance, unaffected by what the player thinks or does. The future of civilisation depends entirely on what we think and do. Its outcomes are unknowable because the knowledge that will shape them hasn’t been created, so we don’t even know the possibilities, let alone their probabilities. “People in 1900 did not consider the internet or nuclear power unlikely: they did not conceive of them at all.”
  • Following Popper, he distinguishes prediction, a conclusion that follows from good explanations, from prophecy, which claims to know what is not yet knowable. Prophecy is biased towards pessimism.
  • Albert Michelson, opening the Ryerson Physical Laboratory at the University of Chicago in 1894: “The more important fundamental laws and facts of physical science have all been discovered … Our future discoveries must be looked for in the sixth place of decimals.” A century earlier Joseph-Louis Lagrange said Newton was the luckiest genius who ever lived, since “the system of the world can be discovered only once”. Lagrange’s own work helped replace Newton’s system, and Michelson’s 1887 experiment with Edward Morley, showing that the speed of light doesn’t change with the observer’s motion, became the centrepiece of special relativity without their realising it.

Leibniz, blind optimism and blind pessimism

  • “Optimism” first meant the doctrine of Gottfried Leibniz (1646–1716) that God created “the best of all possible worlds”: every evil is outweighed by remote good consequences, and every failed improvement would have been worse. It is “a spectacularly bad explanation”. One could equally claim this is the worst of all possible worlds, as Arthur Schopenhauer’s philosophical “pessimism” did. Both make true explanations unimaginable, so both are pessimism in Deutsch’s sense.
  • Optimism is not a mood. Winston Churchill suffered from severe depression yet held unusually positive expectations for civilisation. Thomas Malthus, the great prophet of doom, is said to have kept his dinner companions in gales of laughter.
  • Blind optimism, or recklessness, proceeds as if bad outcomes won’t happen. The builders of the Titanic, the largest ship of its day, called it “practically unsinkable”. It sank on its maiden voyage in 1912.
  • Blind pessimism, the precautionary principle, avoids everything not known to be safe. But “blind pessimism is a blindly optimistic doctrine”: it assumes disaster can’t come from existing knowledge, or existing ignorance. There would be no ship designs to stick with if no one had ever broken the principle. “The harm that can flow from any innovation that does not destroy the growth of knowledge is always finite; the good can be unlimited.”
  • Pessimists usually claim an exceptionally dangerous moment has arrived. Rees says the period since the mid-twentieth century is the first in which technology could destroy civilisation. But fire and the sword destroyed many. Of all civilisations, the overwhelming majority have been destroyed, and almost all could have been saved by a little more knowledge; “Very few, if any, could have been saved by greater caution about innovation.” Most enthusiastically applied the precautionary principle. What they lacked was wealth, defined as “the repertoire of physical transformations that they would be capable of causing”.
  • Stephen Hawking, in his television series Into the Universe, advised hiding from extraterrestrials, comparing contact to Columbus’s arrival in America. The novelist Greg Bear imagined a galaxy of hidden predators and prey, which would solve Fermi’s problem. Deutsch: hiding carries its own dangers, and any civilisation able to cross the galaxy would have cheap transmutation and no need of our gold, oil or water. “The idea that there could be beings that are to us as we are to animals is a belief in the supernatural.”
  • The precautionary principle rules itself out. Our civilisation isn’t following it, and switching to it would be an unprecedented change.

Narrow escapes, and Malthus

  • By Rees’s standard, civilisation had already had narrow escapes. Nazi Germany came close to nuclear weapons. The Japanese Empire weaponised bubonic plague, tested it in China and planned to use it on the United States. Churchill warned of “a new dark age, made more sinister and perhaps more protracted by the lights of perverted science”, and worked to prevent it. The Austrian writer Stefan Zweig and his wife killed themselves in 1942, in the safety of neutral Brazil, believing civilisation already doomed.
  • Malthus, in 1798, argued that the nineteenth century would see a permanent end to progress: “Population, when unchecked, increases in a geometrical ratio. Subsistence increases only in an arithmetical ratio. A slight acquaintance with numbers will shew the immensity of the first power in comparison of the second.” The population explosion happened as he said. The catastrophe didn’t, because food production grew faster still, and in the twentieth century both grew faster.
  • Why did he get one half right? Population growth depended less on the creation of knowledge, so it was more predictable, though not more probable. Malthus, Michelson and Lagrange all made the same parochial error: “we do not yet know what we have not yet discovered.”
  • Problems are inevitable. The Neanderthals and our other sister species died out from challenges they could have met had they known how. Genetic studies suggest our own species fell to a few thousand people about 70,000 years ago. People died of exposure sitting on the means of making fire, and hundreds of millions died of cholera within sight of hearths that could have boiled their water. The line between a “natural” disaster and one caused by ignorance is parochial.
  • A one-kilometre asteroid on a collision course at any time before the early twenty-first century would have killed a large share of humanity. Such impacts come about once every 250,000 years, so a typical person is far likelier to die from one than in a plane crash, and “the next such object to strike us is already out there at this moment”. There are also ice ages, “mini ice ages” with perhaps a few years’ warning, the super-volcano under Yellowstone, and pandemics like the fourteenth-century Black Death. Even colonising space, which Rees and Hawking rightly advise, wouldn’t survive a nearby gamma-ray burst without far more knowledge and wealth.

Who should rule? How do we remove bad rulers?

  • Policies, like scientific theories, are conjectures, to be judged by how good they are as explanations. Popper, in “Knowledge without Authority” (1960), proposed replacing the question of the best sources of knowledge with another: “How can we hope to detect and eliminate error?” Richard Feynman: “science is what we have learned about how to keep from fooling ourselves”.
  • Political philosophy traditionally asked “who should rule?”: a monarch, aristocrats, priests, a dictator, “the people”? Popper saw that this has the same form as empiricism’s question, how are theories derived from data? Induction, instrumentalism and Lamarckism all expect explanationless progress.
  • The question begs violent answers. If the right system is not yet in place, no ruler is legitimate. Once it is, opposing it is opposing rightness. Hereditary monarchy was the precautionary principle in action, and pretenders to thrones invoked it to justify sudden violent change. Revolutionary utopians are blind optimists about their plans, and pessimists about improving on them or persuading anyone.
  • Popper’s alternative: how can we rid ourselves of bad governments without violence? Systems should be judged “not for their prophetic ability to choose and install good leaders and policies, but for their ability to remove bad ones that are already there”.
  • Why would anyone want to remove bad leaders? Only if they expect the new ones to be better. Without that expectation, “Better the devil you know than the devil you don’t.” There is a closed loop: if knowledge won’t grow, the precautionary principle is true, and if it is true, knowledge can’t be allowed to grow. Only societies that expect their knowledge to grow, unpredictably, can meet Popper’s criterion.

The Principle of Optimism: All evils are caused by insufficient knowledge.Chapter 9

  • Optimism is first “a way of explaining failure, not prophesying success”. When we try to improve things and fail, it is never because of spiteful gods or a limit on reason, but because we did not know enough in time. It follows from the explicability of the physical world argued in Chapter 3.
  • So there is no disease that cannot be cured, except brain damage that has destroyed the knowledge that made up a person. Ageing and death are problems of the same kind as disease: complex, but finite, in a field where knowledge is growing exponentially. Arguments that immortality would cause overpopulation repeat Malthus’s fallacy. We can calculate what each surviving person would consume, but not what they would contribute.
  • The talking horse. A prisoner sentenced to death by a tyrant is reprieved by promising to teach the king’s favourite horse to talk within a year. Asked why, he says: “A lot can happen in a year. The horse might die. The king might die. I might die. Or the horse might talk!” He may persuade the king, learn a conjuring trick, escape, or think of something better. He cannot know the new idea today, so he mustn’t plan as if it will never exist.
  • Hard is not the same as unlikely. John F. Kennedy in 1962: “We choose to go to the moon … not because they are easy, but because they are hard.” He listed a vehicle “made of new metal alloys, some of which have not yet been invented”, for “an untried mission, to an unknown celestial body”. Rational people expected success without any probability estimate, because the problems were soluble.

Mini-enlightenments

  • “As far as I know, no historian has investigated the history of optimism.” Deutsch’s guess is that wherever optimism emerged there was a mini-enlightenment, and that every one except ours was snuffed out.
  • Athens. The Golden Age of the fifth century BCE had Socrates, Plato and Aristotle; Aeschylus, Aristophanes, Euripides and Sophocles; Herodotus, Thucydides and Xenophon. It continued a tradition of criticism going back to Thales of Miletus and Xenophanes of Colophon (570–480 BCE). Pericles, in his Funeral Oration of about 431 BCE, gave reasons for Athens’ success: democracy, because “we think it an indispensable preliminary to any wise action at all”, freedom, openness to foreigners even at the risk of spies, and a lax education in which “we live exactly as we please, and yet are just as ready to encounter every legitimate danger”.
  • Sparta was the opposite: a militarised society with every male citizen a full-time soldier, the Messenians reduced to helots, and no philosophers, historians, artists or writers. In 404 BCE, twenty-seven years after the oration, Sparta defeated Athens. Athens later regained its democracy but “became unexceptional”. Deutsch’s guess: “its optimism was gone.”
  • Florence. The early Renaissance revived Greek and Roman learning and became an enlightenment when Florentines began to believe they could improve on it. The Medici, especially Lorenzo “the Magnificent”, in charge from 1469 to 1492, fostered Leonardo, Michelangelo and Botticelli, collected ancient books and promoted humanism. Niccolò Machiavelli was “the first secular political philosopher since antiquity”.
  • In 1494 the monk Girolamo Savonarola seized power with prophecies of doom. Secular music was banned, clothing had to be plain, homosexuality and prostitution were violently suppressed, the Jews were expelled, and mirrors, cosmetics, instruments and books were burned in the Bonfire of the Vanities. Botticelli is said to have thrown in his own paintings. “It was the bonfire of optimism.” The Medici returned; optimism did not. It smouldered in Italy and eventually lit the Enlightenment in northern Europe.
  • Roger Bacon (1214–94) foresaw microscopes, telescopes, self-powered vehicles and flying machines, but belonged to no tradition, and his optimism died with him. The Islamic Golden Age, roughly from the eighth to the thirteenth century, produced scholars such as Alhazen (965–1039); whether it had a tradition of criticism is disputed.
  • “If any of those earlier experiments in optimism had succeeded, our species would be exploring the stars by now, and you and I would be immortal.”

The chapter’s own terms

Blind optimism (recklessness, overconfidence); blind pessimism (the precautionary principle); the principle of optimism; wealth, “the repertoire of physical transformations that one is capable of causing”. From the summary: “Some problems are hard, but it is a mistake to confuse hard problems with problems unlikely to be solved.”

Chapter 10

A Dream of Socrates

A dialogue in which Socrates learns Popper’s epistemology from the god Hermes, and the young Plato gets most of it wrong.

The setting

  • Socrates and his friend Chaerephon have travelled to the Oracle at Delphi to ask who is the wisest man in the world, so that they can learn from him. The priestess replies, “No one is wiser than Socrates.” That night, in an expensive room at an inn, Socrates dreams that a god appears. He assumes it is Apollo. It is Hermes.
  • Hermes will reveal no facts and no moral truths, only “knowledge about knowledge”. He praises two Athenian customs as “two sides of the same valuable coin”: defying anyone who seeks their submission, and giving a hearing to honest criticism.

What Hermes teaches

  • Certainty is not wanted. Socrates: “obviously I can’t be sure of anything. But I don’t want to be.” Hermes: the objective knowledge Socrates seeks is hard to come by but attainable. Justified belief, sought by priests and philosophers, is “a chimera”: unattainable except by self-deception, unnecessary and undesired by the wisest. Beliefs can be justified only by other beliefs, which leads to an infinite regress.
  • Nothing is easy to see. Asked what is before his eyes, Socrates ends with the inside of his eyelids, which he can’t see either. People are fooled by the sun’s dazzle, by mirrors, mirages and a pile of crumpled clothes that looks like a monster. Nothing is easy to see without prior knowledge.
  • Moral knowledge can also be trapped. Athenians disagree about virtue because moral questions are hard, yet they agree on the easy ones. Spartans disagree even on those, because their education holds their ideas immune from criticism. Xenophanes: “The gods did not reveal, from the beginning, / All things to us; but in the course of time, / Through seeking we may learn and know things better.” And: “People who converge upon the truth converge with each other.”
  • A Spartan Socrates would never worry that the Athenian one was right. If unchanging Sparta were right, it must have been perfect from the start and right about everything, yet it knows almost nothing. If Athens is not trapped, that implies nothing about whether it is right about anything else. The idea that improvement is possible implies there are errors to correct.
  • Fallible about everything. Socrates proposes exceptions: two plus two is four, Delphi exists, a triangle’s angles make two right angles. Hermes asks how he chose them. By criticising candidates, of course. So Socrates is a thoroughgoing fallibilist: “My doubt improved my knowledge of an important truth.”
  • Legalised theft. If Athenians were persuaded that thieving was a virtue and repealed the laws against it, disaster would follow, and at first they would demand more thieving. Eventually some would find a better explanation and persuade others. “Salvation would come about through persuasion,” and Hermes says this is how Athens really looks from his point of view. But if the error were to ban debate, elections and philosophy, that would be “a rare and deadly sort of error: it prevents itself from being undone.” That, says Hermes, is what Sparta looks like.
  • Socrates wonders whether “the moral imperative not to destroy the means of correcting mistakes is the only moral imperative”. Hermes is silent.
  • Knowledge comes from within. Does it matter whether Hermes is real? No: “I have no need to trust the source if the argument itself is persuasive.” One may disbelieve a friend and believe an enemy. Xenophanes again: “But as for certain truth, no man has known it … For all is but a woven web of guesses.” Socrates’ party got lost on the way from the ship because they misunderstood the captain’s directions. The wrong idea came from within them. So does the right one. “It all comes from within, from conjecture and criticism,” even revelation by a god.
  • The senses provide input to criticism, not to the source of ideas. We make a “waking dream” of reality, test it against the evidence and then control it. Socrates: “Here we sit, for ever imprisoned in the dark, almost-sealed cave of our skull, guessing … It is like magic, only real. We are like gods!” Hermes: “Somewhat … (Though you will always remain fallible.)” Is there an upper bound? “Generations from now, a book will be written which will provide a compelling —” and someone knocks on the door.

The next morning

  • The teenage Plato, called “the Broad” for his wrestler’s build, has spent the night wrestling Spartan boys by candlelight under the sacred truce and writing a poem about why Athens is better than Sparta. The companions guess the difference between the cities: war against philosophy, fighting against having something to fight for.
  • Socrates’ answer: “we Athenians are concerned above all with improvement, the Spartans seek only — Stasis.” That explains why Sparta has no philosophers, honours only dead poets, and keeps the statue of Ares chained. The mere existence of Athens threatens Sparta’s stasis. Athens needs traditions of virtue, tolerance and liberty to chain its own monster, democracy.
  • Plato scribbles notes, and gets it wrong in instructive ways: philosopher kings, “Let no one unversed in geometry enter here!”, prisoners chained in a cave who can never know reality, knowledge “remembered from a previous life”. The stage direction notes that Plato “has been increasingly misinterpreting Socrates”. Asked to write it all down, Socrates replies, “Posterity is right here, listening.” Plato: “If you say so, Master.”

The Socratic problem

  • Socrates left no writings, so historians must guess his views from Plato and a few others. Deutsch stresses that his dialogue is not history. Its epistemology is “largely that of the twentieth-century philosopher Karl Popper, together with some addenda of my own”, and he guesses Socrates would have liked it. “In some universes that were very like ours at the time, he thought of it himself.”
  • We habitually underestimate how hard communication is. Plato’s intelligence and devotion are no guarantee he understood Socrates. “Objective knowledge, though attainable, is hard to attain.”
  • Philosophy courses dwell on original texts. Physics courses almost never do: Deutsch learned relativity without reading Einstein, and knew Maxwell, Boltzmann, Schrödinger and Heisenberg only as names. Original sources are rarely the best, since later expositions improve on them. If a historian found that Einstein wrote his papers as a joke, or at gunpoint, and really believed in Kepler’s laws, physics textbooks would not change at all.
  • Good theories pass faithfully between generations although no one cares about fidelity, because learners want to know how the world is, the same aim as the originator, and a hard-to-vary theory leads them to the same place. “The way to converge with each other is to converge upon the truth.”

Chapter 11

The Multiverse

A science-fiction story about a faulty teleporter, made step by step to make sense, turns into the real physics of quantum theory.

Doppelgängers that make sense

  • Star Trek’s transporter malfunctions produce doppelgängers. Real doppelgängers occupying the same place would be a disaster: coinciding nuclei fuse, and two bodies overlapping would explode, since water at twice its normal density exerts hundreds of thousands of atmospheres of pressure. Other stories use parallel universes: the film Sliding Doors, Robert Harris’s Fatherland (Germany won the war), Robert Silverberg’s Roma Eterna (Rome never fell).
  • Deutsch is one of the pedantic fans who want fictional science to make sense. The Simpsons mocked such fans: asked why a winged Appaloosa became a winged Arabian between scenes, the star answers, “A wizard did it.” Since a wizard could explain anything, it is a bad explanation. Good plots rest on good explanations, a point Chapter 14 extends to all good art.
  • “Phantom zone” stories, where exiles see and hear the ship but can’t touch it, collapse under questioning. Eyes that absorb light would cast shadows. Where does the zone’s air come from, and why doesn’t it leak into space? What holds the exiles up? There would have to be a whole parallel universe, which a malfunction couldn’t create. “Good fictional science is hard to invent: it is a variant of real science.”
  • Quantum theory is “the deepest explanation known to science”. The account that follows, the many-universes interpretation, “remains at the time of writing a decidedly minority view among physicists”.

Fungibility

  • Start with two universes identical atom for atom, mutually imperceptible, with symmetrical, deterministic laws and the speed of light as a limit. A universe “is not a receptacle containing physical objects: it is those objects.” But two universes with one history would be a pointless story and an inexplicable world. Why two and not thirty?
  • For identical universes to become different under deterministic, symmetrical laws, they must be more than identical. They must be fungible (the g as in “plunger”): identical in every way except that there are two of them. The word comes from law, where dollar bills and barrels of oil are fungible and horses are not. Leibniz’s “identity of indiscernibles” ruled it out, but photons in lasers, and atoms in “atomic lasers”, can be fungible. It is “much weirder than multiple universes … which are, after all, just common sense, repeated.”
  • Money shows how it works. If a bank adds a loyalty dollar to your one-dollar balance and later withdraws a dollar in charges, there is no such thing as which dollar went. Energy is the same: brake away half of ten kilojoules and there is no fact about which five went. In quantum physics elementary particles are like this too, “excitations of the vacuum” in a quantum field.
  • So a law can say that in one of two fungible universes, the transporter causes a small voltage surge, without saying which. And “diversity within fungibility” is common: if you owe the tax authority one of the hundred dollars in your account, the dollars are fungible yet don’t all have one owner. The difficulty is one of language only.
  • A surge in one universe makes a passenger spill coffee on another, a romance begins, and the histories differ. There are three possible reasons a phenomenon may be unpredictable: genuine randomness, which real physics doesn’t have; deterministic factors too complex or unknown, including future knowledge; and, new with quantum theory, fungible instances of the observer becoming different.
  • Suppose the ship could send messages between universes, and a crew member receives “HAVE MARRIED SONAK.” News from another universe is as fallible as any, and perhaps the next message would have been “ALREADY REGRETTING IT”. Real quantum physics has no inter-universe communication. But histories can still affect each other, and that is the main evidence for the multiverse.

Measure and probability

  • Differences spread from the ship at up to the speed of light, a “sphere of differentiation”: messages home, light through portholes, heat through the hull. Most things inside it stay the same, because change is quantised. An atom absorbs a whole quantum of energy or none, which gave the theory its name.
  • Effects fall off with distance: the sun seen from a hundredth of a light year away is a cold bright dot, and a quasar jet seen from a neighbouring galaxy “little more than an abstract painting in the sky”. The only known exception is knowledge, which can travel far having scarcely any effect and then transform its target.
  • To keep the laws universal without allowing faster-than-light signals, the story needs more universes: uncountably many, initially fungible, with a law that says the surge happens “in half the universes in which the transporter is used”. That requires a measure, which the real quantum theory provides.
  • Now the plot can branch unevenly. A voice message during the honeymoon, in half the universes where the couple married, leads to divorce. Half the original universes have the couple single, a quarter married, a quarter divorced.
  • Scientists on board who understood the physics would know that it is “meaningless (not merely impossible to know)” to ask which outcome they will see. Two predictions follow: nothing can reliably predict whether the voltmeter will register a surge, and it will register one with probability one-half. Outcomes are subjectively random although everything is objectively determined. “This is also the origin of quantum-mechanical randomness.”
  • Histories multiply fast. With the transporter used ten times a day, within a month there are more distinct histories than atoms in the visible universe. Uncanny chains of “accidents” occur in a small but growing share of them, though all are determined. Richard Dawkins’s example of a television psychic in Unweaving the Rainbow shows the logic: with 10 million viewers, about 25 watches will stop in any given minute, enough for half a dozen amazed phone calls. What we observe is part of a larger phenomenon, most of it unobserved.

Interference: removing the fan

  • A multiverse in which histories only branch, never rejoining, would make exactly the same predictions as one universe with random events. It would be a bad explanation, like the galaxy that turned out to be a flaw in the emulsion. Lewis Carroll’s White Knight had a plan “To dye one’s whiskers green, / And always use so large a fan / That they could not be seen.” “Now it is time to start removing the fan.”
  • In quantum physics, histories can rejoin. This is interference, the time-reverse of splitting, and it conserves the total measure. Run the transporter twice in quick succession and the voltage always ends up normal, which could not happen if the intermediate value were randomly one or the other. Histories affect each other without any message being sent.
  • Interference happens only in objects that are not entangled with the rest of the world. Once differences have spread outwards, undoing them needs fine control of everything affected, which rapidly becomes infeasible: decoherence. That is why interference, ubiquitous at microscopic scales, is hard to demonstrate in the laboratory.
  • The standard demonstration uses single photons and half-silvered mirrors, which reflect a photon in half the universes and let it through in the other half, in a Mach–Zehnder interferometer (see the figure). A photon entering one way always leaves the same way. Detectors on the paths show only one photon per history. So “the fact that the intermediate histories X and Y both contribute to the deterministic final outcome X makes it inescapable that both are happening”.

What things are made of

  • Real particles split and rejoin all the time, into many trillions of histories of unequal measure. Because of interference, a particle doesn’t keep its identity from moment to moment, and in a collision between two atoms, some instances of each end up fungible with the other.
  • A fungible collection of instances must be diverse in some attributes: this is the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. So an electron has a range of positions and speeds and spreads like an ink blot, quickly when small and more slowly as it grows. That is how motion works in quantum physics.
  • Put a proton in the middle of the blot and the spreading stops where it balances the electrical attraction. That is a hydrogen atom. In classical physics atoms could not exist: the electron would spiral into the nucleus in a flash of radiation, and the solar-system picture fails, since orbiting bodies do slowly spiral in (observed in binary neutron stars) and solar systems could pass through each other. In its lowest state the electron isn’t orbiting at all.
  • The name “uncertainty principle” is doubly misleading. It has nothing to do with anyone’s uncertainty, any more than the tax dollar does, and it is not a separate principle: “no more drop it from quantum theory than one could omit eclipses from astronomy.” An electron is an irreducibly multiversal object, and the old saying that particles are “particles and waves at the same time” was a garbled glimpse of this.
  • Quantum theory doesn’t mention universes or histories at all. They are emergent, like geological strata: channels of information flow that are approximately autonomous, which is why a fossil or a classical prediction can be trusted. Large objects are partitioned into near-autonomous histories because entanglement suppresses interference, and at everyday scales those histories can fairly be called parallel universes.
  • Microscopic events amplified to everyday scale are rare in any one history but common in the multiverse. A single cosmic-ray particle spreads wider than the earth; in some histories it triggers lightning, in others a cancer. “Some non-negligible proportion of all cancers are caused in this way.” So there are histories resembling Fatherland and Roma Eterna, but none where the laws of physics differ.
  • Quantum computers, Deutsch’s own field, protect their qubits from entanglement, for instance by cooling them close to absolute zero, so that computations in vast numbers of histories can be combined by interference. A few hundred qubits could perform more computations in parallel than there are atoms in the visible universe, but only for problems where the mathematics of interference happens to fit. “At the time of writing, quantum computers with about ten qubits have been constructed.”
  • “Forget anything that you may have read about ‘quantum jumps’: they are a myth.” Halfway through absorbing a photon, an atom’s fungible instances are half in the ground state and half excited. Time is not yet understood quantum-mechanically, but Deutsch is fairly sure that different times will turn out to be a special case of different universes: time is an entanglement phenomenon, as the physicists Don Page and William Wootters first understood in 1983.
  • Imagine people in a dense cluster of stars, puzzled that their unlikely home allowed life, concluding that something selected them. They were selected by coincidence, which is to say by the story Deutsch is telling about them: “All fiction that does not violate the laws of physics is fact.” A kettle can, in a tiny sliver of the multiverse, turn into a top hat and its water into a rabbit. But that history begins at the transformation. Winning a lottery can’t be explained without the losers, and “In the multiverse, the losers are other instances of oneself.”
  • Knowledge-creating entities are unique twice over: their effects don’t fade with distance, and since there are many more ways to be wrong than right, they become more alike across histories, not less. Might something else create coherent structure across the multiverse, with purposes of its own? Deutsch asks us to shun parochial answers, like “love, or trust”, and remember that anything else that creates explanations would share our rank: “And there is always room at the top.”

Chapter 12

A Physicist’s History of Bad Philosophy

With some comments on bad science. Some philosophy is worse than false: it stops knowledge from growing, and it kept quantum theory unexplained for decades.

“By the way, what I have just outlined is what I call a ‘physicist’s history of physics’, which is never correct …”Richard Feynman, QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter (1985), the chapter’s motto

Why so few physicists agree

  • The chapter opens with a dialogue between READER and DAVID. You don’t need the full theory to see that other universes exist. In a Mach–Zehnder interferometer “the path that was not taken affects the one that was”, and a quantum computer’s output depends on results computed in vast numbers of histories. Those are only atoms? “Are you claiming to be made of something other than atoms?” If a cloud of photon instances were all absorbed by the one barrier we see, it would vaporise, so there must be other instances of the barrier, the floor holding it up and the experimenters.
  • So why does only a small minority of physicists agree? “Bad philosophy.”
  • Werner Heisenberg and Erwin Schrödinger found quantum theory independently between 1925 and 1927. In Heisenberg’s version a particle’s variables are matrices, not numbers; in Schrödinger’s, a single particle is a wave, but two particles are a single wave in a higher-dimensional space. With hindsight, both describe many instances in the multiverse. At the time no one thought the matrices described what Einstein called “elements of reality”.
  • Adding a rule of thumb made the two theories agree and predict superbly: whenever a measurement is made, all the histories but one cease to exist, the survivor chosen at random with probability equal to its measure.
  • “At that point, disaster struck.” Instead of explaining why the rule worked, most physicists retreated into instrumentalism, the “shut-up-and-calculate interpretation”. That meant ignoring that the rule contradicted both theories and worked only where quantum effects were negligible; that it made no sense for one observer measuring another; and that both theories plainly described a physical process. Many physicists trained their students not to wonder.

Bad philosophy: philosophy that is not merely false, but actively prevents the growth of other knowledge.The chapter’s definition

Copenhagen

  • Niels Bohr’s “Copenhagen interpretation” declared the theory, rule of thumb and all, a complete description of reality. He denied the “possibility of speaking of phenomena as existing objectively”, counted only the outcomes of observations as phenomena, and avoided the question of one observer measuring another, later called the paradox of Wigner’s friend after the physicist Eugene Wigner.
  • His “principle of complementarity” allowed classical language only for variables just measured. Asked which path a photon took, the answer was that there is no such thing. Asked how it then knew which way to turn, the answer was “particle-wave duality”. Ironically that is literally true of the multiversal photon, which is spread out while its instances are localised, but that was not what Copenhagen meant. It meant particles have mutually exclusive attributes, period.
  • Heisenberg later called the unaskable values “potentialities”, one of which becomes actual at measurement. The implication, made explicit in later presentations, was that human consciousness causes the transition.
  • For decades this was taught as fact, with no one understanding it, and students were told “If you think you’ve understood quantum mechanics then you don’t.” It placed itself outside the reach of criticism, the hallmark of bad philosophy, and opened the door to pseudo-science and mysticism dressed in quantum language.
  • Einstein never accepted it. David Bohm built a realist alternative which Deutsch regards as “the multiverse theory in heavy disguise”. In Dublin in 1952 Schrödinger warned an audience that what he was about to say might “seem lunatic”: that his equation’s several histories are “not alternatives but all really happen simultaneously”. It is the earliest known reference to the multiverse, and he never published it. Five years later Hugh Everett, independently, published a full theory. Decades passed before many physicists noticed it.

“Because I say so”

  • Error is the normal state of knowledge and no disgrace. Bad philosophy is different. The parent’s “Because I say so” contains five of its themes: it would explain anything; it concerns who said something, not what was said; it turns a request for an explanation into a request for justification; it confuses authority over ideas, which doesn’t exist, with power over people; and it claims to stand outside the jurisdiction of criticism.
  • Before the Enlightenment, bad philosophy was of the because-I-say-so kind. After it, good philosophy grew, but bad philosophy got worse.
  • Empiricism was harmless while no one took it literally. Positivism in the nineteenth century tried to remove from science everything not “derived from observation”, which left it to whim. Ernst Mach, father of the Ludwig Mach of the Mach–Zehnder interferometer, encouraged Einstein to drop untested assumptions, such as time flowing at one rate for everyone, but opposed relativity and denied that atoms exist. When Ludwig Boltzmann used atoms to unify thermodynamics and mechanics, Mach and other positivists vilified him, which may have contributed to his suicide shortly before the tide turned. Einstein became a realist, which is why he never accepted Copenhagen. Could a positivist have imagined spacetime “bucking and twisting under the influence of massive objects”?
  • Anti-realism spread through twentieth-century philosophy, Popper being an important exception. Thomas Kuhn, in “Reflections on my Critics”, refused to compare scientific theories “as representations of nature, as statements about ‘what is really out there’”.
  • Logical positivism held that unverifiable statements are meaningless, which made itself meaningless. Wittgenstein embraced the conclusion, declared all philosophy including his own meaningless, and was hailed as a genius. Linguistic philosophy treated philosophical problems as puzzles about everyday word use. Postmodernism treats all theories as “narratives” and truth as endorsement by a group, and is itself a narrative immune to criticism because it rejects criticism as narrative.

Explanationless science

  • The damaging legacy is the idea that a theory can be split into predictions and an “interpretation”. It makes no sense: without an explanation you can’t tell when a rule of thumb applies, and in fundamental physics the predicted outcome of an observation is itself an unobserved process.
  • Palaeontology avoids the split. Dinosaurs are the explanation of fossils, not an interpretation of them, and we know the chemistry of their genes though no fossil of a gene exists. Rival “interpretations” (dinosaurs as palaeontologists’ sensations, fossils coming into existence when extracted, dinosaurs as relations between experiences) are general-purpose means of denying anything.
  • Behaviourism still shapes psychology. Consider a study of whether happiness is genetic. How do we know people’s ratings are comparable? Someone who rates 8 may be unhappy and unable to imagine better; someone who rates 3 may have joined a chanting craze promising future bliss. A gene linked to higher ratings might code for willingness to quantify feelings, or for good looks that make people treat you better. Measuring whistling or smiling adds another layer, since some people act happy to fight unhappiness.
  • Proxies are legitimate only when an explanation links them to what we care about. Opinion polls are tested at the election, and a drug trial can test whether people say they feel better. Happiness has no independent measure.
  • Explanationless science calls its proxy “happiness” anyway, studies university students seeking extra income, defines “heritability” as correlation with genetic relatedness (by which standard slavery was once highly heritable in America), and concedes that correlations are “suggestive, though”. It finds happiness 50 per cent heritable, and the headline reads “New Study Shows Happiness 50% Genetically Determined”.
  • Deutsch’s rival conjecture: happiness is a state of continually solving one’s problems, and unhappiness is being chronically baulked, so, external factors aside, it comes from not knowing how. Interpreters will say the study refuted this, as one could have concluded in 1860 that slavery was 95 per cent genetically determined. A cultural change in what counts as good-looking, or a book persuading people that all evils come from lack of knowledge, could change the number. Such studies lean pessimistic because they are prophecy, and “Behaviouristic studies of human psychology must, by their nature, lead to dehumanizing theories of the human condition.”
  • On animal suffering, the zoologists Patrick Bateson and Elizabeth Bradshaw, commissioned by the National Trust in 1997, found that hunted stags suffer, the hunt being “grossly stressful … exhausting and agonizing”. The next year the Countryside Alliance commissioned the veterinary physiologist Roger Harris, who found the same measurements resembled those of a person enjoying football. Bateson rightly said nothing in Harris’s report contradicted his. Neither bore on the question, because there is no theory of qualia.
  • The City Museum. Count visitors at every door, subtract those leaving from those entering, and call any difference the “spontaneous-human-creation count”. The less competent the counting, the more “inconsistencies with conventional physics” appear. Headlines follow: “Teleportation Possibly Observed in City Museum, Say Scientists”. Just don’t call the paper “Errors Made When Counting People Incompetently”. Without explanations the biggest errors produce the most exciting results, which is how eminent physicists have been fooled by conjurors.
  • Bad philosophy can’t be refuted by argument, since it holds itself immune, but it can be defeated by progress. A living Mach, shown atoms under a microscope, could still say he saw only a video monitor, but he would tire of the game. “Bad philosophy is philosophy that denies the possibility, desirability or existence of progress. And progress is the only effective way of opposing bad philosophy. If progress cannot continue indefinitely, bad philosophy will inevitably come again into the ascendancy — for it will be true.”

The chapter’s own terms

Bad philosophy; interpretation (“the explanatory part of a scientific theory, supposedly distinct from its predictive or instrumental part”); Copenhagen interpretation (“Niels Bohr’s combination of instrumentalism, anthropocentrism and studied ambiguity”); positivism; logical positivism.

Chapter 13

Choices

There is no coherent “will of the people”, and no fair way to share out seats. Judge institutions instead by how well they let a society correct its mistakes.

Washington’s veto

  • In March 1792 George Washington used the first presidential veto in US history. The issue was the apportionment problem: the Constitution requires seats in the House of Representatives to be “apportioned among the several States … according to their respective Numbers”, but a state’s exact share, its quota, is almost never a whole number. With today’s 435 seats, a state with 1 per cent of the population has a quota of 4.35. An apportionment rule that always gives either the number below or the number above “stays within the quota”.
  • Deutsch’s own first idea was to round each quota to the nearest whole number. It violates quota. Take ten seats and four states: one with just under 85 per cent of the people, three with just over 5 per cent each. Rounding gives 8 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 11 seats, and 85 per cent of eleven is 9.35, so the large state is short by more than a seat (see the table). Adding seats to fix it takes three extra seats. Taking a seat from the smallest state stays within quota but disenfranchises 5 per cent of the population.
  • The Founding Fathers held conflicting ideals. “No taxation without representation” demanded that everyone be represented; abolishing privilege demanded exact proportion. The Constitution adjudicated: “Each State shall have at least one Representative.”
  • The “Numbers” counted the whole population, including women, children, immigrants and slaves, though voters were free men over twenty-one. So states with many non-voters gave their voters extra power. For slave states this was so offensive that a slave was counted as three-fifths of a person, and “three-fifths of an injustice was still considered an injustice by many”. Illegal immigrants raise the same issue today.
  • After the 1790 census, seats were apportioned under Thomas Jefferson’s rule, which violated quota and favoured populous states. Congress replaced it with Alexander Hamilton’s, which stays within quota and has no obvious bias. Washington vetoed the change, holding that “apportioned” meant divided and rounded, nothing more. Some suspected he, like Jefferson, was protecting their home state of Virginia, the most populous.

Paradoxes that keep coming back

  • Jefferson’s rule was dropped in 1841 for Senator Daniel Webster’s, which uses reallocation and violates quota only rarely. A decade later Hamilton’s rule was adopted. It soon produced the population paradox, in which a state that grew can lose a seat to one that shrank. Adding seats to compensate could break quota or trigger the Alabama paradox, in which enlarging the House costs some state a seat.
  • The paradoxes are random, not biased, and cancel in the long run. But fairness in the long run could be had by choosing legislators at random, and a random House of 435 would typically be off by about eight seats. Since votes often pass by one, politicians care. The paradoxes “no matter how firmly they are kicked out of the front door, they instantly come in again at the back”.
  • In 1871 Congress tried adjusting the size of the House until Hamilton’s and Webster’s rules agreed. The result was so chaotic, with extra seats added at the last minute, that it satisfied neither rule and many thought it unconstitutional. In 1921 no apportionment was made at all.
  • In 1901 the Census Bureau tabulated Hamilton’s rule for every House size from 350 to 400 seats. Colorado got three seats at every size except 357, where it got two. The chairman of the House apportionment committee, from Illinois, proposed 357 seats. Congress chose 386 seats and Webster’s rule instead. By what criterion was that more rightful? Taking a majority vote among rules is just another rule.
  • Representative Roger Q. Mills of Texas, in 1882: “I thought … that mathematics was a divine science … [but] here is a new system of mathematics that demonstrates the truth to be false.” Representative John E. Littlefield of Maine, threatened by the Alabama paradox in 1901: “God help the State of Maine when mathematics reach for her and undertake to strike her down.”
  • Mathematicians were consulted, twice through the National Academy of Sciences, and gave different advice without accusing each other of mathematical errors, a sign the problem is not mathematical. A 1948 panel including John von Neumann judged the rule devised by the statistician Joseph Adna Hill, still used today, the most impartial between states. The mathematicians Michel Balinski and Peyton Young later concluded it favours small states.

Every apportionment rule that stays within the quota suffers from the population paradox.Balinski and Young’s theorem, 1975

Arrow: no will of the people

  • After the Second World War a consensus emerged that the future depended on society-wide planning. In the West the aim was to satisfy citizens’ preferences, which raised the question of what society “wants” when people disagree. It became the subject of social-choice theory, a branch of game theory, and von Neumann and others turned to it. Interesting mathematics was found, and again and again the assumptions were shown to be inconsistent.
  • A bigger legislature would shrink rounding errors, but its factions would face the same problems internally. Taking people’s second and third choices into account brings more paradoxes.
  • In 1951 the economist Kenneth Arrow proved one of the first no-go theorems, which contributed to his Nobel prize in 1972. He set out five apparently self-evident conditions for any rule defining a group’s preferences, and proved them inconsistent (see the list). “So there is no such thing as ‘the will of the people’.”
  • Weighting preferences by intensity doesn’t help; there are still no-go theorems. Many systems also reward lying: someone who mildly prefers one option has reason to call the preference strong. Civic responsibility then gives extra weight to those who lack it, and a society too close-knit for lying has no secret ballot.

Decisions are creative

  • Arrow’s theorem applies inside one mind too. If each piece of evidence, feeling or argument is a voter with a weight, all thinking is irrational, unless one internal agent is a dictator, which starts an infinite regress.
  • The flaw is the model. It treats deciding as selecting among existing options by a fixed formula, which is only the perspiration phase at the end. “At the heart of decision-making is the creation of new options and the abandonment or modification of existing ones.” To choose an option rationally is to choose its explanation, and good explanations are hard to vary, so there is usually only one. Rivals are “not outweighed, but out-argued, refuted and abandoned”.
  • That is why splitting the difference between explanations usually produces something worse than either. Good explanations are discrete, separated by bad ones. An architect asked how tall a skyscraper can be begins with design ideas, not a formula; a voter who weights issues does so only after thinking through a political philosophy. Social-choice theory makes Zeno’s mistake, confusing an abstract process it has named “decision-making” with the real one.
  • Arrow’s “dictator” is just any agent with sole right to a decision. Laws against rape and involuntary surgery make everyone a dictator over their own body, and a free election makes every voter a dictator over their own ballot paper. It has nothing to do with secret police.
  • Commentators regret these theorems. Webster wrote that what cannot be done perfectly must be done “as near perfection as can be”. But “what sort of ‘perfection’ is a logical contradiction?” If a conception of justice conflicts with logic, it is unjust. “Wishing for something that is logically impossible is a sign that there is something better to wish for.”

Popper’s criterion and plurality voting

  • The better wish is Popper’s criterion: good political institutions make it as easy as possible to detect that a ruler or policy is a mistake and to remove it without violence. That replaces “who should rule?”, which framed the whole apportionment debate.
  • “The essence of democratic decision-making is not the choice made by the system at elections, but the ideas created between elections.” Voters are not a fount of wisdom from which policies are derived. They are fallibly seeking truth, and they are choosing which experiments to try next and which to abandon. “The politicians, and their policies, are those experiments.” Society is not zero-sum: the Enlightenment “got here by creating ex nihilo”.
  • Proportional representation leads to coalitions and compromise policies. But “If a policy is no one’s idea of what will work, then why should it work?” When a compromise fails, no one learns anything, because no one believed in it.
  • Proportional systems also hand disproportionate power to the third party. Peter Kurrild-Klitgaard found paradoxes in every one of eight recent Danish elections, including cases where most voters preferred party X to party Y but Y got more seats. In West Germany from 1949 to 1998, the Free Democrats, never above 12.8 per cent of the vote, chose which large party governed, twice switching sides and three times installing the less popular one. For the last twenty-nine years of that period Germany went only two weeks without an FDP foreign minister. When the Greens overtook them in 1998, the FDP was ousted at once and the Greens became kingmakers and took the Foreign Ministry.
  • Israel, with the world’s most proportional system, had so many small parties that at the time of writing the two largest together could not command a majority. Even proportionality failed: in 1992 right-wing parties won most votes but left-wing parties most seats, because more right-wing fringe parties fell below the threshold.
  • Plurality voting, “first past the post”, over-represents the two largest parties and can even put the party with fewer votes in power. But under Popper’s criterion it is better. Losers are removed entirely, winners carry sole responsibility, and a small shift in opinion produces a large change in government in the same direction. In Manitoba in 1926 the Conservatives won more than twice as many votes as any other party and none of the seventeen seats, and lost national power despite winning the most votes. At the next election a 3 per cent rise in their vote brought a 17 per cent rise in their share of seats and an overall majority.
  • This is not a blanket endorsement: racial divisions or traditions of violence can defeat any system, and democracy itself sometimes fails. But in advanced political cultures, “the idea that representative government depends on proportionate representation in the legislature is unequivocally a mistake.”
  • The US Senate gives California, nearly seventy times as populous as Wyoming, the same two seats, yet the Senate and House are rarely controlled by different parties. Persuasion changes options and preferences, and opinions converge, as they do in science, on issues once bitterly contested: slavery, women working, legal autopsies, promotion regardless of skin colour. Giving women the vote showed that half the population had been disenfranchised in every previous election, an injustice dwarfing all the apportionment disputes.
  • “The purpose of deferring to the majority in democratic systems should be to approach unanimity in the future.” And unanimity grows without controversy dying down: “human beings will never disagree any less than they do now, and that is a very good thing.” Human life can improve “as we advance from misconception to ever better misconception”.

The chapter’s own terms

Representative government; social-choice theory; Popper’s criterion: “Good political institutions are those that make it as easy as possible to detect whether a ruler or policy is a mistake, and to remove rulers or policies without violence when they are.”

Chapter 14

Why are Flowers Beautiful?

There is objective beauty as well as parochial attraction, and flowers are the evidence: they reliably please a species they never evolved to attract.

“I asked her what she thought wildflowers were for. She gave a rather thoughtful answer. ‘Two things,’ she said. ‘To make the world pretty, and to help the bees make honey for us.’ I was touched by this and sorry I had to tell her that it wasn’t true.”Richard Dawkins, Climbing Mount Improbable (1996), on his daughter Juliet, then six

  • In Peter Shaffer’s play Amadeus (1979), Mozart’s music is described thus: “Displace one note and there would be diminishment. Displace one phrase and the structure would fall.” That is Wheeler’s “how could it have been otherwise?” again: hard to vary while still doing the job, said of music and of scientific theory alike.
  • The prevailing view is that beauty is in the eye of the beholder. Deutsch calls the standard argument for that a relic of empiricism. You can’t deduce aesthetic values from facts, but you can’t deduce moral values or scientific theories either, and facts can still criticise aesthetic theories.
  • Objection: art depends on parochial human senses, so aliens who sense radio waves would have inaccessible art. Replies: our arts may be a first approximation to something universal; deaf composers have made great music; and prosthetic senses, wired in to create new qualia, would be no stranger than radio telescopes.
  • Deep truth is often beautiful. That beauty is called elegance. John Keats’s “Beauty is truth, truth beauty”, which Deutsch thinks was ironic, is refuted by what Thomas Huxley called “the great tragedy of Science — the slaying of a beautiful hypothesis by an ugly fact”. Yet the truth is so often elegant that elegance is a useful guide, and a slain beautiful hypothesis is usually replaced by a more beautiful one, as spontaneous generation was. That regularity needs explaining.
  • Science and art can look alike. Feynman said a theoretical physicist needs only paper, a pencil and a waste-paper basket; so did novelists before the typewriter. Beethoven laboured through draft after draft. Mozart and the mathematician Srinivasa Ramanujan seemed to create effortlessly, but the effort and mistakes must have happened invisibly inside their heads.
  • Is it all cultural fashion, like hemlines, or genetic, like the sparrows that the psychologist Shigeru Watanabe found prefer harmonious music to discordant? Cultural relativism struggles to explain what people think they are doing when they improve a tradition. And beauty is in form, not content: two photographs of the same scene can differ in value because one shows design in its composition, framing, cropping, lighting and focus.
  • Human taste is not simply genetic. People fast and abstain from sex. Skydivers go “not in spite of” the inborn fear of falling “but because of it”: the very sensation evolved to deter them becomes part of the beauty.

The flower puzzle

  • Flowers evolved to attract insects, which carry pollen between plants of the same species. “Before there were insects, there were no flowers on Earth.” Insects evolved to find the flowers with the best nectar, and the two co-evolved.
  • What is surprising is that flowers attract humans too, reliably, across cultures, including flowers never seen before. Most animals that choose mates by sight have evolved to find their own kind attractive, and most look hideous to us. The peacock’s tail is a rare coincidence; a leaf or a puddle is occasionally beautiful by chance. Flowers are beautiful reliably. Their roots and leaves are not.
  • The flower–insect signal faced a hard problem. It had to be easy for the right insects to recognise and hard for other flowers to forge, since a flower that got pollinated without making nectar would win. So both the criterion and the means of meeting it had to be hard to vary. Within a species, mating signals build on much shared genetic knowledge, as giraffe necks may have begun as a feeding adaptation before sexual selection took over. Between distant species there is nothing shared to build on.
  • Deutsch’s guess: “the easiest way to signal across such a gap with hard-to-forge patterns designed to be recognized by hard-to-emulate pattern-matching algorithms is to use objective standards of beauty.” So flowers must create objective beauty and insects recognise it, and humans can too. Dawkins’s daughter was partly right: prettiness is what flowers evolved to have. Honey is different. Its appeal to bees and people alike comes from a shared genetic heritage of using sugar.
  • Could we just like colour, contrast and symmetry? Some flowers are white to us, a spider in the bath contrasts more than any flower and is symmetrical, and orchids are very asymmetrical. The night sky, waterfalls and sunsets are beautiful but show no appearance of design, like Paley’s stone rather than his watch. Flowers have it: “Displace even one petal, and there would be diminishment.” We are like an archaeologist facing inscriptions in an unknown language.
  • Why do humans appreciate objective beauty? Because two humans face the same gap as two species. A human mind holds more information than any genome, and far more than the genetic information unique to one person. Artists signalling across that gap can use species-specific criteria, or reach for objective beauty. The same goes for all knowledge: to communicate something new, “we had better strive to rise above parochialism and seek universal truths.” This may be why humans began to do so.
  • A corollary Deutsch finds amusing: human sexual selection may have made human appearance objectively more beautiful than that of apes.
  • Applied beauty solves practical problems, like a graphical interface, a patriotic song or an advertisement, and usually uses the easier parochial kind. Pure art creates beauty for its own sake, including new standards and styles, and is the analogue of pure science. Both seek good explanations; in music the explanation is inexplicit, but “one day it will be expressible in words”. Poetry, like mathematics and physics, builds a variant of ordinary language to say efficiently what ordinary language can’t.
  • Greek art’s skill at reproducing the human body was perfectible, in the bad sense that it could reach a state hard to improve on. Utilitarian theories of art deprecate pure art as they deprecate pure science: “artistic values are not subordinate to, or derived from, anything else.” Art as self-expression conveys what is already there. Throwing paint at canvas or pickling sheep lacks the means of progress, because real progress is hard and involves many errors for each success.
  • The future of art is as open as the future of science. We may design new senses and new qualia. To Thomas Nagel’s question, “What is it like to be a bat?”, the full answer may come not from philosophy but from technological art that gives us the experience.

The chapter’s own terms

Aesthetics, “the philosophy of beauty”; elegance, “the beauty in explanations, mathematical formulae and so on”; explicit; inexplicit; implicit.

Chapter 15

The Evolution of Culture

Cultures are made of memes. Most societies in history survived by memes that disable criticism. Ours is the first long-lived society run on memes that depend on it.

Ideas that survive

  • A culture is “a set of ideas that cause their holders to behave alike in some ways”. Many of them are inexplicit: riding a bicycle, the meanings of words, concepts like freedom. The long-lived ones are memes, ideas that are replicators.
  • Memes can’t be copied exactly. No two people define every word identically, which is why movements split when their founder dies, and why holy books are argued over. Most ideas die within a lifetime. A long-lived meme is an exceptional idea, copied faithfully many times over. Two questions follow: what lets a meme resist change, and when can memes change for the better?
  • Most attempts to apply evolution to culture have got evolution wrong. Karl Marx thought his theory of history evolutionary because it had stages driven by economic “laws of motion”, and thought Darwin gave “a basis in natural science for the historical class struggle”. Nazism used “the survival of the fittest” to justify violence. But the competition in biology is between variants of genes within a species, and it produces cooperation, like that between flowers and insects, as well as conflict.
  • The biosphere is a grim place, full of plunder, deceit, conquest, enslavement, starvation and extermination, so analogies with it lead to grim politics: either opposing cultural evolution or excusing cruelty. “Arguments by analogy are fallacies.” Genes and memes share only the deepest level of explanation. There is no cultural analogue of a species, an organism, a cell or sex.
  • Jokes. In Isaac Asimov’s 1956 story “Jokester”, a scientist finds that people make witty remarks but never invent full jokes; they only repeat them. It is plausible that some jokes had no author and evolved as retold stories got funnier, or had many co-authors, none of whom knows why the joke works. Creativity itself is an evolutionary process inside a single brain.
  • Critics call memes vague. Were the Greek gods the same memes once the stories became fiction? Is Newton in English the same meme as Newton in Latin? That is like arguing about whether Pluto is a planet. Genes were understood long before DNA. A memeplex, like quantum theory, can be treated as one meme, but “2 + 3 = 5” is not a meme, since it can’t get itself copied except as part of arithmetic.
  • We often don’t know why we enact a meme. We may think a joke is funny because of surprise and find it still funny on rehearing. British English says “learning to play the piano” but never “the baseball”; American English allows “learning to play piano”. It isn’t that the British like the definite article: they say “in hospital”, Americans “in the hospital”.

How memes are copied

  • Genes are copied indiscriminately, whether or not they are expressed; you inherit genes for mending broken bones whether or not your parents broke any. A meme must be expressed as behaviour every time it is copied, because no one can see inside another’s brain. So it lives alternately in two forms, as a memory and as behaviour, and must survive two separate selections in every generation (see the figure).
  • Treating behaviour as the replicator would be Lamarckism. And intending to pass on a meme is not enough. Most new religions fail however hard their founders try. The survival of old religions isn’t explained by children being gullible either: if it were that easy, every adult would be a proficient algebra teacher.
  • Memes are “selfish”. The variant that spreads is the one best at displacing other memes, whether or not it helps its holders. Societies have been destroyed by memes that spread well, and people killed by irrational ideologies and fads. Some memes act like viruses; a newsworthy suicide is often followed by copycats.
  • Because a brain runs thousands of rounds of variation and selection before acting, and memes pass to people other than one’s children, meme evolution is far faster than gene evolution. The metaphor that puts civilisation in the last second of life’s day is misleading. “The whole of biological evolution was but a preface to the main story of evolution, the evolution of memes.”

Static societies

  • The post-Enlightenment West is the only society in history that has changed fast enough for people to notice for more than a couple of lifetimes. In a static society, people died under the same values, technology and way of life they were born under, and most changes were for the worse.
  • Deutsch tries to imagine one and fails. If people try to relieve their suffering and pass on improvements, even imperfectly, a single good idea spreads, and multiplied over minds and generations the result is “an exponentially increasing, revolutionary force transforming every aspect of the society”.
  • So something else must be happening. Taboos that enforce behaviour and forbid criticism are only a mopping-up operation, because no one can specify behaviour exactly or tell which deviations will matter. The primary method “is always — and can only be — to disable the source of new ideas, namely human creativity”. Static societies raise children to see themselves as existing to enact the society’s memes, and to feel pride and shame by that standard.
  • The memes didn’t plan this; the knowledge evolved. For each long-lived meme, millions of variants lacked that extra degree of ruthless efficiency at preventing rivals. “Just as genes for the eye implicitly ‘know’ the laws of optics, so the long-lived memes of a static society implicitly possess knowledge of the human condition, and use it mercilessly to evade the defences and exploit the weaknesses of the human minds that they enslave.” The damage is narrow and deep: a fear of particular supernatural entities, not general gullibility.
  • New ideas in a static society are not usually good ones. When the Black Death struck fourteenth-century Europe, the ideas that spread best were terrible: the end of the world, killing Jews and “witches”, crowding into churches (where fleas spread the plague), and the Flagellants whipping themselves. The authorities suppressed them to restore stasis. A few good ideas spread too, and may have helped end that society well, with the Renaissance.
  • Static societies settle issues by violence and sacrifice individuals to the prevention of change: “oppression is what it takes to keep a society static; oppression of a given kind will not last long unless the society is static.” Every society before the West was static or destroyed within a few generations. So the belief that people in primitive societies were free and happy is false. Hunter-gatherer tribes must have been static, and “creating knowledge is itself a natural human need and desire”, which they suppressed.

Dynamic societies: rational and anti-rational memes

  • A meme that must be adopted again and again by diverse people with unpredictable aims does best by being useful to all of them, and “the best way to seem useful to diverse people under diverse, unpredictable circumstances is to be useful”. That means true in the broadest sense: factually true, beautiful, morally right or funny. Newton’s laws help build cathedrals, bridges and artillery, so people with opposed aims all keep them alive.
  • Such rational memes depend on criticism for their faithful replication, since criticism weeds out their rivals. The old kind, which survive by disabling criticism, are anti-rational memes.
  • The hobgoblin. If a story about a hobgoblin makes children who fear it grow up to make their own children fear it, it is a meme. As a rational meme, criticism would push it towards extinction, open fiction, or pleasurable thrills. As an anti-rational meme, unpleasant emotions help it, and the hobgoblin’s non-existence is an advantage, because real dangers are finite and can be fought. “Just as rational memes evolve towards deep truths, anti-rational memes evolve away from them.”
  • The West became dynamic gradually. Deutsch suspects the transition began with Galileo and became irreversible with Newton, whose laws were so useful that their philosophical implications couldn’t be ignored. Jean-Jacques Rousseau tried, by arguing that reason was harmful and primitive life happy. Other nations are changing too, mostly through Western memes, and must become “Western” in how they operate or lose their knowledge.
  • The Enlightenment is incomplete. Girls striving to be ladylike and boys not crying are enacting ancient anti-rational memes about gender, though endorsing them is now stigmatised. Try going shopping in pyjamas, or painting your house in blue and brown stripes. In earlier societies “Because I say so” was “a curious type of meme whose explicit content is true though its holders do not believe it”.
  • The mix of rational and anti-rational memes makes our era unstable. Some anti-rational memes evolve towards rationality, as autocratic monarchy became constitutional monarchy. Others survive in subcultures; bigotry now exists “almost entirely in subcultures that suppress criticism”. Education still drills a standard curriculum, so people can spend the day using lasers to count cells in blood samples and the evening “sitting cross-legged and chanting to draw supernatural energy out of the Earth”.
  • Susan Blackmore’s The Meme Machine downplays creativity. She cites the historian George Basalla’s The Evolution of Technology, which rejects “the myth of the heroic inventor”, and denies progress towards anything objectively better. That is true of static societies. In ours, when evolution happens mostly inside one mind, “it is not meme evolution. It is creativity by a heroic inventor.”
  • How to spot an anti-rational meme in yourself: a complex, narrowly defined behaviour copied faithfully, that thwarts your aims, persists when its justification is gone, and that you explain badly. Watch for “Because I say so”, “It never did me any harm” and “Let us suppress criticism of our idea because it is true”. We failed at first to notice that the force of gravity doesn’t exist; locating hang-ups is easier.
  • The Enlightenment is the moment when explanatory knowledge begins to be the main cause of physical events, and what it attempts “has never worked before”. We have gone from victims and enforcers of an eternal status quo to mostly passive beneficiaries of innovation. The next step is to become “active agents of progress in the emerging rational society — and universe”.

The chapter’s own terms

Culture; rational meme, “an idea that relies on the recipients’ critical faculties to cause itself to be replicated”; anti-rational meme, one that “relies on disabling the recipients’ critical faculties”; static society, one whose changes happen too slowly for its members to notice, dominated by anti-rational memes; dynamic society, one dominated by rational memes. From the summary: “primitive societies are unimaginably unpleasant to live in.”

Chapter 16

The Evolution of Creativity

Creativity evolved in societies that barely innovated. It evolved to copy memes, because copying a meaning means guessing it, and that same ability can create new knowledge.

What use was creativity?

  • Creativity is the only adaptation that produces science, mathematics, art and philosophy, and its effects now dominate the planet: land use, rivers diverted, space stations carrying people higher than any biological adaptation could. “By the time you read these words, the computer on which I am writing them will be obsolete.”
  • Yet for most of our species’ existence an alien observer would have seen no sign of it. Other species use tools, bees use symbolic language, ants domesticate other species, and several species have memes. What they lack is a way to improve memes other than by random trial and error.
  • Creativity must have evolved fast, with our ancestors steadily out-breeding cousins who had slightly less. If they had used it to innovate, the fossil record would show improvements over generations. It doesn’t. Some members of Homo erectus made campfires 500,000 years ago, and before agriculture began about 12,000 years ago many thousands of years passed between noticeable changes. “Their ability to innovate was increasing rapidly, but they were barely innovating.” How could a rarely used ability be selected for?
  • Sexual selection? Perhaps creative displays attracted mates, as peacocks’ tails attract peahens. But creativity is too sophisticated a target, having evolved only once, and small differences in it would be hard to detect on any one occasion. Why not evolve multi-coloured hair instead? A variant, that creativity won social status by intrigue, faces the question of why it wasn’t also used for better spears. A skill for making beads in one generation might become a skill for making slingshots in the next.
  • In a static society innovation lowers your status. The only way to gain status by being more creative without breaking taboos is to enact the society’s memes more faithfully than anyone else. So the pivotal question is: “can enhanced creativity help one to be less innovative than other people?”

How do you replicate a meaning?

  • Meme replication is often described, by Susan Blackmore among others, as imitation. It can’t be. We can’t see ideas in other people’s brains or download them. We see only behaviour, and then later the meme is in our own brain. It looks like induction, and induction is impossible.
  • When we learn to wave, we learn when, how and to whom, though no one tells us. Popper used to start his lectures by asking students to “observe”, and waiting until someone asked what they were supposed to observe. He could have asked them to imitate him. Should they push him from the podium and stand there? Face the back of the room, as he did? Copy his heavy Austrian accent? There are infinitely many ways to “imitate Popper”, each a different theory of what was in his mind. Blackmore’s suggestion to “imitate the best imitators” has the same problem.
  • Stating a meme doesn’t transmit it either. Popper: “It is impossible to speak in such a way that you cannot be misunderstood.” Laws refer to what is “reasonable”, which no one can define precisely enough for someone from another culture to apply, yet people within a culture learn it. We need inexplicit knowledge to understand explicit statements, not the other way round.
  • Parrots and apes manage because their genes already know what imitating means. A parrot records sounds that meet an inborn criterion and replays them under inborn conditions, using a translation system from ears to voice, thought to involve “mirror neurons”. It never chooses: faced with a barking dog, it copies the sound, never the wagging tail. A parrot at Popper’s lectures would transmit his words, with his accent, as the air does, and would repeat recipes for fried parrot just as eagerly.
  • Apes seem to understand more. An ape learns to crack nuts with rocks, aiming, fetching the nut if it rolls away, and hitting until it cracks. But the evolutionary psychologist Richard Byrne has shown that apes do this by behaviour parsing: breaking a stream of behaviour into elements they already know how to perform, such as biting or grasping nettles without being stung, and linking them by simple statistics, such as which right-hand action goes with which left-hand one. It takes years, allows only simple memes, and still gives apes food no other animal can reach. Apes cannot imitate sounds at all.
  • Humans do almost the opposite. The meaning is what they are trying to discover. A student can acquire a lecturer’s idea without being able to repeat a single sentence, and with no trace of the lecturer’s accent. Repeating an idea in different words helps a human and hinders a parrot. A human can correct for a lecturer’s slips, or for a reporter’s misconceptions, as a conjuring expert can reconstruct a trick from an audience’s false account. “Rather than imitating behaviour, a human being tries to explain it.” If we end up behaving like others, “it is because we have rediscovered the same idea.”

Both puzzles have the same solution

  • Someone acquiring a meme faces the same logical challenge as a scientist: discovering a hidden explanation from evidence. Imitation theories of culture make the same mistake as empiricism, inductivism and Lamarckism, expecting the environment to instruct the mind. Popper, in The Myth of the Framework: “there is no such thing as instruction from without the structure … As Ernst Gombrich says, ‘making comes before matching’.” “Memes, like scientific theories, are not derived from anything. They are created afresh by the recipient.”
  • So, as so often with universality, the capacity for universal explanation didn’t evolve for a universal purpose. It evolved to increase the volume, speed and accuracy of acquiring memes, and the easiest way for evolution to do that was to make us universal explainers.
  • The likely sequence: early pre-humans had simple, valuable memes like apes’; creativity filled the niche for replicating them more cheaply; memory, finer motor control and brain structures for language co-evolved; meme bandwidth grew; memes evolved towards faithfulness, which meant becoming anti-rational; and static societies, presumably tribes, formed. Innovation stayed imperceptibly slow while the capacity for it grew.
  • To thrive you had to meet the expectations of parents, priests, chiefs and potential mates, which were never fully stated. You had to understand the point of what respected people did and achieve it. “Hence, paradoxically, it requires creativity to thrive in a static society — creativity that enables one to be less innovative than other people.”
  • Creativity is software. The hardware that makes it practicable, chiefly memory, and perhaps mirror neurons able to copy the sounds of a language, was already being selected for pre-creative meme replication. Deutsch speculates that the creativity program is partly genes and partly memes. “We do not know what was being gradually increased in that approach to a universal explainer. If we did, we could program one tomorrow.”

The future of creativity

  • Other proposed triggers for human evolution include walking upright, which freed the hands; climate change; sexual selection; the “Machiavellian hypothesis” that intelligence evolved to predict and deceive others; and enhanced aping, which Deutsch has argued is impossible. Whatever set things off, Blackmore’s central idea that brains evolved to replicate memes must be true, because no human-level achievement is possible without explanatory memes, and those require creativity.
  • Creativity is also sufficient. Deaf, blind and paralysed people acquire and create ideas to a more or less full extent, so upright walking, fine motor control and speech parsing are not what makes people people.
  • “The beginning of creativity was, in that sense, the beginning of infinity.” And static societies were “a hideous practical joke that the universe played on the human species”: a creativity that could have produced endless innovation was turned, by the very memes it preserved, to preventing it, keeping people “locked in a crude, suffering state for eternity. Only the Enlightenment, hundreds of thousands of years later … may at last have made it practical to escape from that eternity into infinity.”

The chapter’s own terms

Imitation: “Copying behaviour. This is different from human meme replication, which copies the knowledge that is causing the behaviour.”

Chapter 17

Unsustainable

Easter Island is no warning to us, history is not driven by geography, and every attempt to live sustainably fails. Only progress is sustainable.

Two films on Easter Island

  • Easter Island is famous for its stone statues. Settlers may have arrived as early as the fifth century CE and built a Stone Age civilisation that collapsed over a thousand years later, by some accounts with starvation, war and perhaps cannibalism. The prevailing theory blames deforestation: they cut down the most useful tree species entirely, although they needed timber for shelter, boats and nets. The archaeologist Terry Hunt disputes this, arguing that settlers arrived only in the thirteenth century, that rats did the deforesting, and that European diseases destroyed the society. Deutsch doesn’t take sides. He uses the prevailing story to expose a fallacy.
  • In 1972 Jacob Bronowski travelled to the island, some 14,000 kilometres by ship from California and back, in poor health and at times carried by his crew, to film part of The Ascent of Man. His point was that our civilisation is unique in its capacity to make progress. “People often ask about Easter Island, How did men come here? They came here by accident: that is not in question. The question is, Why could they not get off?”
  • For Bronowski the statues were evidence of failure: “Why were they all made alike? You see them sitting there, like Diogenes in their barrels … These frozen faces, these frozen frames in a film that is running down, mark a civilization which failed to take the first step on the ascent of rational knowledge.” Of the hundreds of statues, fewer than half reached their intended places, and as many as 10 per cent are still in transit on roads built for them. Statue-building accelerated just before it stopped. The roads were made of trees.
  • David Attenborough had commissioned Bronowski’s series as controller of BBC2. A quarter of a century later he ended his own series, The State of the Planet (BBC, 2000), at the same statues with the opposite message. He called them “vivid evidence of the technological and artistic skills of the people who once lived here”, and the island “A warning of what the future could hold”: a “miniature world” with “ample resources” whose people “lived well” until “The old culture that had sustained them was abandoned and the statues toppled.”
  • “Sustain” has two almost opposite meanings: providing people with what they need, and preventing change. The knowledge that sustains life in Oxfordshire does so only in the first sense. Building a giant statue is sustainable: you can live exactly as before. Inventing better farming, or curing a disease that kills children, is unsustainable. The population grows, fewer are needed in the fields, and “You have to live the solution.” That is why Britain, with a harsher climate than subtropical Easter Island, supports at least three times its peak population density at a far higher standard of living, without the forests that once covered it.
  • The islanders’ culture sustained them in both senses. It “placed forests — literally — beneath statues”. It went on sustaining a fixed pattern of behaviour after it stopped feeding them, and so prevented the only remedy, creative thought. Attenborough saw its fall as the tragedy; Bronowski and Deutsch see its survival for centuries as the tragedy.

Resource management explains nothing

  • “Good resource management” has almost no content, since anything can be called a resource. Julius Caesar’s mistake could be summarised as “imprudent iron management, resulting in an excessive build-up of iron in his body”. A Popperian account would note that Caesar had made sure he couldn’t be removed without violence, and that his assassination entrenched that arrangement. One must understand the politics, the psychology and the philosophy. “Not the cutlery.”
  • The question is never why people made mistakes, since problems are inevitable, but why they failed to correct them. The islanders failed at medicine and astronomy too, and their errors were too elementary to teach us anything. Their small successes, such as “stone mulching” to grow crops in poor soil, might be worth study.
  • The knowledge that would have saved them has been ours for centuries: a sextant to explore the ocean and bring back seeds and ideas, wealth and writing to recover from disaster, and the rudiments of a scientific outlook. Alchemists never achieved transmutation because they would have needed nuclear physics, and the progress they did make, which became chemistry, depended mostly on how they thought, not on which chemicals were nearby. “The conditions for a beginning of infinity exist in almost every human habitation on Earth.”

Against geography as destiny

  • In Guns, Germs and Steel, the biogeographer Jared Diamond offers what he calls an “ultimate explanation” of why Europeans conquered the Americas, Australasia and Africa and not the reverse: the continents had different plants, animals, microbes and geography. The Americas, for example, lacked animals fit to be beasts of burden.
  • Llamas were used as beasts of burden in the Andes. Friedrich Engels made the same argument, with the same proviso, in 1884, in The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State: America “had no mammals that could be domesticated except the llama, which, moreover, was only found in one part of South America”. Diamond saw that the llamas’ failure to spread “cries out for explanation”, and proposed that a hot lowland region blocked them from the highlands of Central America.
  • But traders travelled between South and Central America for centuries, certainly by sea. Knowledge can aim at a distant target and ignore the space between. Polynesians carried livestock across a far more formidable barrier. Perhaps the Andeans were set in their ways, or new uses of animals were taboo, or attempts failed by bad luck, but it wasn’t geography.
  • Had the Polynesians been better traders, horses might have reached South America across the Pacific, no more impressive a feat than Hannibal taking elephants over the Alps. Had Athens’ enlightenment continued, Athenians might have settled the Pacific. Had the Andeans bred giant war llamas, their historians would credit the llama. The Americas once had wild horses, mammoths and mastodons; one hunter who thought of riding one might have sent mounted armies back through Alaska to conquer the Old World.
  • In early prehistory biogeography did explain much, but abstract language, explanation, surplus wealth and long-range trade gave ideas causal power. The Soviet Union lost the Cold War “because its ideology wasn’t true”, and no study of climate or minerals could show that. One of the most false things about that ideology was the idea of a mechanical, non-human ultimate explanation of history.
  • Diamond says he wrote his book to counter racist explanations of European success. Deutsch replies that the only alternative to one dehumanising explanation isn’t another. The differences between Sparta and Athens, or Savonarola and Lorenzo de’ Medici, were not in their genes or their landscapes but in their ideas. “It is knowledge alone that converts landscapes into resources.” “Marx, Engels and Diamond have it the wrong way round.”
  • A thousand years is a long time for a static society; most were probably destroyed by the first challenge that needed a new pattern of behaviour. Even the island’s isolation and hospitality depended on what its people knew. Thousands live on Easter Island today without a forest, and are planting one because they want to and know how. Sustaining the statue-obsessed society was never an option. The only options were sudden, painful collapse or slow change for the better. “The fall of tyranny is never enough”: without optimism and a tradition of criticism, nothing improves. Western civilisation already has what it takes to avoid an Easter Island disaster; if it falls, it will be in some other way.

Prophets of resource depletion

  • In 1971, as a schoolboy, Deutsch attended a lecture called “Population, Resources, Environment” by the population scientist Paul Ehrlich. People would be starving by the billion within ten years, twenty at best. The Vietnam War was a last struggle for tin, rubber and petroleum. Crime and mental illness were linked to overpopulation. The United States would have to cut its population by three-quarters, to 50 million, to keep its 1971 standard of living; the world was overpopulated by a factor of seven; even Australia was near its limit.
  • A girl asked what would happen if one of the problems were solved within a few years. Ehrlich asked how it could be, and said it would only delay the catastrophe. Deutsch stopped worrying: the prophecy amounted to “If we stop solving problems, we are doomed.” The catastrophe scheduled for 1991 has not arrived. Ehrlich thought he was measuring resources; he was prophesying the content of future knowledge.
  • A few years later a graduate student in environmental science told Deutsch that colour television was a sign of the coming collapse of consumer society, “conspicuous consumption” in Thorstein Veblen’s phrase. Its red phosphor needed europium, one of the rarest elements, and known reserves would last for only a few hundred million more sets, leaving a world of permanent class distinction between those with colour and those without.
  • Deutsch was puzzled: why should the laws of physics allow colour television only just, as if the centre of the earth happened to be within a few kilometres of the centre of the universe? The student was right that no alternative red phosphor has been found. Yet Deutsch now writes in front of a colour display with no europium in it, made of liquid crystals of common elements, and enough europium has since been mined to give everyone on earth a dozen europium screens. Colour displays are given away free with magazines.

In the pessimistic conception, that distinctive ability of people is a disease for which sustainability is the cure. In the optimistic one, sustainability is the disease and people are the cure.Chapter 17

  • Expecting problems always to be solved in time would be the same fallacy in reverse. The deeper mistake of the Malthusians is claiming a way to avert disaster, sustainability, which denies that problems are inevitable. Malthus couldn’t have known that the newly discovered element uranium would matter to civilisation. No resource plan prevents disaster, just as no political system guarantees good leaders; “the idea that the future can be scientifically planned” is one of the ideas that reliably cause disasters.
  • Antibiotics may be only a temporary triumph, as resistant bacteria evolve and air travel and big cities make a pandemic worse than the Black Death possible. But “all triumphs are temporary”, and “Only progress is sustainable.” Dispersing people and restricting travel would not pay for research into new antibiotics, did not prevent the Black Death, and would not cure cancer. We need a large research community, the wealth to fund it and the technology to use what it finds.

Climate change

  • Carbon dioxide from technology is warming the atmosphere, and the harms (droughts, rising seas, disrupted farming, extinctions) are forecast to outweigh the benefits (better crop yields, more plant growth, fewer deaths from cold). Trillions of dollars hang on supercomputer simulations and on economists’ projections. Deutsch makes three observations.
  • First, we have been lucky. The laws of physics alone say the emissions must eventually warm the planet harmfully. Had the danger arrived in 1902, Veblen’s time, there would have been no computers, thanks partly to Babbage’s failure, and almost no atmospheric physicists. A sea wall was affordable only to places like the Netherlands. The attempt to build a Panama canal had just failed with thousands of deaths. Today almost any coastal nation could build coastal defences, buying decades.
  • Second, the simulations make conditional predictions; the economic forecasts are prophecies. A forecast of twentieth-century emissions made in 1902 would have missed nuclear power, since radioactivity had only just been discovered, and then would have missed the campaign that stopped nuclear power’s growth, “ironically, on environmental grounds”.
  • Third, the sensitivity of temperature to carbon dioxide isn’t accurately known. The political argument has fixed on how far warming is human-caused, as if one should prepare only for man-made hurricanes, and as if a natural rise in sea level should simply be endured.
  • Deutsch calls global warming a disaster we did not foresee, and the prevailing policy of preventing emissions by vast spending and severe worldwide restrictions “already a disaster by any reasonable measure”. It was under way in 1971, yet Ehrlich warned that agriculture would be devastated by global cooling, from smog and the contrails of supersonic aircraft.
  • “An ounce of prevention equals a pound of cure” only when you know what to prevent. The world should be busier with ways to lower the temperature or to thrive at a higher one, cheaply: removing carbon dioxide from the air, making clouds over the oceans to reflect sunlight, encouraging marine organisms to absorb carbon. These are still minor research efforts.
  • Even aspiring to sustainability is dangerous: it means forcing the future into our image, “endlessly reproducing our lifestyle, our misconceptions and our mistakes”. The alternative is “an open-ended journey of creation and exploration whose every step is unsustainable until it is redeemed by the next”. If that became our ethic, “the ascent of man, the beginning of infinity, will have become, if not secure, then at least sustainable.”

The chapter’s own terms

The ascent of man: the beginning of infinity; Bronowski’s The Ascent of Man was one of the inspirations for the book. Sustain: two almost opposite meanings, to provide someone with what they need and to prevent things from changing.

Chapter 18

The Beginning

We will never be “nearly there”. Infinite ignorance is the condition of infinite potential, and what lies ahead is infinity either way.

“This is Earth. Not the eternal and only home of mankind, but only a starting point of an infinite adventure. All you need do is make the decision [to end your static society]. It is yours to make.” [With that decision] came the end, the final end of Eternity. — And the beginning of Infinity.Isaac Asimov, The End of Eternity (1955), the chapter’s motto

We are never nearly there

  • Eratosthenes of Cyrene measured the earth’s circumference in the third century BCE, fairly close to the true 40,000 kilometres. For most of history that seemed enormous. Astronomy and travel have made the earth small. In theory, though, we still assume our knowledge is nearly complete. The economist David Friedman has remarked that most people think an income about twice their own would satisfy anyone reasonable. It is as hard to imagine knowing twice as much.
  • Even Richard Feynman, in The Character of Physical Law (1965): “I think there will certainly not be novelty, say for a thousand years … It is like the discovery of America — you only discover it once.” He forgot that the idea of a “law” of nature itself changes, and that fundamental discoveries bring new modes of explanation. “Like the guests at Infinity Hotel, we shall never be ‘nearly there’.”
  • The dismal version of “nearly there” says knowledge is bounded by law or decree. The cheerful version says ignorance will soon be eliminated, and is pessimistic underneath: in politics, utopians promise that a finite set of known changes will perfect humanity, “a well-known recipe for dogmatism and tyranny”.
  • Had Lagrange and Michelson been right, gravity, the chemical elements and the sun’s light would have stayed lists of facts forever; there would be no atomic structure, no dynamics of space and time, no cosmology, no link between physics and computation. Physics would be “a tiny, frozen island of explanation in an ocean of incomprehensibility”. Both men had made discoveries that could have led them to the progress they denied, but “almost no one is creative in fields in which they are pessimistic.”
  • Perhaps theories should be called misconceptions from the start: Einstein’s Misconception of Gravity improved on Newton’s, which improved on Kepler’s; the neo-Darwinian Misconception of Evolution improved on Darwin’s and his on Lamarck’s. Or knowledge should be framed as moving from problems to better problems. Einstein’s problems were rooted in Newton and Euclid. Most of today’s are rooted in twentieth-century physics.
  • Socrates claimed to be deeply ignorant. Popper, in Conjectures and Refutations (1963): “while differing widely in the various little bits we know, in our infinite ignorance we are all equal.” “Infinite ignorance is a necessary condition for there to be infinite potential for knowledge.”

The end of science?

  • In 1996 the journalist John Horgan argued in The End of Science that the final truths in all fundamental areas had been found in the twentieth century, as far as human minds can grasp them. His basic misconception was empiricism: he thought science alone “resolves questions”, while art and philosophy produce only incompatible interpretations. In fact there is objective truth in all those fields, and finality in none.
  • He took from postmodern criticism a confusion between two kinds of ambiguity: multiple true meanings, “an attribute of deep ideas”, and deliberate vagueness, “an attribute of deep silliness”. He called art and philosophy “ironic” fields and predicted that the future belongs to “ironic science”. His premises made the conclusion inevitable: any future discovery can be split into a new equation, not fundamental, and a new interpretation, merely ironic.
  • Past failed prophecies don’t refute his, as he rightly said. But “Never before in the history of human thought has it been so obvious that our knowledge is tiny and our ignorance vast.” Physics has two radically inconsistent systems of the world, quantum theory and general relativity. The Chapter 11 picture of continuous clouds of instances needs continuous spacetime, and a spacetime affected by the cloud’s gravity would become discrete.

Cosmology since 1996

  • When Horgan wrote, and when Deutsch wrote The Fabric of Reality, all viable models had the expansion of the universe slowing forever, ending either in endless expansion or in a “Big Crunch”. That mattered because it decided whether a computer could execute unboundedly many steps. In a universe expanding forever, it seemed, the inhabitants would run out of energy. The cosmologist Frank Tipler found that in certain collapsing universes the Big Crunch allowed Infinity Hotel’s faster-and-faster trick: infinitely many computations in finite time, powered by the collapse’s tides, with minds uploaded into computers made of “something like pure tides”. He called them omega-point universes.
  • Observations of distant supernovae ruled them out. The universe is expanding at an accelerating rate, driven by something called dark energy. The best working hypothesis is an extra term in the equations of gravity, first mooted by Einstein and then dropped, and proposed again in the 1980s as an effect of quantum field theory. No theory yet predicts its size. “So much for cosmology being a fundamentally completed science.”
  • Dark energy might itself be harnessed, with computation running ever slower but for unboundedly many steps. “Dark energy, which has ruled out one scenario for the unlimited growth of knowledge, would provide the literal driving force of another.”
  • In infinite space, ever more improbable things come into view as the visible volume grows: watches that formed spontaneously, “asteroids that happen to be good likenesses of William Paley”. Light fades with distance, but one phenomenon will always reach us: a civilisation colonising the universe without limit.
  • A single infinite universe could serve anthropic arguments better than parallel ones. But all functions in physics are analytic, and an analytic function that is non-zero at one point is zero only at isolated points. So the probability of astrophysicists, non-zero for our constants, is non-zero for almost any, and there would be infinitely many astrophysicists whatever the constants. The anthropic explanation “has cancelled itself out”.

Suicide, simulation and doomsday

  • Quantum suicide. Buy a lottery ticket and a machine that kills you in your sleep if you lose; in every history where you wake, you have won. That needs an extra assumption, that histories without the decision-maker can be ignored, which Deutsch guesses is false.
  • The simulation argument, whose most cogent proponent is the philosopher Nick Bostrom, says the future will simulate our world many times, so most instances of us are simulated, so we almost certainly are. Suppose space were layered like puff pastry, with a million layers in London and one in Oxford. Waking in the dark, unsure of the city, you would not bet on London. “We should be counting histories not instances.” And is running such a simulation moral, given the suffering it recreates? Are two identical qualia one or two? He knows no good answer. Nor to this: set every bit of a large computer at random with a quantum randomiser and switch it on, running every possible AI program, including ones in every state of suffering, in some history. Is that “the worst crime ever committed”, merely inadvisable, or trivial?
  • The doomsday argument says a typical human is about halfway through all humans who will ever live. It is prophecy. If the total is infinite, every human lives unusually early, “so, if anything, it suggests that we are at the beginning of infinity.” Illness and old age will be cured “certainly within the next few lifetimes”, and brain backups will prevent accidental death, so the present generation will be “one of the last that will have short lives”. A count of humans then says nothing about how long the species lasts.
  • The Singularity. In 1993 the mathematician Vernor Vinge predicted that within about thirty years technology would become impossible to predict, linked to AI. Deutsch hopes AI arrives by then but sees “no sign yet of the theoretical progress” it needs. There can be no superhuman minds, because humans are already universal explainers. AIs will think faster with more memory, and “humans and AIs will never be other than equal.” Nor will innovation become too fast to cope with: “if everyone were suddenly able to think a million times as fast, no one would feel hurried.” The economist Robin Hanson counts several past singularities, such as the agricultural and industrial revolutions. The early Enlightenment was one: someone who lived through the English Civil War and the religious fanatics’ victory in 1651 could see the Royal Society founded in 1660 and, in 1688, the Glorious Revolution, where the historian Roy Porter dates the English Enlightenment’s start.
  • Every prediction carries the proviso “unless the creation of new knowledge intervenes”. Prophesying beyond that horizon is futile, but wondering and speculating beyond it are vital, since every unforeseeable new idea starts as a speculation, and every speculation starts with a problem.

Candles in the dark

  • We are where Eratosthenes was: we know an immense amount, and we know how ignorant we are. Universality tells us AI is attainable by programming, but we have no idea how to write the program. We don’t know what qualia are or how creativity works. We learned the genetic code decades ago without knowing why it has the reach it has. Both of our deepest theories in physics must be false. We know people matter fundamentally, but not whether we will be among them: “we may fail, or give up, and intelligences originating elsewhere in the universe may be the beginning of infinity.”
  • John Wheeler, in Gravitation (1973, with Charles Misner and Kip Thorne), imagined writing every candidate equation for the ultimate laws on sheets across the floor, then ordering them to fly: “Not one of those equations will put on wings, take off, or fly. Yet the universe ‘flies’.” What distinguishes laws that are instantiated from laws that are not? What distinguishes a simulated person from a recording of one? Do two identical simulations carry double the moral value?
  • “We have lit only a few candles here and there. We can cower in their parochial light until something beyond our ken snuffs us out, or we can resist.” The world is explicable, with higher levels of emergence and access to abstractions in mathematics, morality and aesthetics. But some things don’t make sense until we rectify them: “Death does not make sense. Stagnation does not make sense. A bubble of sense within endless senselessness does not make sense.”

What lies ahead of us is in any case infinity. All we can choose is whether it is an infinity of ignorance or of knowledge, wrong or right, death or life.The book’s last words

Fifteen years on

What held up

The book is mostly philosophy, but it makes datable claims about technology, science and politics. Here is how they look from 2026. Where a claim was a statement of fact in 2010, we note what has changed since.

Held

Scaling quantum computers is “gradually being met” Ch. 11

In 2011 the best machines had about ten qubits. Google’s 53-qubit Sycamore processor performed a narrow task in 2019 that it claimed was beyond classical supercomputers, IBM’s Condor chip passed a thousand qubits in 2023, and in 2024 Google’s Willow chip showed errors falling as its error-correcting codes grew. Useful computations of the kind Deutsch describes, exploiting a few hundred error-corrected qubits, are still ahead.

Partly

Better imitation of people would not bring machines that think Ch. 7

In 2010 chatbots were “no better at the task of seeming to think than Eliza was”, and Deutsch expected no real progress without a theory of creativity. The imitation came anyway. Large language models, from ChatGPT in 2022 onwards, converse fluently on any topic, and in a 2025 study by Cameron Jones and Benjamin Bergen, GPT-4.5, told to adopt a persona, was judged the human in three-way Turing tests 73 per cent of the time. His behavioural expectation missed. His deeper claim, that passing for human is not the same as creating knowledge, is exactly what is now argued over.

Held

This is the first century in which we could defend ourselves from an asteroid Ch. 9

In September 2022 NASA’s DART spacecraft struck the small asteroid Dimorphos and shortened its orbit around its partner by about half an hour: the first demonstration that a human-made impact can change an asteroid’s path.

Held

Easter Island’s collapse may not have been ecocide Ch. 17

Deutsch declined to judge the prevailing theory and cited Terry Hunt’s alternative. Evidence has since moved Hunt’s way. A 2024 survey of the island’s rock gardens suggested the island only ever supported around three thousand people, and a 2024 study of ancient Rapanui genomes found no genetic sign of a population crash, along with evidence of contact with Native Americans centuries before Europeans arrived. The book’s argument never depended on the parable being true.

Held

The unsolved problems he lists are still unsolved Ch. 8, 18

Quantum gravity, P ≠ NP, the nature of qualia and why the genetic code has its reach all remain open. The twin-prime conjecture has seen real progress: in 2013 Yitang Zhang proved that infinitely many pairs of primes differ by at most 70 million, and a collaborative project soon cut the bound to 246. The conjecture itself, a gap of exactly 2, is still unproved.

Open

Dark energy is an extra term in the equations of gravity Ch. 18

Deutsch’s “best working hypothesis” was Einstein’s cosmological constant. Acceleration is well established, and a constant still fits most data, but in 2024 and 2025 results from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument hinted that dark energy may be weakening over time. If confirmed, the “constant” is not constant, and his point that cosmology is far from finished is only strengthened.

Held

Proportional systems give power to small parties and make governments hard to remove Ch. 13

Israel held five general elections between 2019 and 2022; after the last, the two largest parties together still held only 56 of 120 seats. In Germany the FDP joined a three-party coalition in 2021; the coalition collapsed in a dispute with the FDP in November 2024, and the party then fell out of the Bundestag in the February 2025 election. The kingmaker pattern he described persisted, and so did voters’ difficulty in removing it directly.

Partly

Climate policy would centre on emissions cuts, with cooling and adaptation neglected Ch. 17

The 2015 Paris Agreement made emissions targets the centre of policy, as he expected. Research on removing carbon dioxide and on reflecting sunlight has grown but remains small and contested beside spending on cuts. His aside about the campaign against nuclear power looks apt: Germany closed its last three reactors in April 2023. Meanwhile the cheap solar power and batteries now cutting emissions are the kind of unforeseen knowledge he said forecasts miss.

Held

Against pandemics, capacity to respond beats prevention Ch. 17

He argued that survival depends on research, wealth and technology able to cope with the unforeseen, and that delaying tactics can only be a minor part. In the COVID-19 pandemic, lockdowns bought time at great cost, and what ended the emergency was the rapid development of vaccines, several authorised within a year of the virus’s genome being published.

Held

The many-universes view remains a minority among physicists Ch. 11, 12

Deutsch called it “a decidedly minority view”, which it still is. Surveys of physicists, including a large one run by Nature for quantum mechanics’ centenary in 2025, still find the Copenhagen interpretation the most popular. His explanation, bad philosophy, is of course contested.

Open

Vinge’s Singularity “within about thirty years”, i.e. about 2023 Ch. 18

Deutsch rejected superhuman minds and the idea of a discontinuity, and saw no theoretical progress towards AI. By 2023 there was no general intelligence in his sense, but there was a burst of AI capability, and much talk of runaway change. Whether today’s systems are a step towards people, or very good perspiration, is the live question.

Not yet

Illness and old age cured “certainly within the next few lifetimes” Ch. 18

Too early to judge. Human life expectancy has not changed course, and no treatment has been shown to slow human ageing, though research on the biology of ageing has grown. His claim was about a few lifetimes, not fifteen years.

Untested

“Eliminate the graduate student” Ch. 7

Deutsch proposed a test of whether artificial evolution creates knowledge: evolve a working robot’s program from random numbers, with only natural processor errors as mutations. To our knowledge no one has run it in the form he specified.

Context

Where it has been argued with

Probability in the multiverse

If every outcome happens, what does it mean to say one is more probable? In 1999 Deutsch argued, in “Quantum Theory of Probability and Decisions”, that a rational agent in a multiverse must weight outcomes by quantum measure, and David Wallace developed the argument in The Emergent Multiverse (2012). Critics, including the physicist Adrian Kent and the philosopher Huw Price in the 2010 volume Many Worlds?, which also contains Deutsch’s “Apart from Universes”, argued that the derivation assumes what it sets out to prove. Chapter 11 presents the measure as settled; the question is still debated.

Is AI blocked by philosophy?

Deutsch restated Chapter 7 in his 2012 essay “Creative blocks” for Aeon: the laws of physics imply AI must be possible, and what holds it up is a missing theory of creativity. Large language models have since shown fluent conversation without any such theory. His supporters answer that these systems recombine existing human knowledge rather than create explanations. Critics answer that his standard, “if it can already be programmed, it has nothing to do with intelligence”, can never be met by any program.

Optimism against existential risk

Rees (2003), then Nick Bostrom in “Existential Risk Prevention as Global Priority” (2013) and Toby Ord in The Precipice (2020), which put the chance of existential catastrophe this century at about one in six, argue that some mistakes leave no one to learn from them. Deutsch’s reply is that such numbers are prophecy and that caution is itself a gamble. Critics ask whether “all evils are caused by insufficient knowledge” could ever be shown false, since any failure can be put down to not knowing enough.

Geography, institutions, ideas

Jared Diamond’s Guns, Germs, and Steel (1997) is Chapter 17’s target. Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson’s Why Nations Fail (2012) also rejected the geography hypothesis, arguing that institutions explain wealth, which sits close to Deutsch’s Popperian stress on error-correcting institutions. Defenders of Diamond say environment shaped the opportunities for creativity without denying it. See Guns, Germs, and Steel and Why Nations Fail on this site.

Do humans imitate?

Chapter 16 says human culture is not copied by imitation. The anthropologist Joseph Henrich, in The Secret of Our Success (2015), argues the reverse: cumulative culture rests on faithful, even blind, copying. In a 2005 experiment Victoria Horner and Andrew Whiten found that children copied causally useless actions when opening a puzzle box that young chimpanzees skipped. Deutsch’s view has an ally in the anthropologist Dan Sperber, who argued in 2000 that ideas are reconstructed by each recipient rather than copied, though Sperber doubted that memes are replicators at all.

Is beauty objective?

Denis Dutton’s The Art Instinct (2009) explains shared tastes, such as a liking for certain landscapes, as evolved adaptations. That is exactly the parochial explanation Chapter 14 rejects for flowers. Evolutionary aestheticians reply that insects and humans could share perceptual biases without any beauty being objective.

Voting systems

Popper himself made the case against proportional representation in “The Open Society and Its Enemies Revisited”, an article in The Economist in 1988. Voters have split on it. The UK rejected the alternative vote in a 2011 referendum by 67.9 per cent to 32.1 per cent, while New Zealand moved to a proportional system in the 1990s. Political scientists note that plurality systems can entrench two parties and leave large minorities without voice, which Popper’s criterion weighs lightly.

Sweep and tone

Many readers find the book exhilarating. Philosophers have objected that its dismissals of induction, Wittgenstein and “bad philosophy” are sweeping, and that its confidence about the multiverse outruns the field’s. Its admirers reply that the book asks to be criticised, and that it names the tests that would refute it.

Terms of art

Glossary

Explanation
A statement about what is there, what it does, and how and why.
Good explanation
One that is hard to vary while still accounting for what it purports to account for.
Reach
The ability of an explanation to solve problems beyond those it was created to solve.
Creativity
The capacity to create new explanations.
Empiricism
The misconception that knowledge is derived from sensory experience.
Induction
The non-existent process of deriving general theories from repeated experience.
Instrumentalism
The view that theories are only instruments for prediction, not explanations of reality.
Justificationism
The misconception that knowledge must be justified by some authority or secure source.
Fallibilism
The recognition that there are no authoritative sources of knowledge and no reliable means of justifying ideas as true.
Theory-laden
Of observation: never free of prior theory about what to look at and how to interpret it.
Parochialism
Mistaking appearance for reality, or local regularities for universal laws.
Principle of Mediocrity
The claim that there is nothing significant about humans. Rejected.
Spaceship Earth
The idea that the biosphere is a life-support system for humans. Rejected.
Person
An entity that can create explanatory knowledge.
Universal constructor
Something that can transform matter into anything the laws of nature allow; people are universal constructors.
Replicator; meme
Anything that contributes causally to its own copying; an idea that is a replicator.
Emergence
The resolution of phenomena into explicability at a higher, quasi-autonomous level.
Reductionism
The misconception that explanation always runs from lower levels to higher ones.
Jump to universality
A gradually improving system’s sudden large increase in functionality, becoming universal in some domain.
Infinite regress
An argument that depends on a sub-argument of the same form, addressing the same problem.
Measure
A way a theory gives meaning to proportions and averages over infinite sets, such as universes.
Prophecy
Purporting to know what is not yet knowable, as opposed to prediction from good explanations.
Principle of optimism
All evils are caused by insufficient knowledge.
Blind pessimism
The precautionary principle: avoiding everything not known to be safe.
Wealth
The repertoire of physical transformations one is capable of causing.
Fungible
Identical in every respect, including having no individual identity.
Multiverse; history
The world according to quantum theory; a set of fungible universes over time.
Decoherence
The process by which undoing a wave of differentiation between universes becomes infeasible.
Bad philosophy
Philosophy that actively prevents the growth of knowledge.
Popper’s criterion
Good institutions make it as easy as possible to detect that a ruler or policy is a mistake and to remove it without violence.
Elegance
The beauty in explanations, mathematical formulae and so on.
Rational / anti-rational meme
An idea that relies on the recipients’ critical faculties to get copied, versus one that relies on disabling them.
Static / dynamic society
One that changes too slowly for its members to notice, dominated by anti-rational memes, versus one dominated by rational memes.
Sustain
Either to provide what is needed or to prevent change; the two meanings are often confused.

Using it

A reading path

The chapters build on one another loosely, and the book rewards reading in order. If you want the core first:

  1. Chapters 1 and 3

    Good explanations, reach, and why people are significant. Everything later depends on these.

  2. Chapters 6 and 9

    The jump to universality, and optimism as the rational stance towards an unknowable future.

  3. Chapters 13, 15 and 16

    Politics as error-correction, static and dynamic societies, and how creativity evolved.

  4. Chapters 4, 5 and 8

    Evolution, emergence and infinity: the book’s philosophical engine room.

  5. Chapters 11 and 12

    The multiverse and the history of bad philosophy. The hardest going, and the most distinctive.

  6. Chapters 14, 17 and 18

    Beauty, sustainability and the argument with Diamond, then the book’s close.
