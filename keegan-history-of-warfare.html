“War is not the continuation of policy by other means.”

The book’s first sentence, aimed straight at Clausewitz.

A History of Warfare

John Keegan · 1993

The whole book in one breath

Keegan argues that war is older than the state, older than diplomacy and older than politics, so Clausewitz’s famous definition can’t be the whole truth. War is a cultural activity. How a people fights reflects what it values, and it can shape that society in return. Keegan tells the story through the materials men fought with: stone, flesh (the horse), iron and fire (gunpowder). Between them sit short interludes on what has always constrained war: climate and terrain, fortification, how armies are raised, and above all supply. The story ends with industrial total war and nuclear weapons, which in his view have made war between great powers self-defeating. If war is culture rather than nature, he concludes, it can change.

The author

A soldiers’ teacher who never soldiered

John Keegan (1934–2012) spent twenty-six years teaching military history to officer cadets at the Royal Military Academy Sandhurst, then became defence editor of the Daily Telegraph. A childhood illness left him unfit for military service, and his breakthrough book opens by admitting he had never been in a battle. That book, The Face of Battle (1976), asked what combat was like for the men in it at Agincourt, Waterloo and the Somme, not how generals moved arrows on maps.

A History of Warfare widens that lens to the whole human past. It is less a narrative of wars than an essay on what war is, organised around the tools, animals and institutions that made fighting possible, and the cultures that decided how it was done.

The argument

War as culture, not only policy

Carl von Clausewitz, the Prussian officer whose On War (1832) still anchors military theory, defined war as the continuation of politics by other means: organised violence used by states to achieve political ends. Keegan’s objection is that this describes one kind of war, the kind fought by the European states Clausewitz knew. It cannot account for most of the fighting humans have done.

  • War predates the state. Hunter-gatherers, tribes and chiefdoms fought for millennia before there was any “policy” to continue.
  • Warriors often fight for reasons of culture: honour, captives, ritual, revenge and status. Some peoples fought in ways that made no political sense at all.
  • Culture limits and shapes war. It decides who may fight, with what weapons and by what rules, sometimes against the plain logic of winning.
  • Clausewitz was himself a product of culture: the Prussian officer corps and the regimental world of Napoleonic Europe. His theory universalises that world.
  • Material and natural limits matter as much as ideas. Weather, terrain and food have bounded war everywhere, whatever anyone’s policy.

If war is a cultural habit rather than an iron law of politics or biology, then it can be changed, as other deep-rooted institutions have been.The hope that runs underneath the book

If you remember nothing else

Ten ideas that carry the book

Ch. 1

War is a form of culture

How a society fights expresses what it values, and war in turn reshapes that society. Politics is one of war’s drivers, not its definition.

Ch. 1

Clausewitz described one culture’s war

“Continuation of policy” fits the wars of European states from about 1800 to 1945. It fits tribal raids, steppe conquest and ritual war badly.

Ch. 1

Culture can overrule winning

The Mamluks scorned firearms and were destroyed by armies that used them. Tokugawa Japan largely set guns aside for two centuries to protect the samurai order.

Interludes i, iv

Nature draws the map of war

Climate, seasons, terrain and, above all, the ability to feed men and horses set hard limits on where and when armies could fight.

Ch. 2 · Stone

The state turned fighting into war

Early war was often raiding and ritual, bounded by custom. Cities, surplus and walls brought organised armies and war for conquest.

Ch. 3 · Flesh

The horse made the steppe a conqueror

From the chariot to the Mongols, mounted peoples of the steppe raided and ruled settled civilisations for some three thousand years.

Ch. 3–4

Two ways of war

The West sought short, bloody, face-to-face decision. The steppe fought from a distance, by evasion, delay and harassment, and closed only when victory was sure.

Interlude iii

How you raise soldiers shapes society

Warriors, mercenaries, slave soldiers, regulars, conscripts and militia each imply a different bargain between rulers, fighters and everyone else.

Ch. 4 · Iron

The Greeks invented decisive battle

Citizen hoplites locked in a phalanx fought one short, deadly clash to settle a dispute. That habit became the core of the Western way of war.

Ch. 5 · Fire

Fire made war total, then suicidal

Gunpowder, the mass army and industry turned war into the whole nation’s business. Nuclear weapons made that kind of war between great powers self-defeating.

The book, in order

Five chapters, four interludes

Keegan’s chapters follow the materials of war, from stone to fire. Between them, short interludes step back to the constants that bound every age: natural limits, walls, the raising of armies, and supply.

Chapter 1

War in Human History

The thesis: war is older than politics and bound up with culture, and Clausewitz’s definition is too narrow.

  • The chapter opens by rejecting Clausewitz’s dictum. Keegan reads On War as the work of a Prussian regimental officer who universalised the wars of his own state and era.
  • Warrior peoples such as the Cossacks fought as a way of life, for booty and standing, rather than as the instrument of anyone’s policy.
  • Four societies show culture overriding the logic of victory (see the figure below).
  • The lesson Keegan draws is that military “efficiency” is not the only force at work. Societies choose how to fight, and those choices can doom them or preserve them.
  • He also grants that modern states did bring war under political control, and that this is what made the Clausewitzian era so destructive.

Interlude i

Limitations on Warmaking

Nature, not only politics, decides where and when war can be fought.

  • Climate and weather confined most campaigning to certain seasons. Winter, rains and heat halted armies.
  • Terrain channels war. Mountains, forests, deserts and swamps block or funnel armies, and open plains and river valleys invite them. Most historic warfare happened in a limited band of temperate lands.
  • Disease and distance made the tropics and deep interiors graveyards for invaders long before enemies did.
  • These limits held for most of history. Railways, motor transport and air power loosened them only in the last two centuries.

Chapter 2 · Stone

Stone

Where war comes from, what “primitive” war looked like, and how the first states changed it.

  • Keegan surveys the explanations for war: aggression as instinct, the hunting inheritance, and anthropology’s accounts. He finds no single cause. Humans have the capacity for violence, but war as an organised activity is learned and shaped by culture.
  • Among peoples such as the Yanomamö of Amazonia and the Maring of New Guinea, fighting took the form of raids, ambushes and ceremonial battles hedged by custom. People were killed, but often within limits that prevented a decisive result.
  • The Aztecs show ritual war on an imperial scale. Their “flower wars” aimed to take captives for sacrifice rather than to kill, which left them badly matched against Spaniards fighting to destroy.
  • With farming, surplus and cities came walls (Jericho is among the earliest), organised armies, and war for land and tribute in Mesopotamia and Egypt. The weapons of the age were the bow, sling, spear and mace.
  • The state is what turned fighting from a limited, customary activity into war aimed at conquest.

Interlude ii

Fortification

Walls are as old as cities, and they shaped war as much as battles did.

  • Fortifications come in rough types: temporary refuges, permanent strongpoints such as citadels and castles, and strategic defences that guard whole frontiers, like the Roman limes, Hadrian’s Wall and China’s Great Wall.
  • Walls are expensive and static, but they protect wealth, buy time and force an attacker into slow, costly sieges.
  • Long frontier walls were less barriers than systems of watch and control, meant to deter raiders and channel trade.
  • Gunpowder artillery broke the high medieval wall and forced a new design: low, thick, angled bastions (the trace italienne), which made fortresses, and states, far more expensive.

Chapter 3 · Flesh

Flesh

The horse: how the steppe’s mounted peoples became the great conquerors of the settled world.

  • The horse was domesticated on the Eurasian steppe. Hitched to the light war chariot in the second millennium BC, it carried charioteer elites into power from Egypt and the Near East to India and China.
  • Riding, together with the composite bow, produced the horse archer: fast, far-ranging and able to strike from a distance. Scythians, Huns, Turks and Mongols raided and conquered settled civilisations for roughly three thousand years.
  • The Mongols under Genghis Khan and his successors built the largest contiguous land empire in history on mobility, discipline, intelligence and deliberate terror.
  • Steppe warfare avoided close combat until the enemy was worn down. It relied on evasion, feigned retreat and the arrow. Keegan calls this the “Oriental” tradition and contrasts it with the West’s face-to-face battle (see the figure below).
  • The steppe’s power ended only when gunpowder armies and expanding agrarian empires, Russia and Qing China above all, closed it in.

Interlude iii

Armies

There are only a few ways to get men to fight, and each implies a whole social order.

  • Every society that fights has to answer who serves, who pays and who controls the armed men. The answers fall into a handful of types (see the figure below).
  • Each type has a characteristic danger. Mercenaries change sides, slave soldiers seize the state, conscripts need a nation willing to be conscripted, and warriors resist anyone who tries to discipline them.
  • The mass conscript army born in the French Revolution looks normal to modern eyes, but across the whole of history it is the exception, not the rule.

Chapter 4 · Iron

Iron

Cheap metal, big infantry armies, and the Greek invention of decisive battle.

  • Iron was more plentiful than bronze. Once smiths mastered it, weapons and armour became cheap enough to arm large bodies of foot soldiers.
  • Assyria built the first great iron-age military state, with a standing army, siege engineering, mass deportations and calculated terror.
  • The Greeks created something new. Citizen farmers armed as hoplites fought in the close-packed phalanx, seeking one short, brutal collision that settled a quarrel in an afternoon. Keegan draws on Victor Davis Hanson’s account of this “Western way of war.”
  • Macedon joined the phalanx to heavy cavalry, and Alexander used that combination to conquer the Persian Empire.
  • Rome made war an institution: the legion, relentless discipline, engineering, roads and a professional army that held an empire for centuries.
  • After Rome, medieval Europe’s mounted knights and castles kept the face-to-face ethic alive in a new social form.

Interlude iv

Logistics and Supply

Armies march on food. For most of history the limits of supply were the limits of war.

  • A soldier can carry only a few days’ food, and pack animals eat much of what they carry. Pre-modern armies had to live off the land, which tied campaigns to harvests and kept them moving.
  • Rivers and seas were the great supply routes. Armies that could use them went farther and stayed longer.
  • States gradually built supply systems: depots and magazines, then railways in the nineteenth century, then motor transport in the twentieth.
  • Industrial logistics made the vast armies of the world wars possible. By then the “tail” of supply and support far outnumbered the fighting “teeth.”

Chapter 5 · Fire

Fire

Gunpowder, revolution and industry turn war into the business of whole nations, until the bomb.

  • Gunpowder came from China. In Europe from the fourteenth century, cannon broke medieval walls and forced the costly new bastioned fortress. Only rich, centralising states could afford the new warfare.
  • Gunpowder empires, the Ottomans, Safavids and Mughals as well as European states, used artillery and muskets to consolidate power. At sea, gun-armed sailing ships carried European power across the oceans.
  • Drill and discipline turned infantry into firing machines. Eighteenth-century armies were professional, expensive and fought for limited aims.
  • The French Revolution changed the scale. The levée en masse and Napoleon made war a national effort, which is the world Clausewitz saw and theorised.
  • Industry followed: railways, rifles, machine guns and massed artillery. The two world wars mobilised entire societies and killed tens of millions. This was Clausewitzian war taken to its extreme.
  • Nuclear weapons end the story. War between great powers can no longer serve any political purpose it was meant to achieve.

Conclusion

Is War Ending?

Major war has lost its purpose among great powers, but war as culture has not gone away.

  • After 1945 the great powers avoided direct war with each other. Keegan reads the nuclear standoff and the cost of industrial war as signs that interstate war is losing its usefulness.
  • Yet the older kinds of war persist. Civil wars, ethnic conflict and warlordism, the kind of fighting that filled the early 1990s, look more like “primitive” warfare than like Clausewitz’s.
  • He looks to disciplined professional soldiers, serving international order and peacekeeping under civilised restraint, as the protectors of civilisation against that older kind of war.
  • The final claim returns to the first: because war is a cultural institution, not a biological necessity or a political law, human beings can change how they fight, and perhaps whether they do.

Context

The arguments it started

Did he misread Clausewitz?

Clausewitz scholars say yes. Christopher Bassford’s “John Keegan and the Grand Tradition of Trashing Clausewitz” (1994) argues that Clausewitz’s Politik is broad enough to include culture and society, so the opposition Keegan draws is largely a misreading.

Was primitive war really so limited?

Lawrence Keeley’s War Before Civilization (1996) marshals archaeological and ethnographic evidence that pre-state warfare was frequent and, relative to population, often deadlier than modern war. That cuts against any picture of early fighting as mostly ritual.

Is there a “Western way of war”?

John Lynn’s Battle: A History of Combat and Culture (2003) accepts that culture shapes war but rejects a single continuous Western tradition running from the hoplites to today. The “Oriental” label has also been criticised as too sweeping.

Culture or human nature?

Azar Gat’s War in Human Civilization (2006) roots war more deeply in evolved human behaviour and treats culture as working on top of it rather than instead of it.

What’s missing

The book is overwhelmingly about land war. Sea power, which Keegan treated in The Price of Admiralty (1988), gets little space, and so does air power.

Terms of art

Glossary

Clausewitzian war
War as a rational instrument of state policy, fought by national armies. Keegan dates its heyday to roughly 1800–1945.
Primitive war
Keegan’s term for fighting in societies without states: raids, ambushes and ceremonial battles, often limited by custom.
Flower war
Aztec warfare arranged to take captives for sacrifice rather than to destroy the enemy.
Horse archer
A mounted warrior fighting with the composite bow. The core of steppe armies from the Scythians to the Mongols.
Chariot
A light, two-wheeled, horse-drawn war vehicle. It carried a new warrior elite to power across Eurasia in the second millennium BC.
Hoplite / phalanx
A Greek citizen heavy infantryman, and the dense formation hoplites fought in.
Western way of war
The tradition of seeking decisive, face-to-face battle, traced from the Greeks.
“Oriental” war
Keegan’s contested label for the steppe tradition of evasion, distance and delay.
Mamluk
A slave soldier, especially of the elite that ruled Egypt and Syria from 1250 to 1517.
Janissary
An Ottoman infantryman recruited as a boy through the levy of Christian children.
Limes
A Roman fortified frontier system of walls, roads, forts and watchtowers.
Trace italienne
The low, angled-bastion fortress designed to withstand cannon.
Levée en masse
Revolutionary France’s mass conscription of 1793, the start of the nation-in-arms.
Logistics
Moving and supplying armies: food, fodder, ammunition and transport.

Using it

A reading path

The chapters are long and the interludes short. If you won’t read it cover to cover:

  1. Chapter 1

    The thesis and the four case studies. The rest of the book depends on it.

  2. Interlude iii

    Armies: the shortest way into how war and society fit together.

  3. Chapter 3

    Flesh: the horse peoples, the book’s most original stretch.

  4. Chapter 4

    Iron: the Greeks and the birth of the Western way of war.

  5. Chapter 5 and Conclusion

    Fire: from gunpowder to the bomb, and whether war is ending.

Read alongside

  • Carl von Clausewitz, On War (1832): the book this one argues with.
  • John Keegan, The Face of Battle (1976): what combat was like at Agincourt, Waterloo and the Somme.
  • Victor Davis Hanson, The Western Way of War (1989): the hoplite argument behind Chapter 4.
  • Lawrence H. Keeley, War Before Civilization (1996): the strongest challenge to the picture of primitive war.
  • Azar Gat, War in Human Civilization (2006): the evolutionary account.
