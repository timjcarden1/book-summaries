“War is not the continuation of policy by other means.”

The book’s first sentence, aimed straight at Clausewitz.

A History of Warfare

John Keegan · 1993

The whole book in one breath

Keegan argues that war is older than the state, older than diplomacy and older than politics, so Clausewitz’s famous definition can’t be the whole truth. War is a cultural activity. How a people fights reflects what it values, and it can shape that society in return. Keegan tells the story through the materials men fought with: stone, flesh (the horse), iron and fire (gunpowder). Between them sit short interludes on what has always constrained war: climate and terrain, fortification, how armies are raised, and above all supply. The story ends with industrial total war and nuclear weapons, which in his view have made war between great powers self-defeating. If war is culture rather than nature, he concludes, it can change.

The author

A soldiers’ teacher who never soldiered

John Keegan (1934–2012) opens the book with a confession: “I was not fated to be a warrior.” A childhood illness left him lame from 1948, and at his national-service medical in 1952 the doctor who examined legs sent him home; he was classed permanently unfit for any of the armed forces. But his father had fought on the Western Front in 1917–18, and he grew up in the West of England among the British and American armies gathering for D-Day. At Oxford (Balliol, from 1953) he chose military history as his special subject, and his friends who had done their national service in Malaya, Kenya and Korea left him feeling he had missed something.

In 1960, aged twenty-five, he joined the academic staff of the Royal Military Academy Sandhurst. “I knew nothing about the army, I had never heard a shot fired in anger.” His first mess night, among Lancers, Hussars, Riflemen, Guardsmen and Highlanders in a dozen uniforms, taught him that the army was not one army but a collection of tribes, and that officers measured each other not by rank or money but by reputation “under the eyes of his regimental tribe”. “Not good with soldiers” was the ultimate condemnation. He met the same warrior values among French veterans of Algeria, German veterans of the Russian front, Indian officers proud to be Rajputs or Dogras, and Americans back from Vietnam and the Gulf.

“Soldiers are not as other men — that is the lesson that I have learned from a life cast among warriors.”Introduction

He taught at Sandhurst until 1986 and then became defence editor of the Daily Telegraph, which sent him to the Gulf in November 1990 and to Yugoslavia between the Croatian and Bosnian wars; both shadow this book. His earlier The Face of Battle (1976), which opens by admitting he had never been in a battle, asked what combat was like for the men at Agincourt, Waterloo and the Somme. He credits his Sandhurst colleague Christopher Duffy, a historian of the Habsburg and Ottoman armies, with first alerting him “to the idea that warfare is a cultural activity”, and the anthropologist Neil Whitehead with guiding him through the literature on tribal war. The book is dedicated to Winter Bridgman, an Irish relative on his mother’s side who fought for Louis XV’s France, “the type of international professional officer who features widely in what follows”. He was knighted in 2000.

A History of Warfare (1993) widens the lens to the whole human past. It is less a narrative of wars than an essay on what war is, organised around the materials men fought with and the cultures that decided how they fought. Its theme is set in the Introduction: in externals there are three warrior traditions, “ultimately, however, there is only one warrior culture”, and its evolution “is the history of warfare”.

The argument

War as culture, not only policy

Carl von Clausewitz, the Prussian officer whose On War (1832) still anchors military theory, defined war as the continuation of politics by other means. Keegan’s first sentence denies it, and the book is a sustained argument with him. The case runs in six steps.

  • War is older than the state. Hunters, tribes and chiefdoms fought for millennia before there was any “policy” to continue, and some peoples (Easter Islanders, Zulus, Mamelukes, samurai) fought in ways that made no political sense at all.
  • War is culture. It is “always an expression of culture, often a determinant of cultural forms, in some societies the culture itself”. Who may fight, with what weapons and by what rules is decided by culture, sometimes against the plain logic of winning.
  • Clausewitz universalised his own world. He was a regimental officer of Napoleonic Prussia who described what war ought to be (“true war”) and admitted that real war fell short. His theory fits European states from about 1800 to 1945, not the rest of history.
  • Nature and supply limit every culture. The interludes show that weather, terrain, walls, the raising and paying of armies, and above all food set bounds that no policy escapes.
  • There are three military cultures. The primitive, bounded by ritual; the “Oriental”, of evasion, delay and indirectness, restrained by law and custom; and the Western, which added to Greek face-to-face battle an ideology of holy war and a ceaseless appetite for new technology.
  • The Western way won, then turned on itself. It conquered almost the whole world, then produced the world wars and nuclear weapons, “the ultimate denial” that war can be a continuation of politics. Since war is a habit, not a law of nature, it can be unlearned; but disciplined armies are still needed, as the protectors of civilisation.

If you remember nothing else

Twelve ideas that carry the book

Introduction

Soldiers are not as other men

Warriors live in a very ancient world of their own, parallel to civilian life and following it at a distance. War connects with politics and economics but is not identical to them.

Ch. 1

War is a form of culture

How a society fights expresses what it values, and war can remake the society in turn. In some societies, like the Zulus, war became the culture itself.

Ch. 1

Clausewitz described one culture’s war

“Continuation of politics” fits the wars of European states from Napoleon to Hitler. It fits tribal raids, steppe conquest and ritual war badly.

Ch. 1

Culture can overrule winning

The Mamelukes refused firearms and were shot down by the Ottomans. Tokugawa Japan all but gave up guns for some 250 years to keep the samurai on top.

Interlude i

Nature draws the map of war

Seventy per cent of the land is unfit for operations, sea battles hug the coast, war follows seasons and old roads, and it has always been an entirely masculine activity.

Ch. 2 · Stone

Primitive war is ritual, not harmless

Tribal fighting is hedged by custom and ends when honour is satisfied, though raids can be murderous. States, surplus and walls brought “true war” and conquest.

Ch. 3 · Flesh

The horse made the steppe a conqueror

From the chariot to the Manchus, horse peoples struck settled civilisations for 3,000 years, fighting “to win — quickly, completely and quite unheroically”.

Interlude iii

How you raise soldiers shapes society

Warriors, mercenaries, slaves, regulars, conscripts and militia each strike a different bargain over who pays and who can be trusted. None has solved it for good.

Ch. 4 · Iron

The Greeks invented decisive battle

Farmer-hoplites settled quarrels in one short, terrible collision. That face-to-face habit became the core of the Western way of war.

Ch. 4–5

Discipline is civilisation’s instrument

Rome’s professional army, and later the drilled armies of the gunpowder age, carried order across continents. When Rome’s army went, the West lived without armies for a thousand years.

Interlude iv

Supply, then industry, decides

A soldier can carry ten days’ food. Railways lifted the limit, then industrial output ground down the armies of 1914–18, and American production won 1939–45.

Ch. 5 · Conclusion

Fire made war total, then suicidal

Gunpowder, the nation in arms and industry turned war into everyone’s business. Nuclear weapons made Clausewitz’s logic self-destroying: politics must continue, war cannot.

The book, in order

Five chapters, four interludes

Keegan’s chapters follow the materials of war, from stone to fire. Between them, shorter interludes step back to the constants that bound every age: natural limits, fortification, the raising of armies, and supply. Chapter titles and order are the book’s own.

Chapter 1

War in Human History

War is older than the state and bound up with culture. Clausewitz, a regimental officer who universalised his own world, missed that, and four societies show what he missed.

What is war?

  • The book’s first sentence rejects Clausewitz: “War is not the continuation of policy by other means.” Keegan notes that Clausewitz actually wrote that war continues “political intercourse” with “the intermixing of other means”, a subtler idea than the English tag, but still incomplete: it assumes states, state interests and rational calculation, while war “antedates the state, diplomacy and strategy by many millennia.”
  • Western culture has learned to hold two opposite types in respect, the pacifist and the “lawful bearer of arms”: in 3 Commando, one of Britain’s toughest Second World War units, the stretcher-bearers were all pacifists and were admired for their courage. Its compromise is to deplore public violence but legitimise its use under military law, and Clausewitz’s formula expressed that compromise for the states he knew.
  • It excluded a far older kind of war: the endless fighting of pre-state peoples, where every man was a warrior. Yet every European army hired such men as “irregulars” (Cossacks, “hunters”, Highlanders, Hussars) and averted its eyes from their looting and murder.
  • The Cossacks. Their name is Turkic for “freeman”; they were Christian runaways from serfdom who took to the steppe. Ivan the Terrible bartered gunpowder, lead and money for their help in 1570; by 1837 Nicholas I made his son “Ataman of All Cossacks”. Clausewitz watched them burn the outskirts of Moscow in 1812 (he thought the city’s burning an accident of their plundering) and ride down the retreating French, and wrote to his wife of “ghastly scenes”. They were cruel to the weak and ran from the brave, the reverse of a Prussian officer’s code; at Balaclava in 1854 they fled the Light Brigade and then rounded up its riderless horses to sell.
  • Yet the “civilised” war of Clausewitz’s day was butchery. At Borodino Ostermann-Tolstoi’s infantry stood under point-blank artillery for two hours; Napoleon’s surgeon Larrey performed two hundred amputations that night. When Takashima demonstrated European drill to samurai in 1841, they laughed. The Greek klephts whom Philhellenes tried to drill in 1821 fought by taunting and running away; Byron was disillusioned, and many Philhellenes came home, as William St Clair wrote, hating the Greeks.
  • Keegan’s verdict: the West’s own way of fighting, which Marshal de Saxe summed up as order, discipline and method, was just as much an expression of culture. War “is always an expression of culture, often a determinant of cultural forms, in some societies the culture itself.”

Who was Clausewitz?

  • A regimental officer, and the regiment was a revolutionary institution. Fifteenth-century Europe was plagued by unpaid mercenaries (the French called them écorcheurs, flayers); in 1445–46 Charles VII picked the best into royal compagnies d’ordonnance to suppress the rest. Handguns let infantry beat cavalry; artillery broke the barons’ castles from the 1490s; by the early 1600s their heirs were happy to be colonels of royal regiments. Colonels were proprietors, and in Britain commissions were bought until 1871.
  • Clausewitz joined the Prussian 34th Infantry Regiment, founded in 1720 and garrisoned at Neuruppin, in 1792 at the age of eleven. Its soldiers, conscripted for life from the poorest, and their families made up more than half the town. Such regiments, meant to isolate society’s disruptive elements, ended up isolating themselves from society.
  • Napoleon destroyed the Prussian army in a few weeks in 1806. Clausewitz became a prisoner, then a conspirator with Scharnhorst and Gneisenau, then in 1812 a “double patriot” who defied his king to join the tsar’s army, fought at Borodino, and came home in Russian uniform in 1813.
  • His dilemma: how to get the fighting power of revolutionary France without its politics? His answer was to persuade Prussian officers that war was a political act, so that they could fight as if politics flowed in their veins while staying politically innocent. The idea of “true” war, the absolute form, versus “real” war, diluted by friction, was already “in the air” among Prussia’s idealist philosophers.
  • Keegan compares him with Marx: both children of German idealism, both reductive (the worse, the better: “true” war, class war), both beloved of Lenin, and both writing ideology dressed as science. Clausewitz died disappointed in 1831 of cholera; his widow published On War (1832–35). It became famous forty years later when Moltke, whose lightning campaigns had just humbled Austria and France, named it with the Bible and Homer as the book that had most influenced him.
  • Prussia had forty regiments in 1831 and more than a hundred by 1871, and other nations copied it, making conscript service Europe’s rite of passage. When Clausewitz began On War in 1818 Europe was disarmed; by 1914 almost every fit man carried a card telling him where to report. There were about four million Europeans in uniform at the start of July 1914 and twenty million by the end of August.
  • In the First World War “real” and “true” war became indistinguishable and politics played no part “worth mentioning”. Keegan calls it “an extraordinary, a monstrous cultural aberration”, and Clausewitz its ideological father, as Marx was of the Russian Revolution.

War as culture: four cases

  • Easter Island, where material necessity overwhelmed religious restraint. About seventy square miles of volcanoes more than 2,000 miles from South America, settled perhaps in the third century AD. Polynesian chiefs ruled as high priests through mana and taboo, and kept the peace. From about AD 1000 the islanders, never more than about seven thousand, raised more than three hundred giant statues over seven hundred years. Then deforestation cut rainfall, crops and the timber for fishing canoes; a new obsidian spearhead, the mata’a, appeared; “the men with bloodied hands” took over; the statues were toppled; and a new cult made whoever found the first sooty tern’s egg chief for a year. When Roggeveen arrived in 1722 anarchy was far advanced; slave raids and disease cut the population to 111 by the late nineteenth century. The islanders had learned Clausewitz’s whole logic by experience (leadership, the strength of the defensive in the Poike ditch, perhaps even decisive battle) and it destroyed first their politics, then their culture, then almost their lives.
  • The Zulus, where social chaos turned a gentle pastoral people into terrible warriors. The Nguni cattle herders, who arrived in the fourteenth century, were described by shipwrecked Europeans as polite and hospitable, valuing ubuntu, humanity. Their battles were ritual, fought with thrown wooden spears before spectators, and a man who killed had to leave the field to be purified. By the late eighteenth century cattle had outgrown the sweet grazing, maize had raised the population, and the Drakensberg, the tsetse belt, the sea and the Boers hemmed them in.
  • Shaka made the new age regiments permanent, barracked away from society, forbade marriage until forty, invented a short stabbing spear, made his men run barefoot, and attacked in a formation of a strong centre and two encircling wings, disembowelling each victim before moving on. The Difaqane, the “forced migration” his wars set off, devastated some 15,000 square miles between the Tugela and Mzimkhulu rivers by 1824 and sent fugitives 2,000 miles to Lake Tanganyika. After Shaka fell in 1828 the Zulus stayed fixed in his system, acquiring guns but never adapting their tactics, until their defeat in 1879. Shaka was “a perfect Clausewitzian”, and his nation “an awful warning”.
  • The Mamelukes, where religion produced military slavery. Islam forbade Muslim to fight Muslim, and pious Muslims shunned soldiering, so from at least the ninth century (the caliph al-Mu’tasim) the caliphate bought non-Muslim Turkish boys from the steppe and raised them as Muslim horse soldiers. In Egypt they became the rulers: they beat the Mongols at Ain Jalut in 1260 and ruled until the early sixteenth century. Trained in a barracks like novices in the Koran and then the furusiyya, the art of horsemanship and mounted arms, they were never hereditary, yet never changed.
  • Faced with the Ottomans’ musketeers and Portuguese cannon, they raised gunners and musketeers from outsiders (black Africans and North Africans) but would not touch firearms themselves. They lost at Marj Dabiq and Raydaniya (August 1516 and January 1517; the book dates them a year early), 7,000 of them killed in the second. The chronicler Ibn Zabul put in their captain Kurtbay’s mouth a challenge to fight three against the whole Ottoman army if the Ottomans would stop firing a “contrivance” with which “even a woman” could kill men. In 1497 they had slaughtered a boy sultan’s black musketeers. They survived under the Ottomans, rode against Napoleon’s guns at the Pyramids in 1798, and were massacred by Muhammad Ali in 1811.
  • Moltke, sent to modernise the Turkish army in 1835, found soldiers who would not salute a Christian and watched Muhammad Ali’s Europeanised army rout the Ottomans at Nezib in 1839. Turkey finally built a modern army of ethnic Turks only, which undermined its hold on its other peoples; in 1918 the empire fell, as Germany’s did, through a deliberate choice of war.
  • The samurai, where a better weapon was outlawed to preserve the social order. The two-sworded warrior class, which repelled the Mongols in 1274 and 1281 (a typhoon destroyed the second fleet), cultivated poetry and Zen as well as swordsmanship. The Portuguese brought guns in 1542. Oda Nobunaga drilled musketeers in ranks of up to a thousand and swept away his enemies at Nagashino in 1575; at Uedahara in 1548 the side with guns had never got to fire them because the other charged the moment the rituals ended.
  • Then Japan gave up the gun. Hideyoshi’s sword hunt of 1587 disarmed all non-samurai, supposedly to cast a giant Buddha. From 1607 Tokugawa Ieyasu concentrated gunmaking at Nagahama under a Commissioner for Guns who approved only government orders; by 1706 production was 35 large matchlocks in even years and 250 small ones in odd years, for a warrior class of about half a million. Guns were linked with Christianity and invasion, especially after the Christian Shimabara rebellion of 1637; they threatened the samurai’s social place (Cervantes’s Don Quixote complains of the same); and isolated, united Japan could afford the choice. It lasted until Perry’s “black ships” in the 1850s. The book calls war here “the perpetuation of a culture by its own means.”

A culture without war

  • Clausewitz had no guide to culture: comparative history hardly existed, and the Enlightenment, speaking through Voltaire, sneered at events “on the banks of the Oxus”. But the Oxus is to war what Westminster is to parliaments. There the horse was tamed and ridden, chariot empires were launched, the cavalry revolution happened, and Huns, Avars, Magyars, Turks and Mongols rode west; Tamerlane began at Samarkand.
  • After 1918 Liddell Hart blamed Clausewitz for the slaughter; after 1945 nuclear strategists made him a prophet. Deterrence needs “true” and “real” war to be identical: Robert McNamara said at the University of Michigan in 1962 that NATO could destroy “an enemy society if driven to it.” Keegan finds the logic mechanical, and schizophrenic for democratic citizens asked to live by liberal values and a warrior code at once.
  • From the seventeenth century states west of the Oder and Drava had demilitarised their societies, disarming the population and absorbing the warrior class. The French Revolution’s mass armies remilitarised them from above, at the cost of 20 million dead in the First World War and 50 million in the Second; Britain and America dropped conscription after 1945, and resistance by America’s conscripts ended the Vietnam war.
  • Remilitarisation from below was worse. Mao’s guerrilla method (1929) and theory of “protracted war” echoed the horse peoples. Of about 80,000 who set out on the Long March in 1934–35, some 8,000 survived; about a million “landlords” were killed after the Communist victory. Tito deliberately spread the war to force Yugoslavs to take sides (his deputy Kardelj: “Terror will bring about armed action”): the Partisans killed at least 100,000 opponents at the war’s end, the Croat regime 350,000 Serbs, and 1.6 million Yugoslavs died in all. Keegan contrasts William Deakin, a British liaison officer who walked away rather than question Chetnik prisoners about to be shot, with Milovan Djilas, who admitted cutting a helpless German prisoner’s throat.
  • In Algeria (1954–62) the victorious FLN killed at least 30,000 and perhaps 150,000 of the Muslims who had sided with France, having lost 141,000 in combat and killed 12,000 of its own in purges; the Algerian government puts the war’s cost at a million out of nine million Muslims.
  • So is war losing its usefulness? Nuclear weapons have killed no one since 9 August 1945, while about 50 million have died since, mostly from cheap, mass-produced small arms. Television has turned opinion against war; enthusiasm for the Gulf War evaporated within days of the images of carnage. Russell Weigley sees an old frustration with war’s “chronic indecisiveness”; John Mueller points out that slavery was substantially abolished between 1788 and 1888, and that human sacrifice, infanticide and duelling also died out. The Balkan and Caucasian wars are the old “territorial displacement” of primitive war, but the new, humanitarian impulse to stop them is, Keegan thinks, the most hopeful outcome of “our terrible century”.

Interlude 1

Limitations on Warmaking

Nature, not only law or morality, has always limited war: weather, season, terrain and supply, the sea’s vastness, and the fact that war is waged only by men.

Permanent and contingent factors

  • The Soviet General Staff spoke of “permanently operating factors”: weather, climate, seasons, terrain, vegetation. Keegan adds “contingent” ones: supply, quartering and equipment. Wealth and technology have reduced some but eliminated none. How to feed, shelter and move an army remain a commander’s first problems.

War at sea hugs the land

  • Fighting on water has always cost more than on land: the oldest boat found dates only to 6315 BC, and warships need capital and specialist crews. The earliest picture of a sea fight, Rameses III against the Sea Peoples in the Nile delta in 1186 BC, shows a sailing ship.
  • Before guns, sail was useless for fighting at close range, so the oared galley ruled the Mediterranean from the second millennium BC to the gun. But it was a summer weapon that could not keep the sea in bad weather, and its long, narrow hull had no room to feed its rowers for more than a few days. As John Guilmartin showed, galley fleets were partners of armies moving along the coast. Every great Mediterranean sea battle, from Salamis (480 BC) to Lepanto (1571), was fought within sight of land. The Vikings were the exception, marauding far from base in a time of weak states, and they sailed more than they rowed.
  • Sailing warships could stay at sea for months (the Portuguese beat an Indian ruler’s fleet in 1502; Blake campaigned in the Mediterranean in the 1650s), yet they still fought near coasts: calmer water, the objects of war (ports, coastal trade, invasion) lie there, and fleets steering by sight could barely find each other. A line of frigates could see about twenty miles; Nelson missed the French before the Nile in 1798. Of Nelson’s three great victories, two (the Nile and Copenhagen) were against fleets at anchor and Trafalgar was twenty-five miles off Spain.
  • Steam made things worse. Coal-burners were tied to coaling stations: HMS Dreadnought (1906) emptied its bunkers in five days at twenty knots, and Russia’s Baltic fleet sailed to Japan in 1904–05 with coal piled so high on deck that it could not have fought between stops. Every sea battle of the First World War was within a hundred miles of land, and most of the Second’s too, radar and carriers notwithstanding.
  • Keegan offers his own “fifteen decisive sea battles” on the model of Creasy’s land battles (see the figure). Camperdown, Copenhagen and Jutland lie within 300 miles of one another; Salamis, Lepanto and Navarino, 2,300 years apart, scarcely more than a hundred miles apart. Only two, Midway and the convoy battles of March 1943, were fought in open ocean far from land.

Most of the land has no military history

  • About seventy per cent of the world’s 60 million square miles of dry land is too high, too cold or too dry for military operations. Rommel and Montgomery clung to the North African coast; Japan took Malaya in 1941–42 along its roads and by amphibious hooks down its coast; China’s soldiers in the 1962 border war had spent a year acclimatising on the Tibetan plateau while India’s, fresh from the plains, fell sick with altitude. The Antarctic has been demilitarised by treaty since 1959; the most northerly land fighting ever was over weather stations in Greenland and Spitsbergen in 1940–43, where the two sides sometimes had to help each other survive.
  • Battles recur in the same places: the “cockpit of Europe” in Belgium, the Quadrilateral in northern Italy, and above all Adrianople (Edirne), where fifteen battles or sieges are recorded between AD 323 and 1913, because three river valleys meet there on the only plain behind Constantinople, the land bridge from Asia into Europe (see the figure).
  • Even apparently free movement follows old grooves. The German armoured drive through France in 1940 ran along Route Nationale 43, which follows the Roman road laid out after Caesar’s conquest. In Russia the 40,000 square miles of the Pripet marshes became the “Wehrmacht hole” where no German unit of worth could operate. The rasputitsa, the twice-yearly thaw and rains that turn the steppe to mud for a month, delayed the invasion in spring 1941 and bogged the tanks short of Moscow that autumn; in March 1943 General Golikov told a subordinate: “There are 200–230 miles to the Dnieper and to the spring rasputitsa there are 30–35 days. Draw your own conclusions.”
  • Organised war has been concentrated in a band between about 10° and 55° north, from the Mississippi valley to the Philippines, largely coinciding with four vegetation zones (mixed forest, broadleaf forest, Mediterranean scrub and dry tropical forest), and seasonally after the harvest: of the first three battles of Adrianople two were in July and one in August.

From poor lands to rich

  • Is war just a quarrel between farmers over the “lands of first choice”? Partly: serious war needs wealth, and farming was for millennia the main source of it. Victor Davis Hanson argues that Greek smallholders invented decisive battle. But farmers are rooted and reluctant soldiers (Marx thought peasants “irredeemable”; Mao thought otherwise).
  • The frontiers between ploughland and wasteland are marked by long walls: the Antonine Wall, the Roman limes in Germany, the fossatum Africae, the Roman desert frontier in Syria, Russia’s cherta lines 2,000 miles from the Caspian to the Altai, the Habsburg Military Frontier in Croatia, and the Great Wall of China.
  • So war tends to flow from poor lands to rich, rarely the other way. Poor peoples from what William McNeill calls “food-deficit areas” sold their fighting skills to the rich (hence hussars, uhlans, jägers, bearskins and kilts), but only when they broke into rich land could they stock up for conquest. Motive was not only plunder: the Arabs fought to extend the House of Submission, Alexander pillaged Persia “largely for the pleasure of it”, and the Mongols and Tamerlane showed almost no ability to keep what they took.
  • “War is always limited, not because man chooses to make it so, but because nature determines that it shall be.”

An entirely masculine activity

  • Women can cause war, incite it (Lady Macbeth), send sons to it and even lead it messianically. They have followed armies, nursed, farmed, dug trenches and made munitions. But with the most insignificant exceptions they have never fought: “If warfare is as old as history and as universal as mankind, we must now enter the supremely important limitation that it is an entirely masculine activity.”

Chapter 2 · Stone

Stone

Why men fight, what science and anthropology can and cannot say, how four peoples with stone-age weapons actually fought, and how farming, walls and the first cities turned fighting into war.

Why do men fight?

  • Social scientists split between “materialists”, who think violence is triggered by circumstances, and “naturalists”, who divide in turn between those who think man is violent by nature and those who see violence as an aberration. In May 1986 a meeting at Seville issued the Seville Statement, five articles each beginning “It is scientifically incorrect”, denying that war is inherited from animals, genetically programmed, selected for, rooted in a “violent brain” or caused by instinct; the American Anthropological Association adopted it. Keegan says the layman can’t easily take sides, but the stakes are high: if Seville is wrong, so is the hope of ending war by improving material conditions.
  • Neurology locates aggression in the limbic system (hypothalamus, septum, amygdala), under the control of the frontal lobes, with serotonin and testosterone involved; but context and risk moderate it, and low testosterone doesn’t mean cowardice (witness the Byzantine eunuch general Narses). Genetics shows aggression can be bred in animals, and the XYY pattern (about one man in a thousand) yields slightly more violent criminals. Both sides “pitch their case too strong”: science can’t predict when a person will be violent, and doesn’t explain why groups fight groups.
  • Psychology and ethology. Freud, after the First World War, told Einstein in Why War? that man has “a lust for hatred and destruction”; Totem and Taboo (1913) imagined sons killing the patriarch. Konrad Lorenz saw aggression as a drive that weapons freed from the instinct to accept submission; Robert Ardrey made it the hunting group’s defence of territory; Lionel Tiger and Robin Fox derived male leadership from the all-male hunting band.
  • Anthropology looked away. The “nurture” school, from Francis Galton’s opening of the nature-nurture debate in 1874, focused on kinship and the origins of political institutions; Franz Boas’s cultural determinism, Ruth Benedict’s Patterns of Culture (1934) and Margaret Mead’s Coming of Age in Samoa (from a 1925 visit) found peace; Malinowski’s structural functionalism and Lévi-Strauss’s exchange of women explained stability. Even Evans-Pritchard, who led tribesmen against the Italians in Ethiopia in 1941, did not study war.
  • Harry Turney-High’s Primitive Warfare (1949), written by an anthropologist who had served in the last horsed cavalry, insulted his colleagues (a sergeant in a second-rate army, he wrote, understood war better) and was ignored. His point: most societies they studied lived “below the military horizon”; only the move to “true” war, marked by “the rise of the army with officers”, allowed a state to form. Only five articles on the anthropology of war appeared in the 1950s. Mead replied in 1964 that “warfare is only an invention”, but in 1967 the American Anthropological Association accepted Turney-High’s distinction, and fieldworkers began asking his question: “How does this group fight?”

Four peoples and their warfare

  • The Yanomamö, some 10,000 slash-and-burn farmers in 40,000 square miles of forest on the upper Orinoco, studied by Napoleon Chagnon for sixteen months in 1964. Villages of 40–250 kin, a code of ferocity (waiteri), women brutally treated and the main prize. Violence escalates by grades: chest-pounding duels at feasts, blow for blow, after which rivals may swear friendship; club fights over adultery, stopped by the headman with his bow; the rare spear fight; the raid, lying in wait to kill someone bathing or fetching water; and the “treacherous feast”. War took 24% of recent male deaths in three groups Chagnon studied. Keegan reverses his ranking: the raids look like murder, the graded duels like controlled ritual that keeps most alive “to fight another day”.
  • The Maring of the New Guinea highlands, about 7,000 people in 190 square miles, studied by Andrew Vayda in 1962–66. Their fights ran through recognised stages: “nothing” fights with bows from behind man-high shields at a border fight ground, where casualties were rare and mediators shouted for peace; “true” fights at close range with axes and spears, with days off for rain or repainting shields; and then raids or “routs”, which killed women and children and drove the losers from their land. Wars came about every ten years, when enough pigs had been fattened to thank the ancestors with one per clan member. Victors rarely took the land for fear of lingering magic, and population had been falling during the last wars, so Keegan suspects they fought partly out of habit, even “for the fun of it”, and admires the system’s sophistication.
  • The Maoris, Polynesians who reached New Zealand perhaps around AD 800 and exterminated some eighteen bird species, including the moa. Their chiefs’ mana and taboo let them command labour and war. War was always revenge: routs in which a fast runner would spear ten or a dozen fleeing men for others to finish; enemies were eaten and their heads mounted on palisades. But at least 4,000 hill forts, built by forty tribes of 100,000–300,000 people, with food stores and no siege engines to breach them, kept wars limited; only the musket changed that.
  • The Aztecs, who rose from mercenaries of the Tepanec to masters of the valley of Mexico between the thirteenth and sixteenth centuries. Their armies marched in commands of 8,000 at twelve miles a day with eight days’ rations. But, following Inga Clendinnen, Keegan shows war as sacrament: the gods needed human blood, and war supplied it. Their best weapon, a wooden sword edged with obsidian, was designed to wound, not kill; a warrior aimed at the legs to take a captive; giving a captive to a comrade was punishable by death; the senior warriors had taken seven captives each.
  • After a revolt of the Huaxtecs perhaps 20,000 captives were sacrificed at a new temple. In the prearranged “flower battles” against fellow Nahuatl speakers, elite captives were taken for the Feast of the Flaying of Men: tethered on a stone and armed with a feather-edged sword, the captive was cut slowly by four champions, “the striping”, before his heart was torn out; his captor, who had called him “beloved son”, then wore his flayed skin. Keegan adds the caveat that this was the ritual of a rich, secure empire; while rising, the Aztecs probably slaughtered like everyone else. The Maya sacrificed only noble captives and enslaved the rest.
  • What the four share: close-range encounters with weapons of little penetrating power, no armour, heavy ritual, and aims (revenge, insult, divine need) remote from what moderns call the causes and results of war. All lie below Turney-High’s military horizon.

The beginnings of warfare

  • J. M. Roberts’s scale: if Christ was born twenty minutes ago, the Sumerians appeared forty minutes before that, modern humans reached Europe five or six hours earlier, and creatures with some manlike features two or three weeks ago. Writing, and so history, began in Sumer about 3100 BC.
  • Australopithecus made the first tool, a chipped pebble; Homo erectus built huts and the first wooden spear. Hunting big game was dangerous: a young man at Arene Candide in Italy was mauled by a bear at least 10,000 years ago; a flint point was found in a bear skull at Trieste from 100,000 years ago; a yew spear in an elephant’s ribs in Schleswig-Holstein; a spear wound in a Neanderthal pelvis in Palestine (possibly an accident of the hunt, Keegan notes). Cave art from about 35,000 years ago shows mostly peaceful human figures.
  • About 10,000 years ago came “a revolution in weapons technology”: the bow, the sling, the dagger and the mace. The bow, “the first machine”, let man kill at a distance; Keegan suspects it answered the need to bring down game that roamed more widely as the ice retreated. Arthur Ferrill reads battle tactics into Spanish Levant cave paintings; Keegan thinks they show Maring-style displays.
  • Surviving hunters (the San, the Semai) seem peaceful, but they are refugees pushed to the margins. At Site 117 at Jebel Sahaba on the Nile, fifty-nine skeletons, men and women, lay with 110 stone points in positions showing they had entered the bodies, one young woman with twenty-one. Ferrill calls it the first extensive evidence of war; Keegan thinks it may be a Maring-style rout, or a clash between hunters and early farmers. (The mass grave of Visby in 1361, 2,000 bodies with shins cut after they fell, shows the same “overkill”.)
  • Farming made for war. Shepherding appears in northern Iraq by 9000 BC. By 7000 BC Jericho was an eight-acre town of 2,000–3,000 people behind a stone wall ten feet thick and thirteen high, about 700 yards round, with a rock-cut ditch thirty feet wide and a tower fifteen feet above the wall: curtain wall, keep and moat, the elements of fortification until gunpowder. Çatal Hüyük, larger, presented only the blank outer walls of its houses. Jericho’s discovery in 1952–58 showed that war preceded the first empires.
  • Yet for millennia the world was empty. Population rose from 5–10 million in 10,000 BC to perhaps 100 million by 3000 BC; farming advanced across Europe about a mile a year and reached Britain around 4000 BC; Rhineland villages never passed 300–400 people. With land free and yields low, there was little worth robbing, except in one place: the rich, open alluvial plain of Sumer.

War and civilisation

  • Mesopotamia falls only 112 feet in 210 miles, has no trees and a flood to be tamed, so irrigation had to be planned and maintained collectively. Priest-kings organised it, temples collected and recorded the surplus, and the records became writing. Harvests of two hundred grains for each one sown paid for temples, trade and luxuries (gold from the Indus, lapis lazuli from Afghanistan, silver from Turkey). None of the thirteen early cities, Ur, Uruk and Kish among them, had walls at first.
  • Egypt was different. United under Menes about 3100 BC and protected by desert on both sides, it fought in an old-fashioned, perhaps ceremonial way: clubs and flint spears even in the Middle Kingdom (1991–1785 BC), soldiers bare-chested and unarmoured, and the same image of a pharaoh raising a mace over a cringing captive from Narmer (about 3000 BC) to Rameses II nearly 2,000 years later. Keegan wonders if Egyptians were spared real war for fourteen centuries, until invaders came; the mummy of Seqenenre the Brave, from about 1540 BC, bears a terrible head wound.
  • Sumer was not spared. War leaders replaced priest-kings and metallurgy accelerated. Uruk had walls more than five miles round by the time of Gilgamesh. The Vulture Stele shows Eannatum of Lagash’s helmeted soldiers in a six-man-deep column; the Standard of Ur shows wheeled war carts. Copper gave way to bronze (copper plus scarce tin) and the socket axe. Sargon of Akkad (reigned about 2340–2284 BC) fought thirty-four wars, perhaps with 5,400 soldiers, reached Syria and southern Turkey, and styled himself “He Who Keeps Travelling the Four Lands”; his grandson Naram-Sin called himself “King of the Four Quarters”.
  • By about 2000 BC Mesopotamia was ringed by satellite peoples (Gutians, Hurrians, Kassites) who had bronze, armour, the bow (perhaps already the composite bow), siegecraft, the rudiments of logistics, and horses bred on the steppe. When those horses were harnessed to a lightened two-wheeled cart, the chariot, the settled civilisations were at the mercy of the horse lands.

Interlude 2

Fortification

Refuges, strongholds and strategic defences: Jericho got the principles right, siegecraft rarely beat a well-stocked wall before gunpowder, and walls are only as strong as the power behind them.

Three forms of fortification

  • A refuge gives short-term safety against raiders who can’t linger, like the hilltop villages of Provence against Muslim sea raiders, or Maring palisades and Maori pa. A stronghold is a base for active defence that must hold water, stores and a garrison, withstand an enemy with siege engines and supplies, and provide fighting platforms and gates for counter-attack; builders must judge between building too small and too big for the men available, a dilemma that dogged the Crusaders. Strategic defences (continuous walls or chains of mutually supporting forts) are the costliest and a sign of a rich, advanced state.
  • Before gunpowder every attack had to be made at close range: escalade by ladder, or “deliberate siege” by mining, ram, projectiles and siege towers. Projectiles rarely paid: a stout wall absorbs them, and they arrive at a poor angle. Gunpowder’s advantage was its flat trajectory, which could strike a wall at its foundations.
  • Jericho already had the answer to every threat: a dry moat as killing ground, walls three times a man’s height, and a tower. In the 8,000 years to gunpowder, engineers added only refinements: multiple walls, keeps, towers projecting for flanking fire, outworks. They improved on Jericho no more than later printers improved on Gutenberg’s Bible.

Strongholds and frontiers

  • Strongholds multiply where central power is weak or being imposed: Greek colonies on the Turkish and Sicilian coasts; perhaps 900 Norman castles in England between 1066 and 1154, costing from a thousand to 24,000 man-days each; the Roman forts of the Saxon Shore.
  • The first designed strategic system was the Twelfth Dynasty’s Nubian forts from 1991 BC, eventually 250 miles along the Nile. At Semna in 1820 BC Senusret III inscribed that any son who held his boundary was his son and any who abandoned it was not. Three forts controlled the river from both banks, with tunnels to draw water, granaries for several hundred men for a year, and a supply depot on the island of Askut. Their orders: let no Nubian pass north except to trade or with a message. A patrol of desert Medjay reported: “We have found a track of 32 men and 3 donkeys.” British officers on India’s North-West Frontier, with their Khyber Rifles and Tochi Scouts recruited from the tribes, would have recognised the system.
  • Fortified settlements spread widely only in the first millennium BC: Old Smyrna in the ninth century, Saragossa and Biskupin in Poland in the sixth, some 2,000 Iron Age hill forts in Britain, and Greek and Phoenician trading ports. Stuart Piggott traced a trade from the Mediterranean ports to the inland hill forts: wine, silk and ivory north (a Barbary ape reached an Ulster king), amber, furs and slaves south. Shang China (about 1500–1000 BC) walled its towns in beaten earth; its character for a city shows an enclosure and a man kneeling in submission.
  • Great states defend at the edge. Alexander fought at least twenty sieges between 335 and 325 BC, none inside the Persian empire, whose battles were fought in the open. Rome besieged its way from Agrigentum (262 BC) to Alesia (52 BC), and its square marching camps became London, Cologne and Vienna; but inside the empire Gallic cities were left unwalled, the pax Romana.
  • Historians argue over what Rome’s frontiers (Hadrian’s Wall, the Antonine Wall, the Rhine and Danube limes, the fossatum Africae, the limes Syriae) were for. Edward Luttwak sees a grand strategy; Benjamin Isaac sees supply lines for aggression against Persia; C. R. Whittaker sees policing, like France’s Morice Line in Algeria.
  • Walls can be defensive (the Habsburg krajina against the Turks), neurotic (seventy-six forts built or begun by 1867 to guard Britain’s ports against a phantom French threat), or aggressive (Louis XIV’s fortresses; Russia’s cherta, which also brought the Cossacks under control). Owen Lattimore argued that China’s Great Wall, nearly 4,000 miles in all its branches, chased a chimera: no line divides farmland from steppe, because the mixed zone between them shifts with the climate. Settlers planted beyond it turned nomad in dry spells. The Wall was also a “diaphragm” through which Chinese ideas civilised the nomads. The Ming rebuilt 500 city walls.
  • Rome was less lucky: its barbarians were too many and too little Romanised. By the fifth century only forty-eight Gallic cities were walled, twelve in Spain, and in Italy south of the Po only Rome. Once the frontier fell, the west lay open.
  • When trade revived (Europe’s population rose from about 40 to about 60 million between 1100 and 1300), towns walled themselves to claim freedoms: Pisa dug its ditch in two months in 1155 in defiance of Barbarossa. Local strongmen built castles, many unlicensed (“adulterine”): Poitou had three before the Vikings and thirty-nine by the eleventh century; Maine none before the tenth century and sixty-two by 1100. A hundred men could raise a small motte in ten days; reducing one was far harder.

Siegecraft before gunpowder

  • Every siege technique before gunpowder was invented between about 2400 and 397 BC (see the figure). None except starvation worked reliably. Polybius advised surprise; treachery took Antioch for the Crusaders in 1098; an unguarded latrine chute took Château-Gaillard in 1204. At Rochester in 1215 King John’s miners brought down a corner of the keep by burning pit props with the fat of forty bacon pigs, but the garrison fell only when food ran out after fifty days. Jerusalem’s fall to a siege tower in 1099 was exceptional.
  • So medieval sieges often ran to an agreed deadline: if no relief came, the garrison marched out free. Keegan warns against reading too much into Egyptian and Assyrian siege reliefs, which are the ancient equivalent of David’s portraits of Napoleon.
  • The lesson: strategic defences depend on the will and resources of the power behind them. “They labour in vain who build” walls expected to stand by themselves.

Chapter 3 · Flesh

Flesh

The horse: first the chariot kings, then 2,000 years of mounted peoples from the steppe, from the Scythians and Huns to the Mongols, whose ruthlessness changed how settled peoples fought.

The charioteers

  • Within about 300 years the chariot carried conquerors into every centre of civilisation: the Semitic Hyksos into Egypt from about 1700 BC; mountain peoples (Kassites and Hurrians) over Hammurabi’s Mesopotamia by about 1525 BC; Indo-European Aryans into the Indus valley, whose civilisation they destroyed; and about 1400 BC the founders of the Shang dynasty into northern China.
  • The horse was first eaten, not ridden: at Srednij Stog on the Dnieper, horse bones dominate fourth-millennium sites. Man learned traction with the ox and the sledge, then the wheel; the Standard of Ur shows a four-wheeled cart drawn by onagers, stubborn animals that can’t be ridden from the shoulders or bred bigger. Equus caballus (64 chromosomes) could be, but harnessing it was hard: a neck band choked it, and the breast band and padded collar came much later.
  • The answer was to make the vehicle as light as possible. Stuart Piggott: the chariot, a structure “largely in tension” with bent-wood wheels, raised the speed of land transport about tenfold, from 2 miles an hour by ox to 20; a reconstruction with harness weighed about 75 pounds. (Samuel Johnson thought the human frame could not stand more than twenty-five miles an hour.)
  • It was probably perfected on the border of steppe and civilisation, in northern Iran, where barbarian herders met Mesopotamian metalworkers (William McNeill’s view), not long before 1700 BC.
  • The composite bow went with it: a wooden core with horn glued on the belly and sinew on the back, cured for a year or more, strung against its natural curve, with a draw of up to 150 pounds. Short enough to shoot from a chariot or saddle, it could hit accurately at 300 yards and pierce armour at a hundred, and a warrior carried up to fifty light arrows. The kit (bow, arrows, thumb ring, quiver, bow case) did not change for 3,000 years; the same objects are Ottoman regalia in the Topkapi.
  • Why were pastoralists the aggressors? Because herding teaches killing. Farmers keep animals alive; herders cull, butcher and castrate as routine, and they know how to split a flock, circle a flank, isolate leaders and kill a few while the mass stays inert. Nomads are also always ready to move, living off their herds, tough beyond any farmer. Owen Lattimore’s account of crossing 1,700 miles of Central Asia with caravan men in 1926–27, on bitter salt water, shows how hard that life was.
  • Chariot and composite bow may have begun as tools for herding and hunting wolves. Against unarmoured infantry they were devastating: a chariot crew circling at 100–200 yards might transfix six men a minute, so ten chariots in ten minutes could cause 500 casualties or more, “a Battle of the Somme-like toll” for armies of the day. Defeated hosts became slaves, and the chariot rulers were slave-masters: in India, the Rig-Veda suggests, slavery became the basis of caste.
  • Chariots could be carried on one man’s shoulders (an Egyptian relief of about 1170 BC) and were probably traded like modern arms; the limit was trained horses, and Mesopotamian texts of the thirteenth and twelfth centuries BC show a whole vocabulary of schooling.
  • The chariot rulers were destroyers, not creators, and they adopted their subjects’ cultures. Their rule was short: Amosis expelled the Hyksos about 1567 BC; Phrygians and Dorians toppled the Hittites and Mycenaeans about 1200 BC; Ashuruballit restored Assyria about 1365 BC; the Chou overthrew the Shang in 1050–25 BC. Only the Aryans lasted. But their lasting legacy was “the warmaking state”: kings everywhere became charioteers.

The chariot and Assyria

  • By the eighth century BC Assyria had the first true long-range army: supply depots, transport columns and bridging trains, able to campaign 300 miles from base, horsed elements moving thirty miles a day. Its central depot at Nineveh (the ekal masharti) held chariots, horses and stores; Esarhaddon complained it had grown too small for chariot exercises.
  • Engineers cut roads with bronze picks in Urartu (714 BC), where Sargon II’s men “turned mutinous”; Ashurnasirpal crossed the Euphrates on skin boats carried with the army; Sennacherib had Syrian shipwrights build sea-going ships at Nineveh, sailed by Phoenicians down the Tigris and across to the Persian Gulf. Armies lived off enemy land and then wasted it: Sargon broke irrigation works and cut down fruit trees.
  • Assyria was the first to recruit without ethnic discrimination, deporting dissidents (as the Ottomans and Stalin later did) while enlisting subjects and prisoners with their own weapons. Sennacherib boasted of taking forty-six of Hezekiah’s walled towns with ramps, rams and mines and shutting him up in Jerusalem “like a caged bird” (2 Kings 18); Hezekiah paid tribute.
  • Chariot war had a chivalrous streak. Rusa of Urartu challenged Sargon to battle. In China the Duke of Sung refused in 638 BC to attack the Ch’u before they had formed ranks (“The gentleman does not inflict a second wound, or take the grey-haired prisoner”) and lost; a duke’s son, missed by an archer, demanded his turn to shoot, got it and killed him. Battlefields were levelled for chariots: Darius built three runways at Gaugamela (331 BC).
  • The first battle of history, Megiddo (1469 BC by the book’s dating), was almost bloodless: Tuthmosis III took the most difficult of three routes through the mountains, surprised the coalition, which fled into the city, and besieged it for seven months. Only eighty-three enemies were killed and 340 captured; the main booty was 2,041 horses. At Qadesh (the book dates it 1294 BC; it is usually put at about 1274) Rameses II met a Hittite army credited, implausibly, with 2,500 chariots.

The warhorse

  • Egyptian art shows riding by 1350 BC, but bareback from the rump. By the eighth century BC breeding let Assyrians ride from the forward seat; one relief shows cavalrymen in pairs, one shooting while the other holds both reins, “charioteering without the chariot”, as McNeill put it.
  • Out on the steppe the Scythians, perhaps from the Altai, learned to shoot from the saddle, following the Cimmerians who raided Asia Minor about 690 BC. In 612 BC Scythians, Medes and Babylonians took Nineveh; Assyria fell by 605. For the next two thousand years the horse peoples raided, enslaved and sometimes conquered along the whole edge of civilisation.

The horse peoples of the steppe

  • North lies the taiga (the ground near Yakutsk is frozen 446 feet down); south, the deserts (the Gobi 1,200 miles across, the Takla-makan, the Dasht-i-Kavir); between them the steppe proper, a grass belt about 3,000 miles long and 500 deep. Grazing improves westward, drawing migration toward Europe, but the Dzungarian gap south of the Altai gives the easiest gateway into China.
  • Why did they come out? Not ritual or territory, since they fought to win and their tribes were fluid. McNeill’s “carrying capacity” (good years swelling herds and families, then bad years forcing them out) helps, as does the wish to force trade (the Huns demanded their Danube market back). But Keegan’s simpler answer: nomads, tough, mobile, used to shedding blood and untroubled by religious scruple, learned that war paid. They wanted the comforts of civilisation and the freedom of the saddle; the Ottoman sultans lived in the Topkapi like nomads in pavilions, with horsetail standards and stables at the door.

The Huns

  • Perhaps the Hsiung-nu who troubled Han China, the Huns spoke a Turkic language, had no writing, read omens in sheep’s shoulder blades, and sold slaves in huge numbers; in 440–50 the eastern empire paid them 13,000 pounds of gold, about six tons, for peace.
  • In 371 they beat the Alans on the Don; in 376 they drove the Goths into the empire, and on 9 August 378 the Goths killed the emperor Valens at Adrianople. The lasting damage was Theodosius’s settlement of 382, which let the Goths live inside the empire as armed “federates” under their own leaders. Alaric invaded Italy in 401 and sacked Rome in 410; Radagaisus’s huge Germanic host crossed the Alps in 405, driven by Huns; Aetius held Gaul with Hunnish mercenaries.
  • Attila appeared before Constantinople in 447, besieged Orléans in 451 (horse peoples could not take cities before the Mongols) and fought Aetius’s coalition of Franks, Visigoths, Burgundians and Alans at Châlons in June 451, retreating behind his wagon laager. In 452 he entered Italy, where refugees fled to the islands that became Venice, then turned back; within two years he was dead and his empire collapsed.
  • Why? Not lack of grazing: the Hungarian plain is estimated to support 150,000 horses, and Hungary mounted 29,000 cavalry in 1914. More likely he rode his horses to death with no way to replace them. In the Boer War the British lost 347,000 of 518,000 horses, only about 2% in battle, at 336 a day (see the figure).

The horse peoples’ horizon, 453–1258

  • Attila had shifted his effort 500 miles between campaigning seasons, something no army on foot could do: the heart of the “cavalry revolution”. The horse peoples fought “true war” by every test (unlimited force, outright victory) yet without any political object; war won them the wealth to stay exactly as they were. Genghis Khan said man’s greatest good fortune was to defeat his enemy, seize his possessions and “leave his married women weeping and wailing”.
  • After the Huns came the Ephthalites (“White Huns”, beaten by Persia in 567), the Bulgars and the Avars, who besieged Constantinople with Persian help in 626 and were destroyed by Charlemagne; then the Magyars. The Turks, once ironsmiths to the Juan-Juan, took their lands and the title khan, reached the Oxus by 563, and exchanged embassies with Byzantium in 568, before quarrelling among themselves.
  • The Arabs were not horse people but became their employers, and they added “the force of an idea”. Islam divided the world into the House of Submission and the House of War, saw no conflict between piety and wealth, and dissolved kinship and territory as reasons to fight. Against a Byzantium and Persia exhausted by their war of 603–28, they won at Qadisiyah (637), took Syria (636), Egypt (642), North Africa (705) and Spain (711), besieged Constantinople (674–77 and 717), and beat the Chinese at the Talas River in 751. Their tactics were primitive (approach on camels, fight from behind obstacles with a desert escape route; perhaps only 600 horses at Qadisiyah), which Keegan says only faith can explain.
  • Forbidden to fight fellow Muslims and short of willing soldiers, the Abbasid caliphs bought slave soldiers: al-Mu’tasim (833–42) is said to have had 70,000 Turkish military slaves. The Seljuk Turks, converted in 960, took Baghdad in 1055 as sultans, beat Byzantium at Manzikert in August 1071 (making Anatolia “Turkey”) and so provoked the Crusades. Saladin, a Kurd, drove the Crusaders to the coast.

The Mongols

  • From 1190, when Temujin began unifying the Mongol tribes, to 1258, when his grandson took Baghdad, the Mongols overran northern China, Korea, Tibet, Central Asia, Persia, the Caucasus, Anatolia and Russia, raided India, and campaigned in Poland, Hungary, East Prussia and Bohemia in 1237–41, withdrawing only on news of the Great Khan’s death. Kubilai founded China’s Yuan dynasty; Babur founded the Mughal empire in 1526, from which Queen Victoria took her title of Empress of India in 1876.
  • China was vulnerable: the T’ang’s defeat at Talas and An Lu-shan’s rebellion of 755–63 had discredited soldiering; the Sung paid overmanned mercenary armies and had lost the north to the Khitay and Jurchen. Sun Tzu’s gradualist strategy suited China but not this enemy.
  • What made the Mongols invincible? Not numbers: the Mongol core in northern China in 1216 is estimated at only 2,000, and most of their armies were Turks. Not technology: bow and pony, the stirrup long known, no gunpowder; the cities that fell (Otrar, Balkh, Merv, Herat, Nishapur) mostly surrendered, and Gurganj held out from October 1220 to April 1221. They had strict discipline under the yasa (booty shared, death for abandoning a comrade), promotion on merit, units of tens, hundreds and thousands (eventually ninety-five “thousands”), spies, and careful strategy: Genghis attacked the Chin by the Kansu corridor, not across the Gobi. Above all Keegan points to terror and an idea: Genghis believed heaven had sent him, and took Islam’s warrior zeal without its mercy. In Bukhara he preached that he was “the flail of God”.
  • In January 1258 Hülegü took Baghdad and strangled the last Abbasid caliph. The Crusaders hoped he was Prester John. But on 3 September 1260 at Ain Jalut the Mamelukes under Qutuz and Baybars beat Kitbuqa’s Mongols in a morning: perhaps 10,000–20,000 a side, Turks on both sides, the Mamelukes feigning retreat and Qutuz charging with the cry “O Islam”. It was the professional horse army of a settled state beating one that lived by plunder.
  • Tamerlane (Timur the Lame) spread even more terror, with towers of skulls, and built nothing. Mongol power was extinct outside India by the end of the fourteenth century.

The decline of the horse peoples

  • The horse peoples could not turn conquest into lasting rule. Genghis’s empire was divided among his sons by nomad custom; Kubilai became Chinese; Hülegü became entangled in the Middle East. Nomad grazing ruins irrigated land, and the steppe economy cannot survive in farmland, so the horsemen either went home or changed their ways.
  • But they changed everyone else. They brought long-range campaigning, speed on the battlefield (at least five times a man’s on foot), the herdsman’s matter-of-fact killing at a distance, and war without ritual or heroics: fighting to win, “quickly, completely and quite unheroically”. Genghis himself kept out of danger, and nomad leaders rode inconspicuously far from the centre.
  • Their ferocity entered Europe through Russia (Christopher Duffy blames Mongol rule for the brutalisation of Russian institutions), through the Ottoman frontier, and through the Crusades to Spain, where the Reconquista’s war to the death led, Keegan suggests, to the fate of the Aztecs and Incas.
  • In China, the Ming who expelled the Mongols became despotic in their image, militarised the state and rebuilt the Great Wall as we see it today; the Manchus kept the system unchanged and stifled innovation, until Manchu bannermen faced European rifles with composite bows. Europe’s imperial armies owed bureaucracy to Sumer and Assyria and the pitched battle to the Greeks; almost everything else (long-range campaigning, speed, missile weapons, the wheel, the bond of horse and rider) came from the steppe.

Interlude 3

Armies

There are six ways to raise soldiers. Each fits a social order, each solves the problems of paying and trusting armed men differently, and none has ever solved them for good.

The medley Clausewitz ignored

  • Clausewitz could see only one rational form of army, the paid, drilled force of a bureaucratic state. Yet around him were Cossacks, the Russian serf militia (opolchenie) that harried Napoleon’s retreat, the Prussian Landwehr (which his own memorandum of January 1813 helped create), volunteer Jäger, Swiss mercenary regiments, Polish lancers, princes’ bodyguards, Croat military colonists (really refugee Serbs) and Lithuanian Tartars in the Imperial Guard.
  • One unit shows how forms mutate: the Bataillon de Neufchâtel, raised in Marshal Berthier’s Swiss principality, passed into Prussian service, became a Guard rifle battalion of the Kaiser, and in 1919 supplied recruits to the Freikorps, whose veterans formed the Nazi strong-arm units: “it is not fanciful to trace a descent” to the Waffen SS.

Andreski’s scheme, and its limits

  • The sociologist Stanislav Andreski, himself the son of a military émigré, measured societies by their Military Participation Ratio (MPR), the share of people under arms, and by two further variables: subordination (how far rulers control the ruled) and cohesion (how united the armed men are). He began from Malthus: strong men take the women of the weak, and a military class forms.
  • Keegan admires the boldness but finds it mechanistic and blind to three things: “value systems” (ritual restraint in primitive war, Islam’s slave armies, the Chinese ideal that “the superior man should be able to attain his ends without violence”), geography (Europe’s exposure to steppe and Viking raids made its knights; Venice rose because it held the Adriatic’s only secure harbour), and the pull of the warrior life itself. “Some men can be nothing but soldiers,” Keegan writes from a lifetime among them; they want the admiration of other soldiers, shared contempt for a softer world, and the rough comforts of the bivouac.
  • Yet warriors are always a minority. “A civilised man, it might be said, is someone who has discovered something more satisfying than combat.”

Six forms of military organisation

  • Keegan’s own list (see the figure): warrior, mercenary, slave, regular, conscript and militia. By coincidence Andreski also has six, with invented names (homoic, masaic, mortasic, neferic, ritterian, tellenic), but few match.
  • Warrior rule is conservative, like the samurai, Manchus and Mamelukes, and when it gives way the new rulers face two linked problems: how to pay soldiers, and how to keep them loyal. Genghis collected all booty centrally; within two generations his subordinates were taxing for themselves and Mongol power declined.
  • Feudalism, the grant of land for service, was a common way out, but a blind alley. Western fiefs were hereditary, so vassals built castles and took their own vassals until they rivalled kings (from the Carolingian division in the ninth century to the gunpowder kings of the sixteenth); the Islamic iqta was revocable, so holders squeezed their taxpayers and skimped their service.
  • Regulars began early, in Sumer, and were perfected by Assyria around the royal bodyguard. Bodyguards were often foreign so they could not conspire in the local language: the Varangian Guard of Byzantium (Scandinavians, then English after 1066, who carved runes on the lion now at Venice’s Arsenal), the Scottish Archers of France, Frederick II’s Arab guard, Franco’s Moorish Guard, the popes’ Swiss; the SAS, Keegan notes, quietly guards rulers Britain wants kept in power. Guards tend to fossilise (Yeomen of the Guard, Bavarian halberdiers with battleaxes into the nineteenth century). Regulars must be paid in peace as well as war, and cutting them after a war can bring mutiny, as the Irish Free State found in 1923.
  • Mercenaries suit rich states with few people and can work well under contract (the French and the Swiss, the British and the Gurkhas; a mercenary market at Cape Taenarum in the fourth century BC; Alexander employed some 50,000 Greek mercenaries in 329 BC). But when the money runs out they turn on their employers: condottieri seized Brescia, Cremona and Parma.
  • Militia makes arms a duty and a condition of citizenship: the Anglo-Saxon fyrd (the Germanic principle that free men bear arms, overtaken in the ninth and tenth centuries by the summons to horse-keeping vassals, but surviving in Switzerland to this day), and above all the Greek farmer-hoplites, whose phalanx Rome adopted. Machiavelli drafted Florence’s militia law of 1505 to free his city from mercenaries. Its flaw is that it arms only the propertied, capping numbers, as Sparta found against Thebes.
  • Conscription includes everyone, so it suits rich states that offer rights to all. In August 1793 the French Republic requisitioned “all Frenchmen” for the armies, and by September 1794 had 1,169,000 men under arms. Its success is what prompted Clausewitz to see war as the continuation of politics. Its costs went unseen: the Revolutionary armies lived by loot (Bonaparte’s Army of Italy became the Republic’s main source of hard currency), and later conscript states paid their soldiers less than pocket money.
  • By 1914 almost every European state had some representative institution and a huge conscript army. The bill came due: mutiny in the French army in spring 1917, the Russian army’s collapse that autumn, the German army demobilising itself in November 1918. The problem of affordable, reliable armies remained “as far from solution as when Sumer had first laid out its revenues to pay for soldiers.”

Chapter 4

Iron

Cheap iron armed the many. The Greeks used it to invent the decisive face-to-face battle, Rome turned it into the first professional army, and when Rome fell Europe spent a thousand years without real armies, held together by the Church and by knights whose code proved as self-defeating as the phalanx.

Why iron mattered

  • Stone is laborious, bronze needs scarce tin, and horses need grassland. With those alone war might never have grown beyond first-millennium BC levels. Iron ores make up some 4.2 per cent of the earth’s mass and are found almost everywhere. Keegan admits it is fashionable to doubt an “Iron Age revolution” (the idea came from Marxist scholars), but says a sudden flood of a metal that holds an edge was bound to change who could fight, and iron tools let farmers clear forests and break heavy soils.
  • Iron may first have been smelted by Mesopotamian smiths around 2300 BC looking for pigments. It came into general use about 1400 BC, centred in Anatolia, where it armed the Hittites. When their kingdom fell around 1200 BC the smiths scattered, just as the key trick was found: hot hammering and quenching in water gave iron a lasting edge. Early furnaces on windy hilltops got about eight per cent iron from ore, in a spongy “bloom” that needed endless reheating and hammering.
  • Iron armed raiders first, then the empires that struck back: the Assyrian army was “an iron army”. The most impressive early iron weapons are the long swords of the Celtic Hallstatt culture, from as early as 950 BC; their exaggerated length shows how freely the cheap new metal could be used.

The Greeks and iron

  • Minoan Crete’s palaces were destroyed about 1450 BC. Mycenaean Greece was rich enough for chariot armies: the Linear B tablets from Pylos list 200 pairs of chariot wheels in the royal arsenal. Homer, writing some 500 years after the Trojan War (which Keegan accepts as real, probably fought over Aegean trading rights), misunderstood chariots as battle taxis. A Dark Age cut Greece off from its past; writing itself seems to have been lost for 300 years after 1150 BC, when Greek-speaking but barbarian Dorians arrived with horses and iron.
  • From Dorian conquest came the city state. On Crete between 850 and 750 BC, constitutions gave rights only to those who bore arms. Boys were drafted into troops at seventeen and into men’s messes at nineteen. A Cretan drinking song: “My wealth is spear and sword, and the stout shield … with this I plough, with this I reap … with this I am entitled master of the serfs.”
  • Sparta took this furthest. Training began at seven; at twenty men moved to barracks (they could marry but not live with their wives); only at thirty did the unanimously elected become full citizens, the “equals”, whose tasks were to hold down the helot serfs and stand ready for war. Each year they waged an internal war on the helots, killing those the secret service marked as unreliable. Sparta conquered Messenia in a twenty-year war, was beaten by Argos at Hysiae in 669 BC, and by the sixth century was the greatest military power in the Peloponnese.
  • Other cities, Athens above all, looked to the sea, with colonies from Sicily and southern France to the Black Sea and Libya. Meanwhile the Greeks developed a new kind of war among themselves: iron weapons, affordable by many, carried by small farmers who were equal citizens, in battles of unprecedented ferocity. Every earlier people had fought tentatively, at a distance, with missiles. The Greeks sought decision in a single test, which led Victor Davis Hanson to the “arresting, if much contested” claim that they invented the “Western way of war”.

Phalanx warfare

  • Greek citizens farmed smallholdings, usually fifteen acres or less, whose surplus bought their armour and their vote. Invasion and “laying waste” threatened both. But Hanson, raised among California vine-growers, found devastation much harder than historians assumed: a vine cut to the roots sprouts again next spring, and uprooting a one-acre vineyard of up to 2,000 vines takes thirty-three man-hours; an olive trunk can reach twenty feet across and will not burn. Only grain was vulnerable, and only for a few weeks in May when it stood dry, and walled fields slowed raiders.
  • So Hanson’s transforming idea: with at least eighty per cent of citizens farmers, their own fields exposed while they marched, and the time for harm so short, the sensible course was to settle things fast, in one battle. The idea of decision took its place beside majority vote, dramatic plot and logic. Keegan cautions that the phalanx came some two centuries before Greece’s intellectual glories, and that Greeks kept a primitive appetite for revenge. Sport may have helped: the Olympic games ran from 776 BC to AD 261, and Herodotus was astonished that Egypt had none, since open contest between equals does not fit a god-king’s society.
  • Hoplites stood usually eight ranks deep, each paying for his own bronze helmet, breastplate and greaves (bronze survived for armour because ironsmiths could not yet make large resilient sheets). The hoplon shield, three feet across, was held on the left arm; Thucydides noticed that a phalanx drifted to the right as each man edged into his neighbour’s shield.
  • The figure follows a battle step by step. Commanders stood in the front rank in Sparta but not everywhere: “an officer class simply did not exist in classical Greek cities.” Socrates survived the rout at Delion in 424 BC by walking off with a group, “making it clear even from a distance” that he would resist. After the Sicilian defeat of 413 BC, Thucydides says, “more arms were left behind than corpses.”
  • Why no pursuit? Keegan offers two reasons. Hoplite battle already broke the deepest human dread of close-range killing, bearable only because the risk was shared; afterwards the survivors felt they had done enough. And Greeks did not fully accept conquest of Greeks: prisoners were not in theory enslaved, the games were for Greek-speakers only, and wars aimed at bigger alliances rather than subjugation (Sparta the exception). Research also suggests slaves were far outnumbered by free farmers in fifth-century Athens, so hoplites were not freed for war by slave labour.

The Persian wars and sea power

  • Persia’s empire stretched from Egypt to the Oxus. The Ionian Greeks rebelled with Athenian help in 499 BC; Darius crushed them in 494 and in 490 BC landed an army of 50,000 on the plain of Marathon, north-east of Athens. The Spartans waited for a religious festival and arrived after the battle, in which the Athenians and Plataeans destroyed a seventh of the Persian host. Hanson imagines the Persians thinking a “destructive madness” had seized the running Greeks; they wanted “to kill but not to die in the process”.
  • Xerxes prepared from 484 to 481 BC, allying with Carthage to keep Sicily’s Greeks away and bridging the straits with boats. Leonidas and “the three hundred at the pass” held Thermopylae until betrayed (August 480). Themistocles then lured the Persian fleet, some 700 ships against 500, into the narrows of Salamis (probably 23 September 480) and destroyed half of it for the loss of forty ships. Plataea and Mycale in 479 finished the invasion.
  • The lasting legacy was naval. The trireme, 120 feet by fifteen, rowed in three banks, made fleets equal to armies around an inland sea. Athens built an empire on it: 150 cities in the Delian League paid tribute. Behind its Long Walls, under Pericles, it became “a city apart”.
  • The Peloponnesian War (432–404 BC) was land power against sea power. Sparta invaded Attica almost every year; Athens lived on Black Sea grain. The Sicilian expedition of 415 overreached; Sparta allied with Persia, and after Aegospotami (405) Athens surrendered in April 404. The war exhausted the city-state system for good.

Macedon and the culmination of the phalanx

  • The fourth century was “doleful”: shifting alliances, Persian money on every side. Thebes under Epaminondas broke the Spartans at Leuctra (371 BC), where, outnumbered 11,000 to 6,000, he quadrupled the depth of his left wing and charged it while masking his right; the Spartans fell for it again at Mantinea (362), where Epaminondas was killed.
  • Philip of Macedon, who had known Epaminondas, rebuilt his army for manoeuvre and crushed Athens and Thebes at Chaeronea (338 BC). His eighteen-year-old son Alexander led the decisive cavalry stroke there. Alexander’s army of some 50,000 (Sparta rarely fielded more than 10,000 in the Peloponnesian War) combined the Companion cavalry, armoured but stirrupless, with a phalanx carrying the longer sarissa at twice the old depth.
  • His three great battles (see the figure) shared a pattern: Alexander attacked where the Persians looked strongest, because their reliance on river banks, palisades and missile screens was an “interestingly primitive” evasiveness that told him their spirit was weak.
  • But the conquest of Persia gave him the old empires’ problem: rich plains exposed to mountain and steppe peoples. His march to Central Asia and India pursued “a chimera”, a new enemy after every victory, until his army made him turn back. He died at Babylon in 323 BC, and most of his Hellenised satellites lapsed within a century.

Rome: mother house of modern armies

  • Rome took hoplite tactics from the Greeks of southern Italy under King Servius Tullius (about 580–530 BC), who by tradition sorted citizens into five property classes, then loosened the phalanx into manoeuvring “maniples”, swapped the thrusting spear for the thrown pilum followed by the sword, and, by the fourth century, paid its legionaries. That was its great divergence from Greece: smallholders became a recruiting pool for a professional army campaigning ever farther from home.
  • Rome’s motives are disputed; William Harris argues gain was “an integral part of successful warfare” for Romans. What stood out was ferocity. Polybius describes Scipio’s men at New Carthage in 209 BC killing everyone they met, “even dogs sliced in two”; Macedonian dead in 199 BC were found dismembered. Harris: “almost every year the Romans went out and did massive violence to someone — and this regularity gives the phenomenon a pathological character.” Still, Rome built no skull pyramids and did not deport peoples as the Assyrians, Mongols and Russians did; an infantry army expands “cumulatively rather than cascading”.
  • The Roman army became regular and bureaucratic early. Legionaries were chosen by the dilectus for six years (up to eighteen), young aristocrats served ten campaigns as tribunes before seeking office, and Rome never shed the belief that the right to rule rested on ability to command.
  • Its real strength was the centurions, “the first body of professional fighting officers known to history”. The career of Spurius Ligustinus, told to the consuls of 171 BC, reads like a modern warrant officer’s (see the figure). At the Sambre in 57 BC Caesar found every centurion of one cohort dead and the chief centurion Sextius Baculus “so exhausted by the wounds … that he could hardly stand up”. Such men did not serve for money but for pride, comrades’ good opinion, tokens of success, promotion and an honourable retirement.
  • Carthage relied on mercenaries. Hannibal crossed the Alps with elephants, won at Lake Trasimene (217 BC) and at Cannae on 2 August 216 BC encircled sixteen legions, some 75,000 men, killing 50,000 as they fled; from Cannae Ardant du Picq drew the insight that armies suffer their worst losses in retreat. Rome raised new legions even from the propertyless and slaves; Scipio took Spain, crossed to Africa, and beat Hannibal at Zama in 202 BC, where a chequerboard formation let the Carthaginian elephant charge pass harmlessly through.
  • Caesar conquered Gaul between 58 and 51 BC. At Alesia his lines of circumvallation and contravallation, each about fourteen miles round, penned in Vercingetorix while he held off a relief army estimated at a quarter of a million with his 55,000.
  • Augustus found half a million men under arms after the civil wars and cut them to twenty-eight legions (about 125,000 men, with as many auxiliaries), created the Praetorian Guard, set service at fifteen years (often twenty in practice) with a pension funded by new taxes, and gave auxiliaries citizenship after twenty-five years. Rome imported 400,000 tons of grain a year for its free ration.
  • Under the Antonines (98–180) the frontiers hardened into lines. Edward Luttwak calls this “a grand strategy based on preclusive security”; critics say the forts were police and customs posts built by local commanders driven by “a desire for glory”. Keegan agrees Rome had no Clausewitzian notion of war as politics, since it granted no enemy civic equality, but says the critics “stumble on the stones of the fortifications themselves”. Units stayed in one place for generations and their frontier became their world (see the figure). The empire extended citizenship to all free men in AD 212.
  • Constantine (312–37) pulled legions into central reserves and added cavalry; frontier limitanei became peasant militia. Theodosius took the fatal step of accepting whole barbarian “federate” units under their own leaders. By the fifth century “Roman” armies were Teutonic in men, weapons and even war cry (the baritus). The Vandals sacked Rome in 455; in 476 Odoacer deposed Romulus and made himself king.

Europe after Rome: a continent without armies

  • In the east the Roman army lived on as Byzantium’s, first mostly non-Roman under Belisarius and Narses (Huns in Africa, Persian archers in Italy), then reorganised into provincial “themes”, thirty by the tenth century, with a steady army of about 150,000, half horse, until the Turkish assaults of 1071. Constantinople fell to Mehmet the Conqueror in 1453.
  • In the west no army was rebuilt, because the tax base that paid for one was gone. Barbarian settlers took a third of each Italian estate (two-thirds in Burgundy) and frittered away their military virtues on scattered farms. “Rome’s principal contribution to mankind’s understanding of how life may be made civilised was its institution of a disciplined and professional army.”
  • Moral authority passed to the Church (the Franks converted in 496). Charles Martel turned back the Muslims at Poitiers in 732; Charlemagne was crowned by Pope Leo III on Christmas Day 800. Kings kept their warriors by merging the Germanic war band with the Roman precarium lease: land for service, sealed by an oath of fealty. In 877 Charles the Bald made fiefs hereditary by the Capitulary of Quierzy; every man with a horse had to come mounted to the muster. “When every man had to have a lord … feudalism was complete.”
  • The stirrup reached Europe by the early eighth century (perhaps from India via China and the steppe). Keegan refuses to take sides in the argument over whether it created the lancer, but by the ninth century the western horseman wore mail and could wield shield and lance at speed.
  • Just in time, because three waves of raiders hit Europe (see the figure). Saracen pirates took Sicily in 827 and raided from the Rhône to the Adriatic. Magyar horsemen roamed from the Danube plain from 862 until Otto I trapped them against the River Lech outside Augsburg in 955 with 8,000 men. Vikings, whose longships could sail to windward, row in calm and beach anywhere, raided from Lindisfarne (793) to Seville (844), shipped horses from about 840, reached Iceland in 870, took Normandy as a fief in 911 and, as Normans, England in 1066.
  • The Church tamed Vikings and Magyars by conversion. It could not tame its own knights: after Hastings the Norman bishops imposed a year’s penance for killing a man and forty days for wounding one. At Clermont in 1095 Urban II urged Christians to “leave off slaying each other and fight instead a righteous war”.

Crusade and chivalry

  • The Crusade idea grew out of the Spanish frontier, where Gregory VII declared in 1073 that knights could keep lands won from the infidel. Younger sons squeezed out by primogeniture, especially Normans “only a few generations removed from nomadic freebooters”, found holy war and southern land an attractive mix. The First Crusade took Jerusalem on 15 July 1099; the last Crusader state fell in 1291. The Fourth Crusade (1202–04) crippled Byzantium, and the fall of Constantinople 250 years later was its delayed result.
  • Crusading pitted the European once-for-all charge against the steppe swarm that would not receive it, especially after Saladin (from 1174) and the Mamelukes (from 1260). Defeats came when horse was separated from foot: Ramla (1102), Marj Ayyun (1179), Cresson and Hattin (both 1187).
  • The response was cultural. Fealty became a personal, religious oath to live honourably, and the military orders, Hospitallers and Templars, lived under discipline, poverty and merit-based hierarchy. Keegan sees in them an origin of the regimented armies of the sixteenth century, and the Teutonic Knights’ secularised Prussian estates later gave Frederick the Great the core of his officer corps.
  • Plate armour after the mid-fourteenth century may have suited the joust better than battle. At Crécy (1346), Poitiers (1356) and Agincourt (1415), the battles we know best, the English knights fought on foot beside archers, and most of the French dismounted in the last two. Knights riding knee to knee into each other “defies belief”. Like the phalanx, the knightly code was self-defeating; its end came from outside, with gunpowder.

Interlude 4

Logistics and Supply

Armies march on what they can carry, and for most of history that meant short wars close to home. Railways and industry lifted the limit, then buried the armies of 1914–18 in their own output; in 1939–45 American production simply overwhelmed the enemy.

The seventy-pound limit

  • A soldier can carry on average no more than about seventy pounds, at least half of it clothes, arms and kit. A man doing heavy work eats at least three pounds of solid food a day, so a marching soldier can carry supplies for ten or eleven days at most (see the figure). The numbers have not changed: Vegetius trained recruits with sixty-pound loads, the British infantry on the Somme on 1 July 1916 carried sixty-six, and the picked men who “yomped” across the Falklands in 1982 briefly carried their own body weight and were exhausted.
  • Living off the country means taking civilians’ food, so the approach of any army made people hide everything portable. Wellington, unusually, had cash and organised markets in Spain, and peasants flocked to sell. Most armies had no money, and foraging soon disperses an army and eats out its area.
  • Twenty miles a day is the best regular marching speed, whether for legions on their internal roads or von Kluck’s army from Mons to the Marne in 1914. That is too slow to find untouched food along the route, so armies either stopped to forage or carried bulk supplies, which meant a river, a coast or wheels. Pack animals were a poor substitute: to take Khiva in Central Asia in the 1870s the Russians used 8,800 camels to feed 5,500 men. Marlborough’s march to Bavaria in 1704 was provisioned down the Rhine; if a river runs the wrong way, the decisive battle may never be fought.

Roads, bullocks and Alexander’s disaster

  • Only Rome (and, with its waterways such as the Grand Canal begun in AD 608, China) built a road network fit for strategic marching. The province of Africa alone had some 10,000 miles of road, and commanders could time marches exactly (see the figure). When Rome fell, strategic marching ended for more than a thousand years: Alfred the Great brought his army out of Somerset against the Danes along the Hardway, a muddy track, although good Roman roads had run nearby. Europe began building real road networks only in the eighteenth century, and all-weather surfaces came with macadam in the nineteenth.
  • Without roads, armies relied on ships or bullocks, the commonest draught animal from the fifth millennium BC to the nineteenth century AD. Wellington wrote in 1804 that “rapid movement cannot be made without good cattle”, and in India: “there is no difficulty in fighting … but to gain your objects you must feed.”
  • Alexander reckoned his range at eight days’ march from a depot, since an ox eats its load in that time, so he kept near his fleet or sent agents ahead to buy food, which treacherous Persian officials increasingly sold. For the 300-mile march from the Indus through the Makran desert in 326 BC (as Keegan dates it) he stockpiled 52,600 tons of provisions, four months’ food for 87,000 infantry, 18,000 cavalry and 52,000 followers, to be landed by a fleet sailing along the coast. The monsoon trapped the fleet at the Indus mouth and three-quarters of the army was lost in the desert.
  • Food rotted. Grain was the staple, with oil, cheese, fish sauce (essential to the legionary), wine, vinegar or beer, so soldiers, like sailors, suffered diseases of malnutrition, and epidemics swept camps and sieges. Canned meat (1845, with a lead-poisoning risk that killed many on Franklin’s polar expedition), dried milk (1855), evaporated milk (1860) and margarine (invented in a competition set by Napoleon III in the 1860s) changed the army diet. The Union ate from the Chicago stockyards; a Confederate wrote in 1862, “We have lived some days on raw, baked and roasted apples … and sometimes nothing.”

Railways

  • The American Civil War was the first railway war. The United States had 30,000 miles of track in 1860, more than the rest of the world combined, and the North held it by 2.4 to 1, laying more each month while Union soldiers tore up Confederate track the South could not replace. Keegan warns against calling it a purely railway war: logistic supremacy alone rarely beats a determined enemy, as McClellan found in 1862, and exhausted Germany and Japan still inflicted setbacks in 1944–45. But Napoleon was right that victory goes to the big battalions, and railways moved them.
  • Europe’s track grew from nothing in 1825 to 175,000 miles in 1900. Prussia had taken half its railways into public ownership by 1860 and all of them twenty years later. In 1866 it moved the Guard Corps to the Austrian front within a week, twelve trains a day; by 1876 the German general staff had a railway department, and border halts had platforms a mile long.
  • From 1 to 17 August 1914 Germany multiplied its peacetime army of 800,000 six times and carried 1,485,000 men to the western front. France managed its railways as well in 1914 as it had badly in 1870, and Russia surprised everyone with its speed. Within a month 62 French, 87 German, 49 Austrian and 114 Russian divisions of about 15,000 men were at the front.
  • Then mobility evaporated. Beyond railhead men marched and horses hauled, and artillery made a fire zone miles deep where only men could carry food and ammunition. Horse fodder was the largest single cargo landed at French ports for the British army in 1914–18. In the Second World War the German army, short of trucks and fuel, took more horses than in the First (see the figure); only the Americans and British could supply their front line by motor alone. America also gave the Red Army 395,883 trucks and 2,700,000 tons of gasoline, which the Soviets admitted carried them from Stalingrad to Berlin.

The arms industry

  • Consumption exploded (see the figure). The British “shell crisis” of 1915 was met by emergency industrialisation. France, which had planned on 10,000 75-mm shells a day, made 200,000 a day in 1915, and in 1917–18 supplied the Americans with 10 million shells and 4,791 of the 6,287 aircraft their air corps flew in combat. German explosive output rose from 1,000 tons a month in 1914 to 6,000 in 1915; Russian shell output from 450,000 a month in 1915 to 4.5 million in 1916.
  • Nothing like this existed before. Rome ran arms factories and in 398 ordered its armourers branded to stop desertion. Charlemagne banned the export of mail shirts in 779 (again in 805); the mail worn by his summoned horsemen, some 180 tons, represented years of his chain-smiths’ output. Plate armour came from royal workshops such as Greenwich just as gunpowder made it obsolete. Saltpetre came only from earth soaked by urine and dung, so states took its collection under control; cannon-founding, which grew out of bell-founding, was a royal prerogative from the start, and state arsenals begin with the artillery revolution of the late fifteenth century.
  • Jean Maritz, a Swiss in French service from 1734, saw that a barrel cast solid and then bored would fit the ball better; his son built the boring machine, copied in England in 1774. Jean Gribeauval standardised French artillery in 1763–67; his guns were still in service in 1829.
  • Then private industry took over. William Armstrong, a maker of hydraulic machinery, read about artillery in the Crimean War and decided it was “time military engineering was brought up to the level of current engineering practice”: his Elswick works made 1,600 rifled breech-loaders between 1857 and 1861. Alfred Krupp showed a steel breech-loader at the Great Exhibition of 1851, turned a profit in 1863 on Russian orders, and by 1914 had reached 420-mm calibre; his 11-inch naval guns beat British 13.5-inch ones. American makers in the Connecticut River valley pioneered interchangeable parts, and Woolwich could turn out 250,000 cartridges a day.
  • Fear of over-supply pushed makers to obsolete their own designs. Hiram Maxim’s gun of 1884 fired 600 rounds a minute using the energy of each shot; its operator was “a uniformed industrial process-worker”. Machine-guns and magazine rifles, reaching 1,000 yards and accurate to 500, gave the defence dominance in 1914. More guns, tanks and bombing all failed to break it; the war was decided by attrition of men by industrial output, the Materialschlacht, and it was “almost fortuitous” that Germany rather than one of its enemies went under.

America’s war

  • Supply won the Second World War outright. Rearmament to supply Britain and Russia had revived American industry, which then made the fastest expansion ever known (see the figure), largely paid from revenue rather than borrowing. U-boats could not sink ships as fast as they were built, even before American long-range aircraft and escort carriers defeated them.
  • So in any future conventional war for national survival, industrial capacity would decide. That no such war has followed 1945 is due to America’s parallel project, the atomic bomb, the end of a 500-year search, begun with gunpowder, for a stored source of military energy to replace the muscles of men and beasts.

Chapter 5

Fire

Gunpowder broke castles, then built new fortress frontiers, turned soldiers into drilled servants, and carried Europe across the oceans. The French Revolution made every man a soldier; industry armed him; and the result was the slaughter of 1914–18, total war under Hitler, and finally a weapon that made Clausewitz’s logic suicidal.

From naphtha to cannon

  • Byzantine “Greek fire” (seventh century), probably based on the naphtha the Babylonians called “the thing that blazes”, was an incendiary squirted at wooden ships and siege works, feared but not revolutionary. The Chinese found that naphtha mixed with saltpetre exploded, and around AD 950, perhaps in Taoist temples, combined sulphur, charcoal and saltpetre into gunpowder. Whether they used it in war is disputed; there is no evidence of Chinese cannon (as opposed to fireworks) before the late thirteenth century.
  • In Europe the first picture of a gun, from 1326, shows a vase-shaped vessel (perhaps cast by a bell-founder) firing a large arrow at a castle gate while the gunner gingerly applies a taper. There may have been cannon at Agincourt in 1415, making noise and smoke. By 1450–53 the French were knocking holes in English-held castles in Normandy and Aquitaine, and in 1453 the Turks battered down the walls of Constantinople with bombards so big they were sometimes cast on the spot. With cannon Louis XI broke the dukes of Burgundy’s castles in 1477; by 1478 the French crown controlled its own territory for the first time since the Carolingians, with “cannon the ultimate tax-collectors from refractory vassals”.

Gunpowder and fortification

  • Early cannon were too heavy to leave home ground. French founders of the early 1490s produced a gun that would decide battles for nearly 400 years: a slender bronze tube no more than eight feet long, firing iron balls three times as destructive as stone, with trunnions cast on so it could ride a two-wheeled carriage hitched to a limber and be aimed by wedges under the breech.
  • In 1494 Charles VIII shipped forty such guns to La Spezia and marched down Italy to claim Naples. Cities surrendered on hearing how fast his guns had breached Firizzano; he entered Florence in November and in February 1495 took the Neapolitan fortress of San Giovanni in eight hours, a place that had once withstood a seven-year siege. Guicciardini: “as much execution was inflicted in a few hours as used to be done in Italy over the same number of days.”
  • The guns worked by cutting a horizontal channel along the base of a wall, turning its height against it: the taller the wall, the faster it toppled, filling the ditch with rubble for the storming party. Refusing to surrender after a breach had long forfeited any right to quarter; in the artillery age that convention became absolute.
  • Engineers improvised (Pisa’s inner earth bank and ditch of 1500, the “double Pisan rampart”; musketeers defending breaches at Cremona in 1523 and Marseilles in 1524) and then found a whole new answer within about fifty years: low, thick walls fronted by angular bastions of stone or brick filled with rammed earth, whose angled faces deflected shot and whose flanks swept the ditch with fire (see the figure). The Sangallo family designed bastions for Poggio Imperiale in 1487 and began Civita Castellana for Pope Alexander VI in 1494; the San Micheli, Savorgnano, Peruzzi, Genga and Antonelli followed. Leonardo inspected fortresses for Cesare Borgia; Michelangelo, who fortified Florence in 1527–29, told Antonio da Sangallo in 1545 that he knew “more about them than the whole tribe of the Sangallos”.
  • These engineers were the first international technical mercenaries since the chariot-builders, working from Russia and Malta to West Africa and the Caribbean, proud, indebted and scorned by soldiers who despised men who mixed the theory of war with its weapons. Their bastion forts restored the defence’s advantage. By 1600 every serious state had fortified its passes, crossings and estuaries, while kings used artillery to flatten their grandees’ castles inside. Europe’s modern frontiers are, Keegan says, largely the outcome of this fortress-building.
  • The Dutch revolt of 1566 against Spain showed it: an eighty-year war won largely by rich towns throwing up earthen bastions. In 1573 Alkmaar and Haarlem absorbed Spain’s whole counter-offensive; the governor’s secretary wrote, “if several towns decide to hold out, we shall never be able to take them.” Siege became a science of trenches (Vauban later fixed the number of parallels at three), and Spanish soldiers hated digging where the water table lay two feet down: Parma had to pay diggers extra and fight “the twisted pride of the Castilians, who regarded begging in the streets as more honourable than labouring for a reward”. Maurice of Nassau went over to the offensive from 1590; Spain took Ostend after a three-year siege but was exhausted, and agreed a truce in 1608.

Gunpowder battles in the experimental age

  • Soldiers overcame their fear of a gun held in the hands by way of the crossbow (in Chinese tombs of the fourth century BC, in Europe from the late thirteenth century AD), whose stock, trigger and kick prepared them for firearms. The first handgunners may well have been crossbowmen.
  • Nobody knew the right mix of arms. The Swiss made the pike famous: at St Jacob-en-Birs in 1444, 1,500 Swiss pushed into a French army of 30,000 and fought until all were dead, and at Granson and Morat (1476) and Nancy (1477) they destroyed Burgundian power. Charles the Rash had had a sensible mix in 1471 (1,250 armoured horsemen, 1,250 pikemen, 1,250 handgunners, 5,000 archers) but not the money for numbers. Machiavelli wanted twenty foot to each horseman. Venice replaced all crossbows with firearms in 1490.
  • Commanders first used guns from behind field entrenchments, as if battles were sieges: Ravenna (1512), where two French cannon brought round to the Spanish rear broke the defence, and Marignano (1515), a two-day battle after which the Swiss made the peace that made them France’s chief mercenaries for 250 years. At Cerignola (1503) and Bicocca (1522) entrenched Spanish handgunners won; at Bicocca 3,000 Swiss died in half an hour and never again attacked handgunners behind an obstacle.
  • The deeper resistance was cultural. The knight wanted to fight on horseback and have infantry face cavalry at the point of the pike. Bayard executed captured crossbowmen as cowards; Louis de la Trémoille’s biographer asked what use knightly skill was now. But the matchlock musket could pierce armour at 200 to 240 paces, and by 1600 full armour had gone. Spanish hidalgos led the way in accepting infantry commissions, a precedent that later made places in the foot guards the most sought-after.

The face-to-face frontier

  • Here Keegan offers one of the book’s big ideas. The Greeks abandoned the evasiveness of primitive war; the Romans learned it from them; the Gauls, with their Hallstatt swords, and the Germans whom Tacitus admired, fought face to face before they met Rome. Since phalanx warfare followed the Dorians, who probably came from the Danube, there may be a common origin there, and a line of division: “east of the steppe and south-east of the Black Sea, warriors continued to keep their distance from their enemies; west of the steppe and south-west of the Black Sea, warriors learned to abandon caution and to close to arm’s length.”
  • Why is baffling. The line follows climate and vegetation more than language (the Iranians spoke an Indo-European language but kept the bow), and race explains nothing: the Zulus and Japanese learned Western-style combat by their own effort in the nineteenth century. There is a “military horizon”, and also a “face-to-face combat frontier”, with Westerners on one side of it and most other peoples on the other.

Gunpowder at sea

  • Ships and cannon “were made for each other”: a ship carries weight, and only recoil had to be solved. Mediterranean galleys mounted guns in the bow. The Ottomans took Constantinople (1453) and then Serbia, Albania and the Peloponnese; Selim I crushed Persia in 1514 and took Egypt from the Mamelukes; Suleiman the Magnificent took Belgrade (1521) and Rhodes (1522), destroyed Hungary at Mohács (1526) and reached Vienna (1529). At sea Khair ed-Din beat Andrea Doria at Preveza (1538) and raided as far as Nice (1543) and Minorca (1558). The Knights held Malta from May to September 1565 until a Spanish fleet relieved them.
  • Lepanto (1571) was won not by ramming or gunnery but by embarked Christian arquebusiers against Turkish archers: 30,000 of 60,000 Turks were killed. The ships were soon replaced; the archers, a life’s training each, were not: “Lepanto marked the death of a living tradition that could not be reconstituted.”
  • In the oceans the load-carrying northern sailing ship, which could stay at sea for months, took great guns below decks to fire broadside: the Mary Rose of 1513 is usually named first, the Great Harry had heavy guns on two decks by 1545, and in 1588 whole fleets fought a seven-day running battle up the Channel against the Armada.
  • Sailing ships carried Columbus and then the conquistadors, whose decisive cargo was horses, an animal extinct in the Americas since the first hunters: seventeen for Cortés in Mexico, fifty for Montejo in Yucatán (1527), twenty-seven for Pizarro in Peru (1531). Elsewhere cannon did the work. The Portuguese won control of the Indian Ocean at Ormuz (1507) and Diu (1509) and reached the East Indies (1511) and China (1557), though they learned at Jiddah in 1517 not to close with a fleet backed by shore guns. Africa was protected by disease, Japan by closing itself, China by its size; India’s Moghuls never built a cannon-armed fleet.
  • The Europeans then fought each other: the Dutch reached India in 1601, the English eight years later, and they fought three naval wars in 1652–74. By 1650 ships carried fifty guns in fleets of seventy or more. In the Three Days’ Battle of 1653 the Dutch lost twenty of seventy-five warships and 3,000 men; by Trafalgar (1805) the largest ships carried a hundred guns, and Keegan puts the Franco-Spanish dead above 7,000. “The warrior culture of the pikeman and the man on horseback had migrated to sea.”

Gunpowder stability

  • Cavalry tried to fight with pistols through the wheeling caracole (whose routines survive at the Spanish Riding School in Vienna). It failed; “firearms and horses do not mix.” Armies kept one pikeman to two musketeers well into the seventeenth century, and the Thirty Years’ War (1618–48) was messy: Gustavus Adolphus died at Lützen (1632) riding into a static fight. The ring bayonet, adopted by almost every army at the end of the century, let one weapon serve as pike and gun.
  • Drill made the eighteenth-century army. It began as a safety measure among men handling loose powder near burning match: Maurice of Orange’s drill book of 1607 broke loading and firing into forty-seven motions. By the eighteenth century sergeants levelled the front rank’s muzzles with a half-pike so the volley struck at one height; men aimed at a mass, not a man.
  • Uniform was livery, the mark of a servant. The slashed silks of the sixteenth-century soldier, loot worn with impunity, gave way to flogging and beating that no free pikeman would have borne. Recruits were no longer the “outlaws, vagabonds, thieves, murderers” of the civil wars but younger sons of poor families under notional conscription, or outright serfs in Prussia and Russia: in effect, Keegan says, a military slave system close to the janissaries.
  • Officers were schooled too: Venice’s academies, John of Nassau’s schola militaris at Siegen (1617–23), the first true military academy, and Louis XIV’s artillery school at Metz (1668). Rote learning and the rod replaced falconry and the joust.
  • The result, Russell Weigley argued, was indecision. Musket, gun and horse balanced each other, and the “great” battles of Blenheim (1704), Fontenoy (1745) and Leuthen (1757) were notable for casualties more than results; wars ended in exhaustion of money and men.
  • Yet drilled armies beat warrior peoples. The janissaries, raised by the devshirme levy of Christian boys, made Europe tremble at Vienna in 1683, but the Habsburgs matched them and forced the Peace of Karlowitz (1699), the start of an Ottoman retreat that ended with the Balkan wars of 1911–12. The Moghuls, rooted in the steppe, never took to drill: Sir Thomas Roe, at their court in 1615–19, called their forces “an effeminat army, fitter to be a spoyle than a terror to enemys”. At Plassey (1757) Clive’s 1,100 Europeans and 2,100 sepoys scattered 50,000.

Political revolution and military change

  • The American War of Independence was “the first truly political war”, fought for principles. Perhaps a third of colonists supported it, a third were neutral and a third loyal. The rebels invaded Canada in 1775, spread the British thin across a huge theatre, and with the French fleet of 1780 forced the surrender at Yorktown in October 1781. Its cost broke French royal finances.
  • Revolutionary France needed an army fast after July 1792. It abolished the National Guard’s property test on 30 July 1792, called for 300,000 men in early 1793, and on 23 August 1793 decreed the levée en masse. Volunteers were brigaded with regulars two to one; officers were elected (until 1794) and discipline was by tribunal (until 1795). Over ninety per cent of officers were noble in 1789, three per cent by 1794 (see the figure). The army reached 983,000 in 1793, when 100,000 had been an enormous force.
  • The Comte de Guibert had already argued for manoeuvre in massed columns rather than lines, but as an absolutist he could not accept its social logic; the Revolution did. The new armies bypassed fortresses, lived off the land, and were not undertrained: officials reported in June 1793 that “the veteran soldiers are astonished” at the volunteers’ precision. Napoleon inherited and extended the whirlwind: Austerlitz (1805), Jena (1806), Wagram (1809), more than a million men under arms by 1812, checked only in Spain.
  • On these victories Clausewitz built his theory. He admitted debts to Machiavelli, whose Art of War ran to twenty-one editions in the sixteenth century and first tied war to government. But Machiavelli gave advice; Clausewitz, like Marx, claimed to reveal inescapable truth. His ideas, spread by the Prussian War Academy and proved in 1866 and 1870–71, made European armies by 1914 “as Clausewitzian as the continent’s coalition of socialist and revolutionary movements was Marxist”. Liddell Hart called him “the Mahdi of Mass”.
  • Keegan thinks that overstated. Generals always wanted more men; conscription worked in 1914 only because a whole culture now accepted that every fit man owed the state service and that service taught civic virtue. “Architects create structures, but they cannot determine moods.”
  • The American Civil War had shown the cost: about 1 million men under arms in the South and 2 million in the North from 32 million people, a participation ratio of about ten per cent, the most a society can bear and still function; 620,000 dead, more than the Americans killed in the two world wars, Korea and Vietnam together. Europe drew the opposite lesson. Conscription seemed to make a man more of a citizen; McNeill: “escape from freedom was often a real liberation.” (“Infantilism” and “infantry”, Keegan notes, share a root.) A French parliamentary report of 1905 said universal service proved “the democratic spirit has once again bound up the chains of time”.
  • On 3 August 1914 the rectors of the Bavarian universities declared: “Students! The muses are silent … the holy war begins.” Keegan counts the primitive strands: reason set aside, the steppe terror invoked, Germany’s barbarian past praised, and holy war, a Muslim idea, proclaimed. The students volunteered almost as a body and in October 1914, after two months’ drill, were thrown against British regulars near Ypres. The Kindermord bei Ypern left 36,000 young men in a common grave at Langemarck, killed in three weeks.

Firepower and the culture of universal service

  • Hitler survived Langemarck; his regiment, the 16th Bavarian Reserve, had 611 of its 3,600 men unwounded after a month. Losses were unprecedented both in total and as a share of manpower. Before conscription armies were tiny (156,000 in France in 1789 out of 29.1 million), battles rarely killed more than ten per cent of those engaged, and battles were rare (the Republic fought fifty in 1792–1800, a very high rate). Napoleon’s battles pushed losses up (see the figure). In the Civil War about seven per cent of Confederate soldiers and three per cent of Union soldiers died in battle, with 400,000 more dead of disease.
  • By 1914 disease no longer killed most soldiers (the Boer War was Britain’s last where sickness killed more than bullets), and healthier, better-fed populations supplied more men to the slaughter. France had a million casualties by September 1915; at Verdun it lost 500,000 killed and wounded and Germany over 400,000; on 1 July 1916 the British lost 20,000 killed, almost as many as died in the whole Boer War.
  • Firepower explained it. Eighteenth-century musketry killed one man for every 200 to 460 rounds, and a musketeer fired three times a minute. By 1914 a rifleman fired fifteen rounds a minute, a field gun twenty shrapnel shells, and a machine-gun 600. A battalion that stood up could vanish in minutes, as the 1st Newfoundland Regiment did on 1 July 1916, and flight across a killing zone hundreds of yards deep offered no escape.
  • By 1917 France had lost a million dead and half its fighting divisions refused to attack after the failed April offensive, “a large-scale military strike against the operation of an unbearable probability”: four in nine Frenchmen in fighting units were killed or wounded by the end. Italy collapsed after a million casualties in eleven Alpine offensives; Russia’s army voted for peace “with its feet” (Lenin). Indirect strategies failed: the Dreadnought fleets (Britain 28, Germany 18 in 1914) met indecisively at Jutland, U-boats were beaten by convoy, Gallipoli (1915) showed that “local firepower ashore defeated strategic power at sea”. The Germans finally flinched in October 1918.
  • “Every man a soldier” rested on a misunderstanding of human nature. Warrior peoples had avoided sustained combat and allowed retreat; no pre-1789 society thought soldiering a calling for more than a few. The Revolution was not wholly wrong (Murat had studied for the priesthood, Bessières medicine, Brune had edited a newspaper), but a war that kills a million shatters a nation’s spirit.
  • The victors swore never again. Cynthia Asquith wrote in October 1918 that peace would make one “fully recognise that the dead are not only dead for the duration of the war”. France built the Maginot Line (3 billion francs for its first phase, as much as Britain’s Dreadnought programme of 1906–13); Britain adopted Churchill’s 1919 “ten-year rule”, renewed until 1932, and did not rearm in earnest until 1937.

Ultimate weapons

  • For Hitler the war had been “the greatest of all experiences”. Versailles cut the German army to 100,000 and abolished its air force; paramilitary parties marched across the defeated and disappointed countries (Turkey, under Atatürk, was the exception). Hitler admired Mussolini and borrowed his legionary symbols, failed in his 1923 coup, and then chose elections, building a stormtrooper militia of 100,000 by 1931. On 8 February 1933 he told his cabinet “the next five years have to be devoted to rendering the German people again capable of bearing arms.” Rearmament was hugely popular (see the figure).
  • In Poland in 1939 sixty-two German divisions, ten of them panzer, overwhelmed forty Polish ones in five weeks; nearly a million Poles were captured, 200,000 of them by the Soviets. Blitzkrieg, a journalist’s word, used the old technique of Epaminondas, Alexander and Napoleon (a concentrated blow at one point), but tanks could advance thirty to fifty miles a day and radio carried orders in “real time”. General Erhard Milch: “The real secret is speed — speed of attack through speed of communication.” It was also meant to be cheap, sparing Germany the Materialschlacht it blamed for 1918.
  • In May 1940 the panzers came through the Ardennes and reached the Channel at Abbeville on 19 May; France asked for an armistice on 17 June. “The great battle of France is over,” a young German officer wrote. “It lasted twenty-six years.” Hitler made twelve generals field marshals on 19 July and planned to demobilise thirty-five of his hundred divisions.
  • Keegan calls Hitler “the most dangerous war leader ever to have afflicted civilisation”, because he combined three beliefs: faith in superior weapons, the primacy of a (racial) warrior class, and a genuine Clausewitzian refusal to separate war from politics. “Not one of you has read Clausewitz,” he told a Munich audience in 1934; his last testament cited “the great Clausewitz”.
  • Air war turned total after a German bomber mistakenly hit Freiburg on 10 May 1940 and the French were blamed. Douhet had argued air power alone could win. The Blitz on London began on 7 September 1940 and killed 13,596 Londoners that year, but after losing 600 bombers in August–September the Luftwaffe gave up daylight attack. Barbarossa (22 June 1941) reached Leningrad and Moscow by December; of 5 million Soviet prisoners, more than 3 million died. At sea the U-boats were beaten in 1943 by air cover, escort carriers and codebreaking.
  • Britain began “area bombing” under Arthur Harris in February 1942; an Air Staff directive of 14 February aimed at “the morale of the enemy civilian population”. At Hamburg on 24–30 July 1943 eighty per cent of buildings were damaged or destroyed and 30,000 people killed. Keegan notes the irony that the Wright brothers hoped the aeroplane would bring mankind closer.
  • Japan had built the world’s best naval air force. Yamamoto warned “we can run wild for six months to a year”, after which “the oil wells of Texas and the factories of Detroit” would tell. A warrior people who had seen off the Mongols and banished guns, Japan after the opening of 1854 deliberately adopted Western industry and universal service, beat Russia in 1904–05, and in 1941–42 its conscripts outfought Indian “martial races” under British officers. At Tarawa (1943) eight of a garrison of 5,000 were found alive. The United States launched twenty-one fleet carriers in 1941–44 to Japan’s five; its submarines sank half Japan’s merchant fleet and two-thirds of its tankers; its fire raids burnt out sixty per cent of the sixty largest cities.
  • Bombing had not beaten Germany: armies had. Bomber crews lost five, sometimes ten per cent a mission. Hitler turned to rockets; the V-2, called “the decisive weapon of the war” in July 1943, entered service in September 1944, and 2,600 were fired, killing 2,500 people in London. Einstein’s warning reached Roosevelt on 11 October 1939; Britain’s Tube Alloys team joined the Manhattan Project; Germany, it turned out, was nowhere near a chain reaction. After Alamogordo (16 July 1945) Churchill said: “What was gunpowder? Trivial. What was electricity? Meaningless. This Atomic Bomb is the Second Coming in Wrath!” Stimson wanted “a tremendous shock”; Hiroshima and Nagasaki killed 103,000, and the emperor broadcast surrender on 15 August.

Law and war’s end

  • War went on after 1945. Japan had humiliated Europe’s empires before their subjects. Britain left Burma in 1948 and fought in Malaya only by promising self-government; the Dutch left the East Indies; France fought the Viet Minh, schooled in Mao’s “protracted war”, from 1946 until it gave up. By the 1960s the empires had conceded almost everywhere. The hundred or so new armies were mostly of little worth: “technology transfers”, a euphemism for selfish arms sales, did not transfer the culture. Only the Vietnamese made the transition Japan had, against the United States in 1965–72.
  • The real fear was the bomb. Once the Soviet Union tested one in 1949 and both sides built hydrogen bombs, Stimson’s phrase, “a psychological weapon”, came true. “How could war be an extension of politics, when the ultimate object of rational politics is to further the well-being of political entities?” Clever men built deterrence theory to rescue Clausewitz (threat, not use) and by the 1960s “mutually assured destruction”; by the 1980s each side had some 2,000 intercontinental launchers and tens of thousands of warheads.
  • Law had long tried to restrain war (see the figure). The indifference of the gunpowder age was summed up by W. E. Hall in 1880: law must “accept war, independently of the justice of its origin”. Since the Pact of Paris of 1928 all war has been technically illegal, and the UN Charter added the means to use force against a transgressor.
  • Keegan’s hope, written in 1993, rests on the UN-authorised war to free Kuwait in 1990–91, which he calls “the first genuine triumph of just war morality since Grotius”. But armies remain necessary: “a world without armies — disciplined, obedient and law-abiding armies — would be uninhabitable.” The alternative is life below the military horizon or Hobbes’s war of “all against all”, already visible on television where cheap weapons, “the industrial world’s most shameful product”, saturate communal feuds.
  • We need not believe, with Margaret Mead, that war is an invention, nor try to change our genes. We need only accept that war has become a habit, once hedged by ritual that post-primitive ingenuity stripped away. “The habits of the primitive — devotees themselves of restraint, diplomacy and negotiation — deserve relearning. Unless we unlearn the habits we have taught ourselves, we shall not survive.”

Conclusion

Conclusion

Three military cultures, primitive, Oriental and Western, each limited or unleashed war in its own way. The Western way conquered the world and then turned on itself. Politics must continue; war cannot.

What is war? No simple answer

  • Keegan returns to the question he began with and hopes he has cast doubt on any simple answer, above all Clausewitz’s.
  • This century’s wars have touched most families in western Europe, the United States, Russia and China over three or four generations and inured people to the expectation of war. Yet everyday life runs on co-operation: “It is the spirit of cooperativeness, not confrontation, that makes the world go round.” Neighbourliness flourishes inside the bounds of law, which means policing, which is coercion: a silent admission that man has a darker side.

Primitive restraint

  • That is why primitive war matters. Modern war has given restraint a bad name, but primitive peoples limit war by exemption (women, children, the old and unfit spared), by convention (agreed times, places, seasons and pretexts for fighting) and above all by ritual, which defines combat and requires that, once it is performed, the sides accept satisfaction and turn to conciliation or arbitration.
  • Keegan warns again against idealising it: primitive war can turn savage, and even restrained war can push the weak onto worse land and destroy cultures.

Oriental warmaking

  • Culture is a prime determinant of how war is fought, as Asia shows. “Oriental warmaking”, if it may be so called, is marked by “evasion, delay and indirectness”. Attila, Genghis and Tamerlane seem to contradict that, but over 3,000 years of horse warfare their campaigns were widely spaced interruptions. The horse warrior fought at a distance, with missiles, withdrew when resisted and wore enemies down rather than overthrowing them, so walls and fixed defences (the Great Wall, the Russian cherta) could usually contain him.
  • Horse peoples who did conquer settled lands (the Moghuls, the Ottomans, the Mamelukes) stayed wedded to “the camp, the horse and the bow” and were extinguished when they met powers that had adapted to technological change.
  • Yet Oriental warmaking had an ideological and intellectual dimension the West acquired only later. China subordinated the warrior to law and custom and made moderation an ideal. Islam, often seen as a religion of conquest, came to restrain war too: divided over fighting fellow Muslims, it handed warmaking to a specialist slave class of steppe horsemen, freeing the pious to stress the “greater” holy war, the war against self. Like China it became circumscribed, and when it met a culture that recognised none of these limits, it succumbed.

The Western way of war

  • That culture was Western, and it had three elements (see the figure). The moral element came from the Greeks: the face-to-face battle to the death. Alexander against Darius is both history and a paradigm of the clash between two ethics, and Darius, killed by his own entourage to save their skins, is “a genuinely tragic figure”. The ethic passed to Rome and somehow to the Teutonic peoples, whose kingdoms married it to horseback combat: the knight, unlike the nomad, charged home.
  • The intellectual element came from Islam. The Crusades resolved Christianity’s dilemma over the morality of fighting by transmitting the ethic of holy war, giving Western military culture an ideological dimension it had lacked. The technological element was the West’s own openness to change, complete by the eighteenth century with gunpowder weapons perfected. Asia’s persistence with obsolete weapons, Keegan suggests, was “a perfectly rational form of arms control”; the West, by forsaking it, arrived at what Clausewitz called war itself.
  • It carried all before it. In the nineteenth century all Asia except China, Japan, Siam and the Ottoman lands fell under Western rule, and “the primitives of the Americas, Africa and the Pacific stood no chance at all”. Only the Turks, “that tough, intelligent and resourceful warrior race”, emerged unsubdued.
  • But the triumph was delusive. Turned in on itself, the Western way brought the First World War, which ended European dominance and handed the future to militarists and totalitarians, then the Second, then nuclear weapons, “the ultimate denial of the proposition that war was, or might be, a continuation of politics by other means”.

Politics must continue; war cannot

  • The warrior is still needed: the world community needs “skilful and disciplined warriors” at its service, “protectors of civilisation, not its enemies”, against “ethnic bigots, regional warlords, ideological intransigents, common pillagers and organised international criminals”.
  • Their style cannot come from the Western model alone. Future peacekeepers have much to learn from Oriental and primitive cultures: intellectual restraint, symbolic ritual, and above all “the denial that politics and war belong within the same continuum. Unless we insist on denying it, our future, like that of the last Easter Islanders, may belong to the men with bloodied hands.”

Thirty years on

What held up

Keegan finished the book in June 1993, after the Gulf War and in the middle of the Yugoslav wars. The book makes several claims about where war was heading. Here is how they look from 2026.

Held

No war between great powersCh. 5, Conclusion

He argued industrial and nuclear war had made great-power war self-defeating. No two great powers have fought each other directly since 1945, now more than eighty years. But large-scale war between states has returned: Russia’s full invasion of Ukraine in 2022 is the biggest war in Europe since 1945, fought very much as politics by other means.

Held

The war of all against allCh. 5

He warned of places “saturated by the cheap weapons which are the industrial world’s most shameful product”. Rwanda (1994), Bosnia, the Congo wars (1996–2003), Somalia, Syria and Sudan have all borne him out, with militias, warlords and massacres closer to his “primitive” war than to Clausewitz’s.

Held

Weapons without the cultureCh. 5

He said Western arms sales did not transfer the military culture that made the weapons deadly. The collapse of the Western-built Iraqi army before ISIS in 2014 and of the Afghan army in 2021 are the starkest cases since.

Partly

Disciplined warriors as peacekeepersConclusion

He hoped professional armies would serve the world community against warlords and bigots. UN and NATO missions multiplied in the 1990s, but Srebrenica (1995) and Rwanda showed peacekeepers without the mandate to fight, and later interventions in Iraq, Afghanistan and Libya were not the disciplined policing he pictured.

Missed

The UN as the arbiter of just warCh. 5

He called the UN-authorised liberation of Kuwait “the first genuine triumph of just war morality since Grotius”. It proved a high-water mark: Kosovo (1999) and Iraq (2003) went ahead without Security Council authority, and vetoes have paralysed the Council over Syria and Ukraine.

Missed

Nuclear danger recedingCh. 5

He saw superpower arms cuts as the most hopeful development since 1945. Arms control has since unravelled (the INF treaty ended in 2019; Russia suspended New START in 2023, and the treaty was due to lapse in February 2026), and India, Pakistan and North Korea have all tested bombs since he wrote.

Partly

A masculine activityInterlude i

His claim that women have never fought, “with the most insignificant exceptions”, was already arguable in 1993 (the women soldiers of Dahomey, Soviet women snipers, pilots and tank crews in the Second World War). Since then many armies, including the American and British, have opened all combat roles to women, and women have fought in large numbers in Ukraine.

Context

The arguments it started

Did he misread Clausewitz?1994 →

Clausewitz scholars say yes. Christopher Bassford’s “John Keegan and the Grand Tradition of Trashing Clausewitz” (War in History, 1994) argues that Clausewitz’s Politik covers culture, society and the passions of peoples, and that his “trinity” of passion, chance and reason already makes room for most of what Keegan says. Martin van Creveld’s The Transformation of War (1991), published just before, made a similar anti-Clausewitz case from the rise of war by non-state groups.

Was primitive war really so limited?1996 →

Lawrence Keeley’s War Before Civilization (1996) marshals archaeological and ethnographic evidence that pre-state warfare was frequent and, relative to population, often far deadlier than modern war. Steven Pinker built on this in The Better Angels of Our Nature (2011); Douglas Fry and others reply that nomadic foragers fought little and that the violent cases are mostly settled or disturbed societies. Keegan himself warns against idealising primitive war, but his closing call to relearn its restraint depends on it.

Can Chagnon be trusted?2000 →

The Stone chapter leans on Napoleon Chagnon’s Yanomamö. Patrick Tierney’s Darkness in El Dorado (2000) accused Chagnon of distorting and even provoking the violence he described; an American Anthropological Association inquiry criticised him in 2002 and then rescinded its report in 2005. The data are still argued over.

Is there a “Western way of war”?2001 →

Victor Davis Hanson extended the idea in Carnage and Culture (2001). John Lynn’s Battle: A History of Combat and Culture (2003) accepts that culture shapes war but rejects a single continuous Western tradition from the hoplites to today. Keegan’s “Oriental warmaking” has drawn sharper criticism: Peter Lorge’s The Asian Military Revolution (2008) shows Chinese and other Asian states fighting decisive gunpowder wars, which cuts against evasion and delay as a cultural constant.

Culture or human nature?2006

Azar Gat’s War in Human Civilization (2006) roots war in evolved human behaviour, competition for resources and mates, and treats culture as working on top of it rather than instead of it.

The stirrup and the military revolution1956 →

Keegan declines to take sides on Lynn White’s claim (Medieval Technology and Social Change, 1962) that the stirrup created the knight and feudalism, which most medievalists now reject. His Fire chapter follows the “military revolution” thesis of Michael Roberts (1956) and Geoffrey Parker (The Military Revolution, 1988), linking bastion forts, drill and the rise of the state; Jeremy Black and others have questioned how revolutionary and how European it was.

What’s missing1993

The book is overwhelmingly about land war. Sea power, which Keegan had treated in The Price of Admiralty (1988), and air power get a few pages each, and the pre-Columbian Americas, Africa beyond the Zulus, and South and South-East Asia much less than the steppe and Europe.

Terms of art

Glossary

Clausewitzian war
War as a rational instrument of state policy, fought by national armies. Keegan dates its heyday to roughly 1800–1945.
Real war / true war
Clausewitz’s distinction between war as it is, limited by friction and circumstance, and war in its ideal, absolute form.
Military horizon
Turney-High’s line between primitive fighting and organised war with command, supply and tactics.
Primitive war
Keegan’s term for fighting in societies without states: raids, ambushes and ceremonial battles, usually limited by exemption, convention and ritual.
Flower war
Aztec warfare arranged to take captives for sacrifice rather than to destroy the enemy.
Chariot
A light, two-wheeled, horse-drawn fighting vehicle with spoked wheels. It carried warrior elites to power across Eurasia from about 1700 BC.
Composite bow
A short, powerful bow of wood, horn and sinew, the horse archer’s weapon from the Scythians to the Manchus.
Mameluke
A slave soldier, especially of the elite that ruled Egypt and Syria from 1250 until the Ottoman conquest of 1517.
Janissary, devshirme
An Ottoman infantryman, and the levy of Christian boys from the Balkans by which janissaries were recruited.
Ghazi
An Islamic frontier warrior fighting the unbeliever.
Iqta
A revocable grant of tax revenue to an Islamic soldier in return for service.
Fief, fealty
Land granted for military service in feudal Europe, hereditary from the ninth century, and the vassal’s sworn loyalty.
Military participation ratio
Andreski’s measure: the share of a population under arms. Keegan treats about ten per cent as the most a society can sustain.
Hoplite, phalanx
A Greek citizen heavy infantryman, named from his hoplon shield, and the dense formation, usually eight deep, in which hoplites fought.
Othismos
The shield-to-shield push in which two phalanxes tried to break each other.
Sarissa
The long Macedonian pike that let Philip and Alexander deepen the phalanx.
Legion, maniple, centurion
The Roman division, its manoeuvring sub-units, and the long-service officers who led them, “the first body of professional fighting officers known to history”.
Limes, limitanei
A Roman fortified frontier of walls, roads and forts, and the frontier troops who garrisoned it in the late empire.
Federates
Barbarian units serving Rome as allies under their own leaders, admitted in quantity by Theodosius.
Bastion, trace italienne
The low, thick, angled fortification developed in Italy after 1494 to resist cannon, and the system of such forts.
Parallels
Trenches dug parallel to a fortress front to bring siege guns closer; Vauban settled on three.
Levée en masse
Revolutionary France’s decree of 23 August 1793 putting all fit men at the Republic’s disposal.
Materialschlacht
“Battle of materials”: the attrition of men by industrial output that decided the First World War.
Blitzkrieg
“Lightning war”: massed tanks and dive-bombers, directed by radio, breaking a front at one point and driving deep.
Area bombing
Bombing whole cities to break civilian morale, adopted by RAF Bomber Command in February 1942.
Mutually assured destruction
Deterrence by keeping, even after a surprise attack, the ability to inflict unacceptable damage on any aggressor.
Ius ad bellum, ius in bello
The law of when war may be waged, and of what is permitted once it has begun.
“Oriental” warmaking
Keegan’s contested label for a tradition of evasion, delay and indirectness, restrained by law, custom and faith.
Western way of war
The tradition of seeking decisive, face-to-face battle, traced from the Greeks, joined to holy-war ideology and technological change.
Rasputitsa
The Russian season of mud, spring and autumn, that halts armies for about a month.

Using it

A reading path

The book runs to some 400 pages; the chapters are long and the interludes short. If you won’t read it cover to cover:

  1. Introduction and Chapter 1

    The warrior tribe, the case against Clausewitz, and the four case studies. Everything else depends on them.

  2. Chapter 3

    Flesh: the horse peoples, the book’s most original stretch, and the source of its “Oriental” tradition.

  3. Chapter 4, the phalanx sections

    The Greeks and the birth of decisive battle, then Rome’s professional army.

  4. Interludes iii and iv

    Armies and supply: the shortest way into how war and society fit together.

  5. Chapter 5 and the Conclusion

    From gunpowder to the bomb, and the case that war can be unlearned.

Read alongside

  • Carl von Clausewitz, On War (1832; Howard and Paret translation, 1976): the book this one argues with.
  • John Keegan, The Face of Battle (1976): what combat was like at Agincourt, Waterloo and the Somme.
  • Victor Davis Hanson, The Western Way of War (1989): the hoplite argument behind Chapter 4.
  • William H. McNeill, The Pursuit of Power (1982): technology, armed force and society since AD 1000.
  • Geoffrey Parker, The Military Revolution (1988): bastions, drill and the rise of the West.
  • Lawrence H. Keeley, War Before Civilization (1996): the strongest challenge to the picture of primitive war.
  • Azar Gat, War in Human Civilization (2006): the evolutionary account.
  • John A. Lynn, Battle: A History of Combat and Culture (2003): culture without a single Western way.

How this summary was made

Checked chapter by chapter against the text of the Vintage edition (1994) in a public scan. Quotations are the book’s own; where the scan garbles a date or a passage, we say so or leave it out. Figures are our own drawings of the book’s numbers, not reproductions.

Where the book itself slips, the summary gives the usual date and notes it:

  • The Mameluke defeats at Marj Dabiq and Raydaniya were in 1516 and 1517; the book puts them a year early.
  • Qadesh is usually dated 1274 BC (the book: 1294 BC); the Pyrrhic battles of Asculum and Beneventum were in 279 and 275 BC (the book: 299 and 295).
  • The Iron chapter dates Adrianople to 396, not 378, and our scan gives Belisarius’s and Narses’s victories impossible dates; they were 533 and 552.
  • In the Fire chapter the book has Cortés landing in 1517 (1519), the Meiji reforms from 1866 (1868), Alexander defeating “Xerxes” at Gaugamela (it was Darius III), and Poland’s air force “wiped out in the first day”, an old myth: most Polish aircraft had been dispersed and fought on for two weeks.
  • It places the legion VII Gemina on the Rhine; its long-term base was in Spain.
