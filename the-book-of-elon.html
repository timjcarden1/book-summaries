The Book of Elon

A Guide to Purpose and Success

“Physics is law. Everything else is a recommendation.”

Musk, in the chapter “Obsess over Truth”, and the idea the book is built on

Compiled by Eric Jorgenson from Elon Musk’s own words · 2026 · foreword by Naval Ravikant

The whole book in one breath

Pick work by how many people it helps and by how much. Treat requirements, prices, schedules and industry habit as claims to be rebuilt from physics, because they usually collapse when you try: a rocket’s materials cost 1 or 2 per cent of its price. Then run a five-step procedure in the right order: make the requirements less dumb, delete, simplify, accelerate, and only then automate. Hire exceptional people, point them at a goal, let information take the shortest path, sleep on the factory floor, and move with maniacal urgency, because the factory, not the design, is the hard part. The same method, applied to cars, rockets and satellites, did what those industries called impossible, and Part IV turns it on the whole species: abundance from robots and AI, a register of risks that could end civilisation, and a million tonnes of cargo to Mars as life insurance. The book never notices that the method did far less well where the problem was a research breakthrough, a regulator or what people want, and that the dates slipped in both cases.

Orientation

What this book actually is

Not a biography and not reportage. Eric Jorgenson, who did the same job for Naval Ravikant and later for Balaji Srinivasan, built it “entirely out of transcripts, tweets, and interviews over his entire life to date” and reassembled the remarks by subject, so scattered answers read as one continuous argument. The notes at the front say the transcripts were edited for clarity, brevity and flow several times; that given fakes, edits and misquotes he can’t vouch for every source; that everything is taken out of context; and that readers should check phrasing against a primary source before citing Musk. Interview questions appear in bold, a few written by Jorgenson for context, and highlighted pull-quotes let you skim. It is, like the Almanack, a “choose-your-own-adventure book”.

His welcome says he put thousands of hours into it, with Musk’s permission and encouragement, and states the aim plainly: “One million Musks. That’s my dream for this book.” It spends “zero pages on Elon’s family life or politics”. It was published by Scribe Media on 24 March 2026, with illustrations by Jack Butcher, and a free edition is on the book’s website. As a manual it is dense; as evidence it needs a discount throughout: a subject narrating his own successes, arranged by an admirer.

What Naval Ravikant’s foreword says

“You’re holding in your hands the only book an entrepreneur needs.” Founders can’t hire the best mentors, books are anecdotal or academic, and the only reliable teacher is bitter experience; Jorgenson “put his ego aside” to compile the lessons as a manual, not a biography. Musk is “the greatest entrepreneur since Steve Jobs”, an occasional Napoleon who reroutes history: “Hard work is common, intelligence uncommon, courage rare—and Elon combusts all three.” Critics who point to his father’s or the government’s money are “crabs in the gravity well”. Borrowing David Deutsch, Naval defines wealth as the set of physical transformations we can effect, mostly made of knowledge, not capital. He closes with a challenge (when humanity reached Mars, were you cheering or jeering?) and a line: “Don’t make the thing to make the money, make the money so that you can make the thing.”

If you remember nothing else

Twelve things to keep

Choosing work

Usefulness is arithmetic

How many people you help, times how much you help each. A good ordinary product counts.

Method

Rebuild the big decisions from physics

Reason by analogy for daily life; for the few decisions that decide what is possible, start from what you are most confident is true.

Cost

The idiot index

A part’s price divided by its materials’ price. A $13,000 nozzle jacket made from $200 of steel scores 65.

Limits

Think in the limit

Would it still be expensive at a million units a year? If so, volume isn’t the problem; the design is.

Procedure

Delete before you optimise

“The most common mistake of smart engineers is to optimize a thing that should not exist.”

Organisation

The org chart shows up in the product

Two teams each sure they need an enclosure give you a box inside a box.

Speed

Run things in parallel

Most things have a gestation you can’t shorten; start them all at once. “If a timeline is long, it’s wrong.”

Manufacturing

The factory is the product

Prototypes are easy; production is hard. The line moves at the speed of its least lucky part.

Strategy

A roadmap that is a financing plan

Start where a new technology can be expensive; let each generation pay for the next one’s scale.

Reuse

Only the propellant should be consumed

A fully and rapidly reusable rocket costs about its fuel, roughly a million dollars a flight.

Risk

Rank risks by irreversibility

Avoid what could end civilisation before what is merely tragic: war, misaligned AI, falling birth rates, one planet.

The reader’s discount

Trust the direction, not the date

“Some dates are not dates that will actually be met.” Research and permission problems arrived much later than manufacturing ones.

Part I

Pursue purpose

“The foundations of a unique life”: a utilitarian test for what to work on, a physicist’s way of thinking about it, and a defence of engineering as what turns thought into consequence. Jorgenson opens it with Rutherford: “All of physics is either impossible or trivial.”

Part I · Section 1

Living a Purposeful Life

Choose to be extraordinary, measure yourself by usefulness, work on what excites you about the future, and accept the fear and the pain that come with it.

Be useful

  • “You can choose to be not ordinary.” His measure of success is how many useful things he gets done; each morning he asks how he can be useful today. “Don’t aspire to glory; aspire to work.”
  • Usefulness is arithmetic: how many people you helped, times how much you helped each, “almost like the physics definition of true work”. A big difference for a few people equals a small difference for very many, so a simple app that makes many lives slightly better is good work, and not every product needs to change the world (see the figure).
  • His advice to his own children: follow your heart in what you find interesting, work extremely hard, and add more than you take from the world. “A useful life is worth having lived.”

Fight for the future

  • “The future will not get here fast enough unless we force it.” He nearly had an existential crisis about what it all means, and concluded that expanding the scope and scale of consciousness is the way to ask better questions.
  • In college, thinking in the shower rather than for a paper, he listed five areas that would matter most to humanity’s future. Three looked clearly good: the internet, sustainable energy, and space exploration, especially life on several planets. Two looked double-edged: artificial intelligence and rewriting genetics. With the PayPal money he eventually worked on all three good ones.
  • His core skill, he says, is technology: designing or inventing things whose value exceeds the cost of their inputs. He sees the future as “a branching stream of probabilities” that actions can shift, and says he isn’t trying to be a saviour; the importance just seems obvious. “Remember the future.”

Obsess for success; start before the world is ready

  • Don’t start a company to be an entrepreneur or to make money; ask what useful thing you wish existed. He says he never asked what gave the best risk-adjusted return, only what needed to happen. Find the overlap of talent and interest; if there is one trait that makes founders, it is being obsessive about product quality. “Make sure you really care about what you’re doing—and take the pain.”
  • Radically new products have no expressed demand. A survey around 1946–48 asked Americans if they would ever buy a television and about 96% said no; at Tesla, nobody ever said they wanted an electric car. “If you need encouragement, don’t start a company.”
  • Create more than you consume. The economy is positive-sum; output per person has grown far faster than population. People who behave badly usually have a zero-sum mindset without realising it, believing the only way to get more pie is to take someone else’s.

Work like hell

  • “I am wired for war.” Anyone who wants to be him is “probably mistaken”: long periods of his life have been very painful, and “you need to have some kind of rage demon in your skull that drives you.” He works all but two or three days a year.
  • His arithmetic: at a hundred hours a week against someone else’s fifty, you get twice as much done; against forty, a year’s work in four months. “Nobody ever changed the world on forty hours a week.”
  • The bill is in the book too. 2007 to 2022 was “nonstop pain”, a gun to his head to make Tesla work, a stream of rabbits pulled from a hat. To his twenty-something self he would say there is merit in not being too intense; on Kwajalein Atoll while developing Falcon 1 he never once had a drink on the beach with the team, and now thinks he should have. “If heat death will inevitably end the universe, it actually is all about the journey.”

Feel the fear; do it anyway

  • He feels fear “quite strongly”; if you don’t, something is wrong with you. When a thing is important enough, you do it anyway. Fatalism helps: accepting the real odds (under 10% for SpaceX) diminishes fear, and even failure might move the ball forward for someone else.
  • “We should not be afraid of doing something just because some amount of tragedy is likely to occur. If our forefathers had taken that approach, the United States wouldn’t exist.” Where does he find strength? He doesn’t need any: it must get done, “or die trying.”

Seek the nature of the universe

  • He doesn’t know the meaning of life and doubts anyone can yet. Written civilisation is about five thousand years old; if it lasts a million more, we are “ancient ancients”. Physics explains the chain from quarks to hydrogen to us over 13.8 billion years, but not why there is a universe, nor where consciousness began: “if you leave enough hydrogen gas sitting around long enough, it starts talking to itself.”
  • His “philosophy of curiosity”: total consciousness is population times consciousness per person, so more people and more digital intelligence improve our chance of finding the right questions. From Douglas Adams he takes the idea that the universe is the answer and the hard part is the question.

“A useful life is worth having lived.”Be Useful

Part I · Section 2

Think Like a Physicist

Obsess over truth, reason from first principles for the decisions that matter, push variables to their limits, and aim to be less wrong.

Obsess over truth

  • “I try to be hyper-rational.” If the reasoning fits and breaks no law of physics, try it. He calls himself obsessed, “pathologically”, with truth, which is why he studied physics (and computer science and information theory, which may be more fundamental still).
  • “Physics is law. Everything else is a recommendation. I’ve met many people who can break the laws of man, but I have never met anyone who could break the laws of physics.” “If you have beliefs that are incompatible with a rocket getting to orbit, the rocket will not get to orbit.”
  • Wishful thinking causes most mistakes; if something feels too easy, it probably is. Check your axioms and your conclusions, look for feedback from every source, and “always assume we’re losing, even when it looks like we might win.”

First-principles thinking

  • Most of life should be reasoned by analogy, or you couldn’t get through the day; analogy gives slight iterations. For important new things it binds you to convention (“It’s always been done this way”). The procedure: ask what you are most confident is true at a foundational level, reason up from it, check the conclusion against it. First test for any technology: does it violate conservation of energy or momentum?
  • Batteries. Packs were assumed to cost $600 per kilowatt-hour forever. What are they made of? Cobalt, nickel, aluminium, carbon, some polymer separators, a steel can. Priced on the London Metal Exchange, the materials came to about $80 per kilowatt-hour (see the figure).
  • Rockets. Aluminium, titanium, copper and carbon fibre: if a magic wand could arrange the atoms for free, what would a rocket cost? His “magic wand number” was “well under 5 percent of the current cost, in some cases closer to 1 or 2 percent”, so the manufacturing had to be very inefficient. The method doesn’t prove success, only “if success is one of the possibilities”.
  • The idiot index generalises it: how much more a finished part costs than its materials. A $1,000 part made from $10 of aluminium probably has a bad design or process. One rocket part, the half nozzle jacket, cost $13,000 and contained $200 of steel. He expects his engineers to know the best and worst parts in their systems by idiot index at all times.

Thinking in limits

  • Scale an idea to a very large or small number and see what changes. Critics of tunnels say they’ll fill up too; but there is no real limit to how many levels you can dig, and mines go deeper than buildings go high. “Cities are built in 3D, but roads are only built in 2D.”
  • Tunnelling is too expensive (the Los Angeles subway extension cost roughly a billion dollars a mile), so he works the limits (see the figure): shrink the diameter, tunnel and reinforce continuously, and turn up the power.
  • In manufacturing: would the part still be expensive at a million units a year? If so, volume isn’t the problem; the design is. At high volume anything approaches the cost of its raw materials plus licensed IP.
  • In design, don’t only start from familiar tools; imagine the platonic ideal, the perfect arrangement of atoms, and work out how to get them into that shape. “The word impossible is more or less banned in physics.” When a team says something is impossible, he asks, “What would it take?”

Aspire to be less wrong

  • Assume you’re wrong and aim to be less wrong. “It’s OK to be wrong. Just don’t be confident and wrong.” Believe things in proportion to the evidence.
  • Read, because reading beats a speaker’s few hundred bits per second; he skipped college lectures for that reason. At nine or ten he ran out of books at home and read the encyclopaedia, and still recommends skimming the concise Britannica to get the lay of the land. Civilization taught him the technology tree: no democracy without literacy. Treat knowledge as a semantic tree, trunk and branches before leaves.
  • He taught himself rocketry from books, the Palo Alto library and experts’ old engine manuals; he had never seen a CNC machine. The Concorde’s retirement set him calculating whether an electric, vertical-take-off supersonic jet could be economic. “Most people self-limit their ability to learn.”

“It’s OK to be wrong. Just don’t be confident and wrong.”Aspire to Be Less Wrong

Part I · Section 3

The Value of Engineering

Engineering is magic, it wins wars, and it is the only thing that creates value; ideas are cheap and execution is everything.

Engineering is magic

  • He spends 80% of his time on engineering. His father, “an extremely talented electrical and mechanical engineer”, built model aeroplanes and circuit boards with him; he mixed his own rocket fuel from the chemist because South Africa sold no model rockets. Scared of the dark as a child, he learned dark was only the absence of photons between 400 and 700 nanometres, and stopped being scared.
  • At about twelve he taught himself programming from the books that came with the family computer and sold his own games to buy new ones: “I was nerdmaster3000.” Arthur C. Clarke’s line that sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic made invention look like being a magician.
  • He considered physics as a career but concluded engineering is the limiting factor: without new instruments there is no new data, as Galileo’s telescope showed. “Science is discovering the essential truths about what exists in the universe. Engineering is about creating things that have never existed before.”

Engineering wins wars

  • The Art of War, which he has read many times, lacks a chapter on technology: a decisive technological edge wins with minimal casualties. Roman metallurgy and roads beat their enemies, while mounted Scythian archers beat the Romans on open ground.
  • In the Pacific early in the Second World War, American squadrons were sometimes shot down with zero Japanese losses; within a few years the US dominated. What matters is the pace of innovation. The limit case: lasers from space would beat Caesar, Guderian or Napoleon alike. The Manhattan Project, he stresses, was the physics community’s creation more than the government’s. “Play to win, or don’t play at all.”

Engineering creates value

  • “Innovation is not the problem. Execution is the problem.” He has more ideas than he could execute; ideas are nearly irrelevant. “Prototypes are easy; production is hard. Production and being cash flow positive is excruciating pain.”
  • Value comes from solving hard problems. His evidence that Tesla does hard engineering: coils of aluminium and plastic pellets go in one end and cars come out the other, and Toyota, Daimler and Mercedes bought electric powertrains from it. “The idea of going to Mars is not hard; that’s irrelevant. Getting to Mars is the hard part.”

Part II

Ultra hardcore work

“Mindsets and principles for achieving the barely possible”: the operating core, and the part most worth extracting. What leadership costs, how teams are chosen and wired, the five-step Algorithm, speed, and the factory.

Part II · Section 4

What It Takes

A chief executive’s job is a distillation of everything going wrong; the pain is the role, and it is shared in public.

Take responsibility; earn deep understanding

  • He is chief executive because he feels responsible, not because it suits his quality of life, and says he gets far too much credit. What the job really is: “a distillation of the worst things going on in the company”, the problems nobody else can fix. Failure “is a punch in the gut”; he rallies the company and feels terrible. “Given the options, I prefer to learn from success.”
  • He studied physics and economics, the latter so he would never have to work for someone who knew things he didn’t, or “for a boss with a business degree”; after physics maths, business-school matrix maths was easy.
  • SpaceX moved fast because engineering and spending decisions sat “in one brain”, with no engineer trying to convince a finance person who can’t judge the case: “My brain trusts itself.” CEOs who don’t know their technology’s details strike him as ridiculous.

Sleep on the factory floor; frontline leadership

  • He has done many true hundred-hour weeks on about six hours’ sleep a night, and doesn’t recommend it except in emergencies. In a crisis he slept on the factory floor outside the conference room, not inside it, “Otherwise how would people know?”, and woke smelling of oil and iron filings.
  • “Nobody bleeds for the prince in the palace.” Never ask troops to do what you won’t; he tries the front-line tasks himself. No executive privileges (same parking, same tables, no management offices; he has no office), managers serve their teams, and technical managers must do technical work: software managers code at least 20% of their time, solar-roof managers go up on roofs.
  • “Always be smashing your ego.” A high ego-to-ability ratio breaks your feedback loop with reality, like breaking an AI’s reinforcement-learning loop.

Adversity forges strength; eat glass and stare into the abyss

  • At six, grounded unjustly, he escaped his nanny and walked ten or twelve miles across Pretoria to a cousin’s party, barely able to read the road signs; he climbed a tree and wouldn’t come down until his mother promised not to punish him. At five or six he thought he was insane, because other minds didn’t seem to explode with ideas all the time.
  • The youngest and smallest in his grade and “kind of a smart aleck”, he was bullied by gangs, partly for sticking up for another boy, in “real hardcore street fights, from about six to sixteen”, once almost beaten to death. Around sixteen, grown, the attacks stopped. “People worried about words have never been punched in the face.” His pain threshold became very high.
  • His friend Bill Lee: “Starting a company is like eating glass and staring into the abyss.” The abyss is constant threat of death (90% or even 99% of start-ups fail); the glass is working on the problems the company needs, not the ones you want. SpaceX, Tesla and SolarCity faced imminent death for years. Reconcile yourself to likely failure and do it only if still compelled; but what’s the worst that could happen? You won’t starve.

Part II · Section 5

Building Exceptional Teams

A company is a group of people with a goal, so its output is the vector sum of their efforts; recruit for exceptional ability, keep only the excellent, and put feedback before feelings.

A group with a goal; a culture of builders

  • “There’s no such thing as ‘a business,’ just a group pursuing a goal”, “a cybernetic collective of people and machines” smarter than any individual. So attract great people, and show them a working prototype as early as possible: “Anything can look good on PowerPoint.”
  • He doesn’t manage smart people; they manage themselves. He states the goal, asks if they agree, gives opinions, and only rarely says “you have to trust me on this one.” Everyone should think like the chief engineer, enough to spot a bad optimisation.
  • “The output of any company is the vector sum of the people within it.” “A small group of technically strong people will always beat a large group of moderately strong people.” Much of Silicon Valley’s engineering talent is underused through comfort; Tesla is demanding. “Only exceptional performance constitutes a passing grade.”

Recruit for exceptional ability; retain only special forces

  • Would Tesla even interview Nikola Tesla, with his odd mannerisms and Eastern European college? Look for “evidence of exceptional ability, or at least exceptional aspiration”, including brilliant people who never finished a degree. He recruited Bülent Altan by getting Larry Page to transfer Altan’s wife from Google’s San Francisco office to Los Angeles.
  • His interview: tell me your career and the hardest problems you solved. Whoever really solved one remembers the details.
  • He couldn’t hire a good chief engineer for the first rocket, so became it himself: “If I could have found somebody better, maybe we would have had fewer than three failures.” Start-ups should run like special forces. Money isn’t the constraint; there is no factory for excellent engineers.
  • He admits hiring for intellect over heart and calls that a mistake: judge character by a person’s friends, and by their enemies. “Skills can be taught. Attitude changes require a brain transplant.”

Feedback over feelings

  • “Physics does not care about hurt feelings.” Bad news loudly and often, good news quietly and once. Criticise the action, not the person.
  • A manager who never fired anyone was told you can’t demand people get their act together and then let nothing happen. Camaraderie makes people reluctant to challenge each other; caring about one person’s feelings can hurt many. “I think it’s a real weakness to want to be liked… And I do not have that.”

Part II · Section 6

Designing the Organization

Information should take the shortest path, words should be plain, failure must be survivable, parts should be few, and the Algorithm runs in order.

Remove organisational boundaries

  • “In any product, you can see the errors in the organization’s structure.” Chain-of-command communication (engineer to manager to director to VP to VP to director to manager to engineer, and back) enhances managers’ power and fails the company; anyone may talk to anyone, including him, and is obliged to until the right thing happens (see the figure). Managers who enforce the chain “will soon find themselves working elsewhere”.
  • The Model 3 case: the battery pack had its own top cover and the car an underbody right above it, because one team enclosed the pack and another the body. The cover added mass and cost and was deleted. Keep design, engineering and manufacturing together so the line can grab a designer and ask “WHY DID YOU MAKE IT THIS WAY!?”; “Let the manufacturers put the designers’ hands on the stove too.”
  • “Go as close to the source as possible.” On Starship’s tank walls he asked the welders what was safe. They said 4.8 millimetres; he asked about 4; they said that would make them nervous; he said let’s try 4, “It worked.”

Simple communication; innovation needs permission to fail

  • Made-up acronyms were creeping into SpaceX and Tesla; nobody admits not knowing them, so they sit in ignorance. He ordered them stopped unless he approved: GUI is fine, “MVac” and “M9” for Merlin engines were allowed, the rest go.
  • “Failure is essentially irrelevant unless it is catastrophic.” If failure is not an option, choices become so conservative things go backward. He didn’t fire anyone for the early SpaceX failures; letting people go is fair only if they can’t commit to the mission. Keep the penalty for failure low and let technologies compete in a Darwinian way. “If we’re not occasionally blowing up an engine on the test stand, we’re not trying hard enough.”
  • No test stand can reproduce 17,000 mph at six g in every orientation, so hundreds, even thousands, of changes follow every Starship flight, and it takes many flights to tell a one-in-ten failure from a one-in-a-hundred.

Simplicity wins

  • “Never use a cruise missile to kill a fly.” Fewer parts means fewer things to buy and to break. Lines of code are not a figure of merit: one point for adding one, two for deleting one. If a company rule would make a good Dilbert cartoon, change it.
  • The Model 3 body line built for 5,000 cars a week reached 7,000 by removing things. At the Gigafactory he replaced a robot–turntable–robot handoff with robot to robot: “The best part is no part. The best process is no process.”
  • Fifty parts each within 0.2 millimetres still stack up into a large variance between cars, and fifty engineers each choosing the best material for one part gave “right answers to the wrong questions” (a “Frankenstein situation” of rivets, welds and sealants). Casting the Model Y rear body in one piece cut the body shop by 30% and removed 300 of roughly a thousand robots.

The Algorithm

  • “I became a broken record on The Algorithm.” Five steps, in order: make your requirements less dumb; try very hard to delete the part or process; simplify or optimise; accelerate; automate.
  • His own case of running it backwards (see the figure): fibreglass mats on top of the Model 3 battery pack. The robot was made faster (up 20%, then 100%), the glue and drying optimised; then he asked what the mats were for. The battery team said noise and vibration; the noise-and-vibration team said fire safety. A test with microphones in cars with and without them found no difference, and deleting them removed a step with $2 million of robotics.
  • Step one. “Your requirements are definitely dumb. It does not matter who gave them to you.” Requirements from smart people are most dangerous, because nobody questions them. Every requirement must come from a named person, not a department, or it may be an intern’s offhand remark from two years ago.
  • Step two. “If you’re not adding deleted things back in 10 percent of the time, you’re clearly not deleting enough.” He warns teams in advance that at least one in ten deletions will come back. On a SpaceX “deletion rampage” nothing was sacred. Mass is recursive: an extra tonne needs fuel to reach orbit, to deorbit and to land, plus structure to carry it; SpaceX calculates a factor of about 1.8, probably nearer 2.
  • Step three, not step one: “The most common mistake of smart engineers is to optimize a thing that should not exist”, because school trains everyone to answer the question in front of them. Step four: then go faster, but “If you’re digging your grave, don’t dig it faster.” Step five: automate last. Automating too early in Nevada and Fremont meant tearing out hundreds of robots, through a hole cut in the side of the building.

Part II · Section 7

Maniacal Urgency

Time is the one thing you can’t replace, so speed is both attack and defence, work runs in parallel, and timelines are set as aggressively as possible.

Don’t waste time; speed is offence and defence

  • “A maniacal sense of urgency is our operating principle.” Cut large and frequent meetings, and walk out as soon as you add nothing: “It is not rude to leave; it is rude to make someone stay and waste their time.”
  • The SR-71 Blackbird’s only defence was speed; he says over three thousand missiles were fired at it and none hit. Real intellectual-property protection is innovating so fast that copiers copy what you did years ago. “A factory moving at twice the speed of another factory is basically equivalent to two factories.”
  • Early SpaceX burned $100,000 a day, and he expected $10 million a day of revenue in ten years, so every day of delay had a price. “You need to be a vector, not just a scalar.” At Tesla, with about $2 billion of weekly revenue, a half-hour meeting has sometimes been worth $100 million.

Do things in parallel; break down the “impossible”

  • PayPal’s links to card processors, the Federal Reserve’s transfer system and fraud databases were all built in parallel with the software, so everything came together in about a year. Avoid serialised dependencies. “If a timeline is long, it’s wrong.”
  • Colossus. In 2024 xAI needed a training cluster of 100,000 H100 chips; suppliers estimated eighteen to twenty-four months, and xAI needed six. They broke it down: a disused factory in Memphis instead of a new building; rented generators to take its 15 megawatts to the 150 needed; about a quarter of America’s mobile cooling capacity in rented chillers; Tesla Megapacks, reprogrammed, to smooth power swings of 50% in 100 milliseconds; networking crews in four shifts round the clock, with Musk sleeping in the data centre and cabling himself. Built in 122 days, doubled to 200,000 chips ninety-two days later (see the figure).

Set aggressive timelines

  • “It’s okay to scrap equipment or money. It’s not okay to scrap time.” He hired a vice-president of machining at 5 p.m. on a Saturday, contract ten minutes after agreeing the price, and the man started that night.
  • Internal timelines are as aggressive as possible because schedules obey “a kind of ‘law of gaseous expansion’”. When he cites a schedule it is one he believes, “It may be delusional… But it’s never some knowingly fake deadline, ever.” Yet he also says some dates are not meant to be met: the first Model 3 date assumed all 7,000 unique components would arrive on time, and was set to hold the company and its suppliers to.
  • He calls himself optimistic with schedules and argues that exponential ramps fool people: 2017’s small area under the curve became 2018, when Tesla doubled its cumulative fleet. The media, he says, list his missed predictions and ignore the right ones; “For radical technology predictions, the point is not that it was a few years late, but that it happened at all.” “I may be a little optimistic, but I always deliver.” (For a check, see Scoring the record.)

Part II · Section 8

We Must Make Stuff

If we don’t make stuff, there is no stuff; designing is easy, the factory is the product, the constraint sets the pace, and manufacturing is the moat.

The real work

  • “Now, let me break it to the fools out there. If we don’t make stuff, there’s no stuff.” The idea that goods come from a magic source, or that governments can send cheques and all will be well, is detached from reality. Technology isn’t inevitable; humans make it.
  • Too many smart people go into finance and law, “both a compliment and a criticism”; manufacturing used to be valued in America and should be again. “I’ve got mad respect for the makers of things.”

The factory is the product; attack the constraint

  • His biggest epiphany building Tesla: what matters is “the machine that builds the machines”. A first-principles analysis suggests five- to tenfold improvement in car production by version three on about a two-year cycle. Tesla, unlike Apple and Google, believes there is more room for innovation in manufacturing than in the design of a car.
  • “Designing a rocket is trivial”; getting one to orbit is very hard. The eureka-then-copies view “is completely false”. A line moves as fast as the slowest, least lucky of ten thousand things; his suppliers have suffered a fire, an earthquake, a tsunami, hail, a tornado, a sunk ship and a shoot-out at the Mexican border (“That delayed trunk carpet”). At SpaceX they spent ten to a hundred times more effort on the manufacturing system than on the Raptor engine.

Manufacturing is the moat

  • Competitiveness is scale times technology, which is why plants are “so freaking giant”. The Texas plant takes in rail cars of raw material and forms cells, packs, motors and castings.
  • The giga-casting idea came from toy cars: toys are cheap because they’re cast. Could a casting machine be built big enough for a car? No one ever had; were they breaking any laws of physics? No. Of the six major casting-machine makers, five said no and one said maybe: “I’ll take that as a yes.”

Part III

Building companies

“Stories and strategies from PayPal, Tesla, and SpaceX”: the narrative half, where the method is arrived at rather than stated, often by being overruled. “What fuels your creativity?” “Pressure. Necessity.”

Part III · Section 9

Becoming a Founder

Zip2 taught him to sell direct and PayPal to follow the customer; both lessons came through losing control, and both fortunes went straight back on the table.

Becoming a founder

  • “You want to embark on something where success is certain to be one of the possible outcomes.” At seventeen he got a Canadian passport through his mother and three weeks later landed in Montreal with a backpack, a suitcase of books and $2,000.
  • A $100 bus ticket took him across Canada: six weeks on a cousin’s wheat farm in Swift Current, Saskatchewan, then a lumber mill near Vancouver, chainsawing logs and crawling through a tunnel in a hazmat suit to shovel steaming sand out of the pulp boiler, “the hardest job I’ve had”, chosen because it paid $18 an hour when others paid under $8.
  • Queen’s University, then the University of Pennsylvania, paying his own way: he left Stanford graduate school with $110,000 of student debt. He dropped the idea of particle physics when the US supercollider was cancelled, since a government could cancel his career too.
  • He went to Stanford to study materials science and the physics of high-energy-density capacitors for electric cars, but feared the work would be academically useful and practically useless (“There goes seven years of my life”). In 1995 the internet was clearly happening; an internet company had success among its possible outcomes, and software needed almost no capital. He wanted to help build “humanity’s nervous system”.

Starting Zip2

  • “Our ambitions were quite low: make enough money to pay rent.” He first tried for a job at Netscape; they didn’t answer his emails and he was too shy to speak to anyone in their lobby.
  • Zip2 built maps, directions and classified ads, he believes the first maps and directions on the internet (Jorgenson adds the patent number). He wrote all the initial code; his brother joined with about $5,000; they had one computer, so the site went down whenever he compiled. He slept on the office futon and showered at the YMCA, and they drilled a hole in the floor to borrow the downstairs internet provider’s connection for $100 a month.
  • Most venture capitalists passed (“Who’s made money on the internet? No one”). After Netscape’s IPO, Mohr Davidow invested $3 million for 60%. Knight Ridder, the New York Times Company and Hearst became investors and customers, and the newspapers took so many board seats that they forced the software through channels that wouldn’t use it: “like building F-22 fighter jets and selling them to people who roll them down the hill at each other.” Lesson: sell straight to the end consumer.
  • Compaq bought Zip2 in early 1999 for “a little over $300 million in cash”; a cheque came to the house he shared with four housemates, and his account went “from $5,000 to $21,005,000”. He took no time off and put almost all of it into X.com.

Going all in again; listen well, correct fast

  • What was digital and low-bandwidth in 1999? Money. “Money is a database for resource allocation across time and space”: useless to a castaway on an island with a trillion dollars. A good money system, like a good internet connection, has high bandwidth, low latency and few errors, and 1990s finance was “a herky-jerky frickin’ monstrosity” of mainframes and batch processing.
  • He put $12.5 million into X.com. Sequoia’s Mike Moritz invested $5 million, told him to hire a CEO (“Great, I don’t want to be CEO”) and warned him not to put in everything but his house and car. “I kept the chips on the table.”
  • X.com was meant to combine all financial services; demos of that bored people, and the easy feature, payment by email, excited them, so they followed the customers. PayPal had perhaps thirty engineers, a flat structure, equity for everyone, “best idea wins”, and a rule: when two options are about equal, pick one and go. “Pay close attention to negative feedback, and solicit it, particularly from friends.”

Unite and conquer; from exile to exit

  • Confinity (Peter Thiel, Max Levchin, Luke Nosek, David Sacks, Ken Howery) was a block away in Palo Alto, at one point in the same building; X.com had Jeremy Stoppelman and Roelof Botha. Over coffee on University Avenue they merged rather than “bludgeon each other to death”, raised $100 million in three weeks in March 2000 at a $500 million valuation, and in April the market collapsed.
  • Growth was viral: $20 for opening an account and $20 per referral, then $10, then $5, perhaps $60–70 million in incentives in all, with no sales force or advertising, and a million customers by the end of year two. Regulators, the FTC, Visa, MasterCard and eBay all tried to shut it down; it nearly died in 2000 and 2001.
  • On a combined fundraising trip and belated honeymoon he was removed as chief executive; he had proposed ideas that looked too risky while companies were “dropping like flies”. He conceded rather than fight, made peace (“Life is too short for long-term grudges”), invested in Thiel’s Founders Fund, and Thiel later invested in SpaceX.
  • PayPal went public in February 2002; to retire the risk that eBay’s own payment system would win, it sold to eBay that October. In this chapter the book gives the price as “about $4.5 billion”; the deal was $1.5 billion in stock, the figure the book’s own timeline gives. “I haven’t spent my winnings.”

Part III · Section 10

Building Tesla

A mission argued from finite fuel, a founding built on the wrong assumptions, a plan that was really a financing staircase, and three near-deaths on the way to volume.

The mission to protect the planet

  • “There’s no way we can conserve our way to a good future.” Tesla’s purpose is to hasten the move from a “mine-and-burn hydrocarbon economy” to a solar-electric one. Energy output is the foundation of an economy, as sunlight is of an ecosystem.
  • The argument is built to survive disagreement: fossil fuels are finite, so the switch must come, and virtually all scientists agree that raising atmospheric carbon is unwise, so sooner is better. He compares doubters to the tobacco industry’s paid scientists (“99 percent of scientists think the other 1 percent are stupid”). He has given $100 million for a carbon-capture prize and expects abundant clean energy to desalinate water and even pull carbon back out of the air.

Going all in again; building the first prototype

  • Big technology changes come from new companies (Schumpeter’s creative destruction), and few entrepreneurs or investors go into cars, solar or space. He planned to use half his PayPal money; SpaceX was meant to need $50 million, Tesla $25–30 million.
  • He knew as a sophomore that all vehicles would be electric; lithium-ion cells, about four times the energy density of lead-acid, made it possible. AC Propulsion had a prototype but wanted to build an “electric Scion”; he offered to fund a tenth of it anyway, then asked if he could commercialise the tech himself.
  • “Almost every decision we made was wrong.” The plan (a Lotus Elise plus AC Propulsion’s drive unit) failed: the hand-built electronics couldn’t be industrialised, the chassis grew 40% heavier and voided Lotus’s crash tests, and in the end about 7% of the Roadster’s parts were shared with the Elise and none of the AC Propulsion technology survived. A demo for Larry Page and Sergey Brin ran at ten miles an hour; they invested anyway. Joining an existing group instead of starting fresh with JB Straubel was “a huge mistake”.

Becoming Tesla’s CEO

  • He wanted to work on product and let someone else run the business while rockets were exploding at SpaceX, and calls that “fundamentally a moral error”. Having provided about 95% of the money, he became chief executive (the timeline says 2008) because otherwise the company would have died. “You could not truly be the chief technology or product officer unless you were the CEO.”

Sequenced strategy of Tesla

  • The master plan (see the figure): build a sports car; use that money to build an affordable car; use that money to build an even more affordable car; meanwhile offer zero-emission power generation. The sports car came first because a new technology must enter at high cost and low volume, and it was all he could afford without risking others’ money.
  • “Starting a car company is idiotic, and an electric car company is idiocy squared.” Only two American carmakers never went bankrupt, Ford and Tesla; the hard thing isn’t a prototype, which hundreds of start-ups managed, but reaching volume production without bankruptcy, which no American car company had done since Chrysler in the 1920s.

Keeping Tesla alive; the edge of sanity

  • In 2008 all three companies nearly died together: Morgan Stanley reneged on a SolarCity deal, he was divorcing and being “dumped on, massively, in the press”. With $30–40 million left he chose not to let either Tesla or SpaceX starve and split the money between them. Tesla’s rescue round, matched by Antonio Gracias, Steve Jurvetson and Aaron Price, closed at 6 p.m. on Christmas Eve 2008, two days before payroll would have bounced; he was sleeping in Jeff Skoll’s spare room.
  • Daimler executives arrived expecting slides and were handed a Smart car with a Roadster motor and battery that hit 60 mph in four seconds; Daimler invested $50 million, without which “we would have died”. In 2008, paying suppliers on time would have bankrupted both companies.
  • The Model 3 line was messed up “in almost every aspect”, so everyone was moved onto it: “a giant flying leap at high volume” like Indiana Jones ahead of the boulder. From 2017 to 2019 he lived in the Fremont and Nevada factories, “the longest period of excruciating pain in my life”, and came out believing he knew more about manufacturing than anyone alive.

A whole new kind of car company; give people more for less

  • Traditional carmakers mostly manage suppliers and dealers; Tesla does its own sales and service (never a profit centre), battery packs, power electronics, drivetrain, software, AI and chips, because the legacy supply chain would impose legacy speed and cost. It sells insurance priced on how you actually drive. “Ultimately, what we induce other companies to do will have a greater impact than the cars we make ourselves.”
  • Beauty matters: combine aesthetics with utility (the hard part in a seven-seat SUV), make it feel bigger inside than out, and train yourself to notice why something looks right. LEGO holds a quarter-millimetre press fit in cheap plastic; so can a car.
  • New technology follows an S-curve, underpredicted early and overpredicted at the midpoint, and usually takes three major iterations to work well; the 1987 Wall Street phone with a briefcase battery couldn’t have been made cheap by scale alone. Every Roadster buyer helped pay for the family car.

The battle of public perception; founding SolarCity

  • A Tesla crash with a broken ankle makes the front page while tens of thousands of US road deaths a year get little coverage; Tesla doesn’t advertise, and legacy carmakers and oil companies are big advertisers. He claims EVs are far less likely to catch fire than petrol cars. His coping method: these writers don’t know him and want clicks. Sales come from word of mouth.
  • SolarCity began at Burning Man, when he urged his cousins, fresh from selling Everdream to Dell, to go into solar; he funded them. Panels are commodities (“about as hard as making drywall”); the hard problem is installing and managing hundreds of thousands of rooftop systems. Tesla absorbed SolarCity so that solar roof plus battery could be one order, one installation, one app.

Part III · Section 11

Building SpaceX

Electric cars were inevitable and space travel was not; a rocket company started to make Mars possible, run on first principles, three failures from death, and aimed at full reuse.

The only one crazy enough for space

  • “Building mass-market electric cars was inevitable. It would have happened without me. But becoming a space-faring civilization is not inevitable.” Year after year he found no date for Mars on NASA’s website; we landed on the Moon in 1969, left in 1972, and after the Shuttle retired the United States could send no one to orbit.
  • He first thought people had lost the will to explore; in fact they saw no way. Apollo was an adventure we all shared vicariously; we need more of those.

I expected to lose everything

  • He put the odds for both SpaceX and Tesla at under 10%, “Maybe 1 percent”, and on a risk-adjusted basis rockets and cars would be the “dumbest things you could possibly do”. A friend made him watch a reel of rocket failures; he had already seen them all.
  • The first plan was a small greenhouse landed on Mars, green plants on a red background, to excite the public and raise NASA’s budget, with $90 million, half his PayPal money, budgeted for two missions. Launch was the one cost he couldn’t cut; three trips to Russia to buy its biggest ICBMs failed. So he started SpaceX in mid-2002 “expecting to fail”, reasoning that a rocket company had less than a 100% chance of losing the money, which the greenhouse didn’t.
  • He funded the first three rounds himself (“I’d rather lose my money than any of my friends’ money”) because investors wanted precedents and rocketry had “a cemetery full of failures”; Founders Fund came in before orbit. The plan started from a known market, small and medium satellites, with people later and Starship, the Saturn V’s successor, as “our holy grail objective”.

Rockets from first principles

  • A Delta II had gone from $60 million to $100 million, bigger rockets cost $200–400 million, and “when I get mad I try to reframe the problem.” The big contractors were risk-averse, used 1960s components and outsourced four or five layers deep, “overhead to the fifth power”, on cost-plus contracts that give “an incentive to never finish the job.”
  • A feasibility study over Saturdays in early 2001 with veteran launch engineers produced a default design; when PayPal sold he moved to Los Angeles, the densest pool of aerospace talent. The press mocked the “internet guy”, and he agreed it sounded improbable.

Keeping SpaceX alive; landing NASA contracts

  • If they couldn’t reach orbit in three tries, “we deserved to die.” The first rocket in 2006 came down a few hundred yards from the pad in fragments; the second failed; the third, in 2008, too, and he had budgeted for three. With the money split, there was enough for a fourth: “We have one last rocket… You have six weeks.” His email: “I will never give up and I mean never.”
  • He put the fourth at better than even; a tiny change in the first-stage engine’s thrust transient, invisible on the ground, had been the difference. It reached orbit in 2008, and he felt only relief, cortisol “clinically high”. Founders Fund’s 2008 investment, from the men who had ousted him, he calls karma.
  • NASA then called with a cargo contract; he shouted “I LOVE NASA” and told Gwynne Shotwell to sign whatever they offered: “like I had been blindfolded and taken out to the firing squad… the guns just went ‘click.’” Falcon 1 cost about $6 million against about $25 million for rivals in its class; after two resupply missions NASA asked about astronauts, and split the crew contract between Boeing and SpaceX.

You have to blow things up

  • “The first goal is to make the damn thing work—we’ll optimize it later.” Dragon, which carries crew, runs in “extreme conservatism mode”; Falcon can lose a booster on landing; early Starships were built to fail and teach.
  • The Shuttle barely iterated because people were aboard: known O-ring and foam problems were tolerated because nothing had gone wrong yet, “like Russian roulette”. None of Starship’s explosions were on its own “risk list”. The optimisation is nested: fastest time to a city on Mars; within it, to a fully reusable rocket; within that, to orbit. The first Starships were built “in a tent in a parking lot” and had no doors.

Building the just barely possible; optimising for mass to Mars

  • We reuse bikes, cars, planes and boats; “It would be insane to chuck a boat away after every trip.” Orbit was solved in the 1950s; on Earth reuse is “just barely possible”, easy on Mars or the Moon. The Shuttle’s partial reuse cost more than expendables, but “you can’t take a single example and make an entire theory out of it.”
  • Falcon 9, the first rocket with any reuse, flies about every two to three days, but boosters land on ships and can’t be turned round in under several days. Starship is the largest flying object ever, about five thousand tonnes at lift-off and twice the Saturn V. Rapid, complete reuse means a flight costs about its propellant (about 80% liquid oxygen, 20% methane), “maybe a million dollars or less”.
  • Landing legs cost mass and handling, so why not land on the tower that launched it? Caught by the tower’s arms, refuelled in thirty to forty minutes, a booster could in principle refly within an hour: “it sounded batshit crazy… But we did it.”
  • The target is cost per ton, not launch count. Landing a useful ton on Mars costs about a billion dollars today (a rover); a self-sustaining city needs under $100,000 a ton, ten thousand times better (see the figure). A fully reusable Starship 3 should put four hundred times Falcon 1’s payload into orbit for less than Falcon 1 cost. “Now it’s gone from utterly insane to merely late.”

Part IV

On behalf of humanity

“How you can be part of elevating the future of humanity”: companies as philanthropy, an age of abundance from robots and AI, a register of existential risks, and life on more than one planet. The most speculative part, and the one where a physics-shaped method meets questions that aren’t physics.

Part IV · Section 12

Building Our Future

If philanthropy means love of humanity, building useful companies is philanthropy; profit shows usefulness, and net worth is stock, not a cash pile.

Companies are philanthropy; companies create wealth for all

  • He has a small foundation, but where a problem can be solved by a profitable company, that is better. SpaceX (multiplanetary life, Starlink), Tesla (sustainable energy), Neuralink (brain injuries and AI risk) and the Boring Company (traffic) are, he says, love of humanity. Because Tesla has done more for the environment than all other companies, “it would be fair to say as a leader of that company I’ve done more for the environment than any other single human on Earth.” “I care about reality. Perception be damned.”
  • Profit shows output worth more than inputs; high growth means years of negative cash flow first. Wealth isn’t consumption: Buffett allocates capital and reads tedious annual reports, a job he doesn’t want. His net worth is company stock, not billions in a bank; handing the stock to the government would just give it control. “Just as my money was the first into Tesla, it will be the last out.”

Companies to start; companies drive progress

  • His suggestions: tunnelling, which he recommended for five years before starting the Boring Company himself (a test tunnel in Los Angeles, then an operating one in Las Vegas); genetics, where synthetic RNA is “like medicine going from analog to digital”; and high-speed travel, the Hyperloop, “a pressurized electric car in a vacuum tube”, because California’s high-speed rail was set to cost around $100 billion for a two-hour trip. Take risks before you have children and obligations.
  • “The rate of innovation is not constant.” Technology only improves if smart people work like crazy on it. Egypt forgot how to build pyramids and read hieroglyphs; Rome forgot its roads, aqueducts and plumbing; America went from the Moon to low orbit to, for almost a decade, no human access to space. Hence the urgency. “I am not one of the doomsday people.”

Part IV · Section 13

The Age of Abundance

Robots remove labour as the limit on the economy, intelligence grows exponentially, brain interfaces widen the human bottleneck, cars drive themselves, and energy becomes sustainable and plentiful.

The end of scarcity

  • “Money is just a database. The actual economy is stuff.” The limit on goods and services is labour, and capital is distilled labour; humanoid robots remove the limit. The only scarcity left will be artificial (a particular artwork) or unique (a house in one spot).
  • Robots start with dangerous, boring, repetitive work; there is a labour shortage, so no one should worry about jobs; universal basic income becomes affordable. The humanoid-robot market will be bigger than cars, with more than one robot per person, perhaps ten, “well in excess of ten billion”, each cheaper than a car. Anywhere intelligence can be applied will be automated, “maybe 2033–2043”.

Exponential intelligence; upgrading the human mind

  • Cost per instruction is falling exponentially. The key ratio is digital to biological compute; humans may be “the biological bootloader for AI”. Artificial general intelligence may be only a few years away, and society unrecognisable in thirty.
  • The internet is the biggest equaliser of knowledge in history; with a Starlink dish in the Amazon you have more information than a 1980 US president. We are already cyborgs, bottlenecked by “poking glass with your meat sticks” at under a bit per second of sustained output.
  • Neuralink: the first patient, Noland Arbaugh, stayed up all night playing Civilization, and with only 10–15% of electrodes working reached a bit per second, twice the previous record. Next, Blindsight, writing directly to the visual cortex; later perhaps restoring movement past a broken spine (“Jesus-level stuff”), memory enhancement, even switching off hunger in morbid obesity. Two linked brains could communicate uncompressed, “consensual telepathy”.

The last human drivers

  • A 2013 crash in which a sleeping Model S driver killed a cyclist convinced him to hurry autopilot. A car without autonomy will be as useful as a horse; an autonomous car is worth five to ten times more and could be used fifty to sixty hours a week instead of ten or twelve. About a million people die on the roads each year, so delaying partial autonomy for fear of bad press would be “morally reprehensible”.
  • His metric for the team: miles per intervention, first slide in every meeting, because “video games without a score are boring.” Turning on autonomy across the fleet “may be the biggest asset value increase in history…overnight”, and a clean robotaxi with no mirrors, pedals or wheel is “the product that makes Tesla a ten-trillion-dollar company.”

Sustainable abundance

  • Master Plan Part 3 claims a clear, published path to a fully sustainable Earth without austerity or destroyed habitats, supporting far more than eight billion people (the six steps are in the master-plans figure above). Its hallmark is meritocracy: technology that lets everyone make the most of their scarcest resource, time.

Part IV · Section 14

Our Existential Risks

This is the best time to be alive, and civilisations are more fragile than they look: rank the risks by whether they are recoverable.

This is the best time to be alive; World War III

  • “Don’t worry about it. I mean, worry about it. Because if you worry about it, ironically, it will be okay.” The present is the most interesting time in history. For most of it, a good year meant losing only 5% of your village; judged by today’s morals, nobody passes. Sumerians, Egyptians, Greeks: civilisations have life cycles, less than 1% of what was written survives, and the birth rate may be the biggest single threat.
  • “We want to prioritize avoiding civilizational risks over things that are painful and tragic on a local level.” A third world war is likely eventually. His grandfather fought for almost six years in North Africa and Italy, saw all his friends killed, was moved to British intelligence after an IQ test, never spoke of it, and said avoid war at all costs. Read about the First World War; read Asimov’s Foundation, which is about shortening a dark age.

Regulation accumulation

  • “Humans die, but the laws they created don’t.” He asked the Transportation Secretary in 2011 to allow cameras instead of side mirrors; nothing happened. His companies answer to a few hundred agencies and, he says, comply with perhaps 100 million regulations and dispute about five.
  • His case of contradictory rules: told he’d go to prison if SpaceX hired non-permanent residents (export controls), then sued by the Justice Department for not hiring asylum seekers. California’s high-speed rail, several billion dollars in, has built a section with no rail: “It’s not an engineering failure; it’s the regulations.” Rules are added and never removed; wars have historically cleared them; we end “like Gulliver, tied down by thousands of little strings.”

Unsustainable energy

  • He is “much less of a climate alarmist than people might think”; the point is to stop an experiment we must end anyway. Long term, most energy will be solar plus batteries (“Do plants grow?”), with wind, hydro, geothermal and nuclear.
  • “Please do not shut down the nuclear power plants.” Coal is a hundred to a thousand times worse for health; nuclear accidents have killed practically nobody. Battery-cell production is the rate limiter, and civilisation could use a hundred times its current energy.

Misaligned artificial superintelligence

  • He has never seen a technology grow as fast. Superintelligence could discover new physics, or be a “great filter”: a small group monopolising it, or AI going rogue. He cites Geoffrey Hinton (spelt “Jeff” in the book) putting human annihilation at 10–20%, and calls AGI a higher risk than nuclear weapons.
  • His safety principle is truthfulness: don’t force an AI to lie. His examples are Google’s Gemini refusing to draw George Washington as white, which he extrapolates to an AI enforcing diversity by force, and HAL 9000 in 2001, which killed its crew when made to hide the monolith. Regulation after harm takes years, too slow for AI; having failed to sound the alarm, he concluded AI can’t be stopped, only steered, and that brain–computer interfaces should keep us integrated rather than become pets, “a house cat would be a good outcome, by the way.”

Population collapse

  • “A low birth rate is a slow death for a civilization.” He credits Will Durant’s Story of Civilization: prosperity lowers birth rates, and Rome fell “because the Romans stopped making Romans”, despite Caesar and Augustus. China, 40% below replacement after its two- and three-child policies; Japan, down 600,000 people in 2021; the US below replacement since the early 1970s.
  • “The biggest myth that exists right now is this ‘overpopulation’ myth.” Earth could support ten times as many people, by a first-principles check of land, water and energy. Having children is “the most optimistic thing somebody could do”; he has twelve with three women, and says friends he urged to have children have never regretted it.

Asteroids and comets

  • “A big rock will hit Earth eventually. We currently have no defense.” Shoemaker-Levy made an Earth-sized mark on Jupiter; long-period comets we don’t know about exist by the billion. Starship gives some ability to stop small asteroids, not large ones or comets.

Part IV · Section 15

Becoming Multiplanetary

A self-sustaining city on Mars would be an evolutionary milestone and life insurance for consciousness; the window to build it is open now and may not stay open.

An evolutionary-scale event; if you love life, protect it

  • In four billion years of life there are about six great milestones (single cells, multicellular life, plants and animals, the move to land, consciousness), and life becoming multiplanetary belongs on the list, at least as important as leaving the ocean. The Soviet Union will be forgotten; “The invasion of Iraq won’t even be a footnote.”
  • Earth is about 4.5 billion years old and the Sun will make it uninhabitable in about half a billion more: “If it had taken consciousness 10 percent longer to evolve, it would never have evolved at all.” Five mass extinctions, the Permian killing 90–95% of species; Yellowstone erupts about every 700,000 years. Stephen Hawking told him he put the chance of civilisation ending at about 1% a century: “Every century is a click.” “This is the first moment in 4.5 billion years that it has been possible to extend life beyond Earth.”
  • Why not spend it on Earth’s problems? About half his money is for problems like climate and half for Mars, and a Mars city would cost well under 1% of Earth’s resources, perhaps a quarter to half a percent of GDP. The Fermi paradox troubles him: we may be the only candle of consciousness in the galaxy. “Life insurance, for life.”

The gateway to Mars

  • Starbase, Texas, “basically a sandbar in the middle of nowhere”, is named the Gateway to Mars. Venus is “hell, almost literally”; the Moon lacks atmosphere and resources and would be worse in a war on Earth, though a Moon science base with giant telescopes is worth building.
  • SpaceX can build a Starship roughly every two or three weeks and aims for a thousand a year, as many as Boeing and Airbus build airliners. Tankers refuel ships in orbit (a propellant depot “like a huge hot dog”); about ten thousand missions would deliver a million tonnes, which he thinks could be done by 2044, though a city might need ten million.
  • The book’s own dated plan: about five uncrewed Starships to Mars in 2026; if they land safely, crew in 2028; otherwise two years later. Starlink pays for it: “thank everyone who has bought Starlink because you’re helping secure the future of civilization.” Today, no amount of money buys a ticket to Mars.

Building the new world; colonising the galaxy

  • It is not a rich person’s escape hatch: six months cramped with a hundred people, bad food, hard work and a high chance of death. “I would like to die on Mars…just not on impact.” Most who go won’t come back, and most ships will stay to be taken apart for materials.
  • Build power first (a solar farm), then fuel, oxygen and food; methane and oxygen propellant are easy to make from Mars’s carbon dioxide and water ice. Live in glass domes, then terraform, perhaps with orbital mirrors or thermonuclear explosions over the poles, “two little suns”, to release frozen carbon dioxide and give a mile-deep ocean over 40% of the planet.
  • Ships only crossed oceans when there was a reason to; nobody building the Union Pacific foresaw Hollywood or Silicon Valley. After the infrastructure, Mars will need “everything from iron foundries to pizza joints”.
  • With a propellant depot on Mars, the whole solar system opens: asteroids, Jupiter’s moons, Titan. On the Kardashev scale we have harnessed perhaps 1–2% of Earth’s energy; given a million years, even without new physics, we could colonise the galaxy. “You are the magicians of the twenty-first century… Go out there and create some magic.”

Methods, Timeline, Reading

The 69 core Musk methods

  • “Edited or paraphrased into short, memorable maxims”, from “You are capable of more than you think” to “Humor is a differentiator.” A selection is in the figure; others are “Empathy is not an asset”, “Double down. Timeline and reading

  • The timeline runs from his birth on 28 June 1971 in Pretoria, a first video game (Blast Star) at twelve, Canada at seventeen, and two 1994 internships (high-energy capacitors, and a games company called Rocket Science), through the companies, a near-fatal case of malaria in 2001, Tesla’s Series A ($6.5 million, 2004), Twitter for $44 billion (2022) and xAI (2023), to a net worth above $500 billion in October 2025.
  • The reading list: fiction led by The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy (“a book of philosophy, disguised as a silly humor book”), The Lord of the Rings (his favourite), Asimov’s Foundation, Dune, Heinlein and Iain M. Banks; science including Dawkins, Oreskes’s Merchants of Doubt and MacAskill’s What We Owe the Future (“a close match for my philosophy”); engineering classics such as Ignition! and J. E. An external check, not from the book

Scoring the record

A compiled book can’t mark its own homework, and Musk argues that a list of his missed dates is unfair without the hits. So here are both: the claims specific enough to be wrong, set against the public record as of 2026. The direction is right far more often than the date.

A critical reading

How much weight it holds

The engineering method is the durable part and survives any disagreement about its author. The further the book moves from things that can be measured, the more it relies on the reader being persuaded already. The structural problem is not dishonesty but format: a compiled book can’t contain a counter-argument, so here are some, by name.

Holds: the Algorithm, the teardown and the org chart in the product

Question the requirement and delete before optimising; price a thing as its materials before spending a decade; watch for the organisation showing up in the object. Free, general and checkable in your own work next week. Conway’s law has empirical support: Alan MacCormack, John Rusnak and Carliss Baldwin (Research Policy, 2012) found that products built by distributed teams were measurably more modular than those built by tight ones, as the “mirroring” idea predicts.

Weak: the hours

The arithmetic that a hundred hours gets twice as much done as fifty assumes output per hour doesn’t fall. The economist John Pencavel’s study of British munitions workers (2014) found output per week nearly flat above about fifty hours, and seventy hours produced hardly more than fifty-six. The book presents long hours as both necessary and harmful, and never separates what the results needed from what the crises demanded.

Weak: survivorship

The method is evidenced entirely by ventures that worked, told afterwards by the man who ran them; SpaceX alone had three failures before the fourth launch he put at better than even. Fooled by Randomness, on this site, is the direct rebuttal to learning from winners’ accounts.

Mixed: autonomy and “moral” urgency

The book calls delaying partial autonomy for fear of bad press “morally reprehensible”. US regulators investigated crashes involving Autopilot for years, and in December 2023 Tesla recalled about two million cars to add driver-monitoring safeguards. Whether early release saved or cost lives is argued; the book presents only one side.

Mixed: truthful AI as the safety principle

Truthfulness is a real goal of alignment research, and the Gemini episode happened. But in July 2025 xAI’s own chatbot, Grok, posted antisemitic replies after a change to its instructions, and xAI apologised: aiming at “maximally truthful” did not on its own prevent harm. Stuart Russell and Bostrom, both on Musk’s reading list, treat alignment as harder than any single property.

Holds, with a caveat: nuclear and coal

Deaths per unit of electricity from coal are hundreds of times those from nuclear on the standard estimates (Anil Markandya and Paul Wilkinson, The Lancet, 2007, and later compilations), which supports his “a hundred to a thousand times worse”. The caveat is cost and build time, which the chapter doesn’t address.

Mixed: population

Fertility is below replacement in most of the world’s countries, as he says. The UN’s 2024 projections still show world population rising to a peak of about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s, so “collapse” is a long-run and uneven risk rather than an immediate one, and “Earth could sustain ten times” is a back-of-envelope claim.

Weak: Mars as a city

Kelly and Zach Weinersmith’s A City on Mars (2023) argues the hard problems are not launch cost but biology (reproduction and child development in low gravity and high radiation), psychology, law and economics, none of which the book’s tonnage arithmetic touches. The rockets may arrive long before the answers.

Weak: philanthropy by market test, and the missing politics

Willingness to pay is not a measure of effects on everyone else, and “I’ve done more for the environment than any other single human” is asserted, not argued. By excluding politics, the book leaves out much of what its subject did between 2022 and 2025, including his role in the US government, which bears directly on its chapters about regulation.

Sourcing, and some slips

About a thousand citations, mostly interviews and posts, make it a faithful record of what Musk has said, not of what happened. The text disagrees with itself: the PayPal chapter says eBay paid “about $4.5 billion”, the timeline $1.5 billion (the right figure); the Zip2 chapter gives $21 million, the timeline $22 million; PayPal proceeds are $180 million in one place and $170 million in another; and Geoffrey Hinton appears as “Jeff”.

Words that do a lot of work

Glossary

First principles
Rebuilding an argument from what you are most confident is true, instead of by analogy.
Reasoning by analogy
Doing what others have done; fine for daily life, a trap for new things.
Magic wand number
What a product would cost if its raw materials could be arranged for free.
Idiot index
A part’s cost divided by the cost of its materials.
Thinking in the limit
Pushing a variable to extremes (a million units, a twelve-foot tunnel) to see what binds.
The Algorithm
Make requirements less dumb, delete, simplify, accelerate, automate, in that order.
Mass recursion
Every added tonne needs fuel and structure that add nearly another tonne; SpaceX uses about 1.8.
Vector sum
A team’s output: effort times direction, added up.
Special forces
His model for a start-up: the minimum passing grade is excellent.
Shortest path
Anyone talks to anyone to solve a problem, against the chain of command.
Tolerance stack-up
Small allowed errors in many parts adding up to a large error in the whole.
Giga-casting
Casting a car’s front or rear third as one piece, an idea from toy cars.
The machine that builds the machine
The factory treated as the product.
Master plan
Tesla’s published strategies: the staircase (2006), Part Deux (2016), Part 3 (2023).
Full and rapid reuse
Recovering and re-flying the whole vehicle quickly, so a flight costs about its propellant.
Cost per ton
What SpaceX optimises: to orbit, to the Moon, to the surface of Mars.
Miles per intervention
The single score for self-driving: how far the car goes before a human takes over.
Age of abundance
His forecast of an economy no longer limited by labour, thanks to robots and AI.
Biological bootloader
His phrase for humanity’s possible role: the program that starts the AI.
Regulation accumulation
Rules added every year and never removed, until “everything becomes illegal”.
Great filter
A barrier that stops most civilisations spreading beyond their planet.
Kardashev scale
Civilisations ranked by the energy they harness: planet, star, galaxy.
Eating glass
Bill Lee’s phrase: working on the problems the company needs, not the ones you want.
Life insurance for life
His case for Mars: a second, independent home for consciousness.

Using it

Reading it

  1. Start at Part II

    The Algorithm, the organisation chapters and “We Must Make Stuff” are the transferable part. Part I reads better once you have seen the method work.

  2. Then Part III as evidence

    Zip2, PayPal, 2008 and the fourth launch show where the lessons came from, often by being overruled.

  3. Read Part IV last, and slowly

    It is where a physics-shaped method meets questions that aren’t physics-shaped, and you have to supply the scepticism the format removes.

  4. Check one quotation, then try one deletion

    Find the original interview for a striking line and see what the surrounding minute does to it. Then take a live requirement at work, find its owner, and try to delete it.
