The Lessons of History

Will & Ariel Durant · 1968

About a hundred pages. Michael Saylor tells people to read it.

The whole book in one breath

After four decades writing the eleven volumes of The Story of Civilization, the Durants asked what the whole story teaches. Their answer comes in thirteen short essays. Geography matters less as technology grows. History is a branch of biology: life competes, selects and breeds, and so inequality is natural. Race doesn’t make civilisations; civilisations make peoples. Human nature barely changes, while morals shift with the economy, and religion has almost always held the moral order together. Wealth concentrates until it is redistributed, peacefully or violently, and capitalism and socialism keep correcting each other. Governments cycle through familiar forms, and war is nearly constant. Civilisations rise by meeting challenges and decline when they stop. Yet progress is real, because each generation is born into a richer inheritance than the last.

The authors

Forty years of history, boiled down

Will Durant (1885–1981) and Ariel Durant (1898–1981) spent most of their lives writing The Story of Civilization, eleven volumes from ancient Sumer to Napoleon published between 1935 and 1975. Ariel, credited as co-author from the seventh volume on, worked on it from the beginning. The tenth volume, Rousseau and Revolution, won the Pulitzer Prize in 1968.

That same year they published The Lessons of History. It is a short book of about a hundred pages in which they review their own work and ask what, if anything, it teaches. The tone is humane, ironic and aphoristic, and the book is best read as distilled judgement rather than as argument with footnotes.

If you remember nothing else

Twelve lessons the Durants draw

I

Hold history lightly

The record is partial and historians are biased. Every lesson is a hypothesis, not a law.

II

Geography sets the stage, less and less

Rivers, seas and climate shaped early civilisations. Technology keeps loosening their grip.

III

History is part of biology

Life is competition, selection and reproduction. Societies are subject to the same laws.

III

Freedom and equality conflict

Leave people free and natural differences produce unequal results. Enforce equality and freedom dies.

IV

Civilisation makes the people

No race has a monopoly on civilisation. Conditions create civilisations, and civilisations shape peoples.

V

Human nature barely changes

What changes is the balance between innovators and conservatives, and both are necessary.

VI–VII

Morals follow the economy

Moral codes shift with how people make a living, and religion has almost always propped them up.

VIII

Wealth concentrates, then is spread

Concentration is natural and recurring. It is relieved by redistribution, peaceful if wise, violent if not.

IX

Capitalism and socialism correct each other

Each has pushed the other toward the virtue it lacked.

X

Democracy is the hardest government

It asks the most intelligence of the most people, and it is fragile.

XI

War is the norm

Nations compete as individuals do, without a sheriff above them.

XII–XIII

Progress is inheritance

We aren’t born better than our ancestors, but we are born into more.

The book, in order

Thirteen short chapters

Each chapter pairs history with one force: the earth, biology, race, character, morals, religion, economics, socialism, government, war. The last two ask whether civilisations must decay and whether progress is real.

Chapter I

Hesitations

Can history teach anything, given how little we know?

  • The Durants admit the difficulties at the outset. Records are fragmentary, historians select and slant, and the present always colours the past.
  • Human history is a tiny episode on a tiny planet. Any lesson drawn from it is provisional.
  • Still, the attempt is worth making. The past is the present unrolled for understanding, and some patterns recur often enough to count.

“Most history is guessing, and the rest is prejudice.”Chapter I

Chapter II

History and the Earth

Geography is the stage, and technology keeps enlarging it.

  • Rivers, seas, climate and resources shaped where civilisations arose and how they traded. Civilisation moved from the river valleys to the Mediterranean, then to the Atlantic and Pacific.
  • As technology advances, geography’s influence shrinks. Humanity becomes less the prisoner of its terrain.
  • But nature still has the last word. An ice age, a drought or an earthquake can undo human work.

“Civilization exists by geological consent, subject to change without notice.”Chapter II

Chapter III

Biology and History

History is a fragment of biology, and biology teaches three lessons.

  • Life is competition. Cooperation is real, but it is mostly a tool of competition: groups cooperate in order to compete with other groups.
  • Life is selection. People are born unequal in ability, and the more complex a society becomes, the more that inequality shows in outcomes.
  • Life must breed. Nature favours groups that reproduce. The Durants worry that the most educated groups have the fewest children, a passage that reads uncomfortably today.

“Freedom and equality are sworn and everlasting enemies, and when one prevails the other dies.”Chapter III

Chapter IV

Race and History

Race doesn’t explain civilisation.

  • The Durants reject the racial theories of history of writers such as Gobineau and Houston Stewart Chamberlain.
  • Great civilisations arose among many peoples: in Egypt, Mesopotamia, India, China and Mesoamerica.
  • Their conclusion is that geographic, economic and political conditions create a civilisation, and the civilisation then shapes a human type.

Chapter V

Character and History

Human nature is constant, and society changes through a tug-of-war between innovators and conservatives.

  • The basic instincts of acquisition, pugnacity, sex and sociability have changed little in recorded history. Only their expression changes.
  • Change comes from innovators, exceptional individuals who propose new ideas, and it is tested by the resistance of the majority.
  • That resistance is valuable. The conservative who resists change, the Durants argue, is as necessary as the radical who proposes it. Traditions carry accumulated experience, and new ideas should have to prove themselves.

Chapter VI

Morals and History

Moral codes adapt to how people live.

  • Morals are the rules a society teaches its members to keep order. They shift with economic life.
  • Hunting societies prized courage and pugnacity. Agricultural ones prized industry, thrift, chastity and large families, because children were economic assets. Industrial, urban life loosened those constraints.
  • Moral laxity is not new. Every age has had its sins, and periods of licence have been followed by restraint. The danger is a transition where the old code has lost its hold and no new one has formed.

Chapter VII

Religion and History

Religion has almost always carried the moral order.

  • Religion has offered consolation, meaning and, above all, supernatural support for moral codes.
  • The Durants observe that history offers no significant example, before their own time, of a society that kept its moral life without the aid of religion. They leave it open whether the modern West will be the first.
  • Faith and scepticism alternate. Doubt spreads in prosperous, educated eras, and faith returns in hard times, so Puritan and pagan periods succeed each other.

Chapter VIII

Economics and History

Marx was partly right, and wealth has a rhythm.

  • Economic forces shape politics and culture far more than older histories admitted. But ideas, religion and individuals matter too, so the economic interpretation is part of the truth, not all of it.
  • Wealth tends to concentrate in the hands of the able, naturally and inevitably. Periodically it is spread again, by reform or by revolution (see the figure below).
  • Solon’s cancellation of debts in Athens and the reforms of the Gracchi in Rome are the Durants’ examples of the two paths: peaceful redistribution, and failed reform followed by violence.

“The men who can manage men manage the men who can manage only things, and the men who can manage money manage all.”Chapter VIII

Chapter IX

Socialism and History

Socialism is as old as civilisation, and so is its struggle with the profit motive.

  • The contest between socialism and capitalism is part of the same rhythm of concentration and dispersal of wealth.
  • The Durants survey socialist experiments across history (see the figure below). They note that most ended in failure or reversal, often because they suppressed incentives.
  • Experience, they conclude, suggests that every economic system must sooner or later rely on some form of the profit motive.
  • But the two systems have improved each other, and the Durants foresaw them converging.

“The fear of capitalism has compelled socialism to widen freedom, and the fear of socialism has compelled capitalism to increase equality.”Chapter IX

Chapter X

Government and History

Monarchy is the most common form, democracy the hardest, and every form tends to decay into the next.

  • Monarchy has been the usual government in history, and the Durants credit it with continuity and order. Aristocracy trains leaders but hardens into privilege.
  • Democracy is the most difficult form, because it requires the widest spread of intelligence. It is vulnerable to demagogues and to the concentration of wealth.
  • They recall Plato’s scheme: governments pass from monarchy to aristocracy to democracy to dictatorship, each failing by the excess of its own principle (see the figure below).

Chapter XI

History and War

War is one of history’s constants.

  • The Durants count only 268 years without war in 3,421 years of recorded history. The hero at the top of this page draws that ratio.
  • Wars have the same causes as competition between individuals: acquisitiveness, pugnacity and pride. Between states, though, there is no law or police above them to restrain those impulses.
  • The chapter closes with an imagined exchange between a general, who argues war is natural and inevitable, and a philosopher, who hopes for a wider order in which nations restrain themselves as individuals have learned to.

Chapter XII

Growth and Decay

Civilisations rise by meeting challenges and decline when they can’t.

  • The Durants accept Arnold Toynbee’s idea of challenge and response: a civilisation grows when a creative minority finds answers to the problems it faces.
  • Decline comes when leadership fails to meet new challenges, not from an inevitable biological ageing of the kind Spengler imagined.
  • Civilisations don’t simply die. Their achievements pass to their successors, as Greece passed to Rome and Rome to Europe.

Chapter XIII

Is Progress Real?

Yes, if progress means a richer inheritance.

  • The Durants define progress as the increasing control of the environment by life.
  • By that measure much has improved (knowledge, health, the reach of education and art), even though human nature, cruelty and war have not.
  • Progress lives in the heritage. We aren’t born wiser or better than our ancestors. We are born into a greater store of knowledge, art and institutions, passed on by education. Civilisation is that inheritance, and each generation must learn it anew.

Context

Where it has been argued with

Sweeping and uncited

The book gives few sources, and its generalisations draw mostly on Western history. Historians value its wisdom more than its method. Some of its famous numbers, such as the count of years without war, can’t easily be traced.

Religion and morals

The claim that no society has kept its morals without religion is contested by the record of highly secular modern societies. The Durants themselves left the question open.

Biology and inequality

The passages on differential birth rates and natural inequality read today as dated, and some readers find them close to eugenic thinking. Others take them as the Durants’ unsentimental realism.

Is war really constant?

Steven Pinker’s The Better Angels of Our Nature (2011) argued that violence, including war deaths per head, has declined over the long run. That challenges the Durants’ picture of war as a fixed feature, though not their account of its causes.

Terms of art

Glossary

Geological consent
The Durants’ phrase for civilisation’s dependence on a stable earth.
Selection
The biological process by which natural inequalities produce unequal outcomes.
Innovators and conservatives
The two forces whose tension produces orderly change.
Moral code
The rules a society enforces to keep order. The Durants tie them to its economy.
Concentration of wealth
The natural drift of wealth toward the able, periodically reversed.
Redistribution
The reversal, by legislation (Solon) or revolution.
Profit motive
The incentive the Durants think every economy must eventually use.
Plato’s cycle
Monarchy, aristocracy, democracy, dictatorship, and round again.
Challenge and response
Toynbee’s account of how civilisations grow.
Progress
In the Durants’ definition, the increasing control of the environment by life.
Heritage
The accumulated knowledge, art and institutions each generation inherits through education.

Using it

Reading it

At about a hundred pages it can be read in an evening, and that is the best way to read it. If you only have an hour:

  1. Chapters II and III

    The earth and biology: the foundations of the Durants’ realism.

  2. Chapters VIII and IX

    Wealth, redistribution and the convergence of systems.

  3. Chapter X

    Government, democracy and Plato’s cycle.

  4. Chapter XI

    War, and the general’s argument with the philosopher.

  5. Chapter XIII

    The answer to whether any of it adds up to progress.
