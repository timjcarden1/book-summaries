The Mobile Wave

How Mobile Intelligence Will Change Everything

A smartphone is not a smaller desktop. It is a personal computer that never leaves you, and it will absorb whole categories of objects and reorganise the institutions around them.

The book’s argument, in our words

Michael J. Saylor · 2012

The whole book in one breath

Every computing wave moved the machine closer to the person and multiplied who could use it; the fifth wave puts a networked, sensor-laden computer in everyone’s pocket all day. Once it is always there, it can replace paper, screens, wallets, keys and service counters, and each replacement removes friction, creates data and turns a static product into a service that keeps improving. Saylor follows the idea through paper, entertainment, money, social life, medicine, education and the developing world, opening almost every chapter with a story from the deep past, from Sumerian clay to Perseus’s wallet. He ends with three economic revolutions, each freeing human energy for new work, and with technology as an acid that burns away inefficiency. Read now, the capability forecasts landed almost everywhere. What the book did not see was who would own the arrangement: who pays, who takes the toll, what the attention bill is, what privacy costs, and what stayed stubbornly physical.

The author

A software chief executive reads the history of technology

Michael J. Saylor was born in 1965 and grew up the son of an Air Force enlisted man who rose to chief master sergeant. He tells the story himself in Chapter 8: MIT’s tuition of $10,000 a year was out of reach, so he “indentured” himself to the Air Force to attend. He took MIT degrees in aeronautics and astronautics and in science, technology and society, and he dedicates the book to his parents and to Carl Kaysen, the MIT scholar who, he writes, inspired his passion for the history of science. That passion shapes the book: nearly every chapter opens in the deep past before it turns to the phone.

In 1989 he co-founded MicroStrategy, a business-intelligence software company. It was a star of the dot-com boom until March 2000, when it restated its results, its shares lost most of their value in a day, and Saylor later settled fraud charges with the Securities and Exchange Commission without admitting or denying them. He was still its chairman and chief executive when he wrote this book, and the company had been pushing mobile versions of its software since 2010. The author, in other words, had a stake in the forecast, which does not make it wrong but is worth knowing.

The book was published in 2012 by Vanguard Press, part of the Perseus Books Group: about 240 pages of text and another forty of notes and references, most of them to press articles and web pages from 2010 and 2011. The acknowledgments thank the writers Dan McNeill and Paul Freiberger alongside his MicroStrategy team.

What the foreword says

The foreword states the thesis in miniature. The transformation of physical objects into software is rippling through industry after industry, driving changes like the bankruptcies of Kodak and Borders Books, companies rooted in the physical world. (Kodak filed in January 2012, which dates the foreword to the last weeks before the book went to press.) Social networks such as Facebook and Twitter are offering “a chance at overnight democratization”, a levelling of entrenched power in parts of the world. He calls mobile intelligence the fifth wave of computer technology, and says the book is his analysis of its impact.

Orientation

The argument in one pass

The first computing waves put machines in institutions, then departments, then on desks, then on a network of desks. The mobile wave puts a networked computer on the person, which changes both the scale and the intimacy. Once the device is persistent, personal and full of sensors, it can replace paper, screens, wallets and service counters, and every replacement removes friction, creates data, and turns a static product into a service. Companies that plant their software on the customer’s phone win; companies that treat the phone as one more channel lose to rivals who rebuild around it.

Saylor then widens the lens. Agriculture freed human energy from hunting, industry freed it from the farm, and the information revolution, with mobile computing as its tipping-point technology, frees it from routine coordination. Poorer countries skip the fixed infrastructure the rich built, and billions of people get the same information as a banker in lower Manhattan. His closing image is technology as acid: it burns away whatever is inefficient and leaves only what is durable enough to survive it.

If you remember nothing else

Twelve ideas to keep

Ch 1

Different, not just smaller

The trap is to see a new machine as a shrunken old one. The phone is a new kind of computer with new uses.

Ch 1

Plant the software on the customer

Every consumer-facing company needs its own app on the customer’s phone: a direct line that cuts out middlemen and allows one-to-one marketing.

Ch 1

Software turns to vapor

On a desktop, software is solid, bound to one machine. On mobile it “boiled off” into a vapor that surrounds you everywhere.

Ch 2

Five waves, each closer to the person

Mainframe, minicomputer, desktop, Internet PC, mobile Internet: less expertise and infrastructure per user, more users every time.

Ch 3

The second copy decides

Printing mattered because it made the second copy cheap. Software makes it free and instant, so paper follows papyrus and parchment.

Ch 4

One universal screen

Camera, console, DVD, television and the ads around them collapse into one personal screen, on demand rather than on schedule.

Ch 5

Intelligent money

Digital cash knows who owns it and who should have it. Cards, coupons, IDs and keys become software you can lend, limit and revoke.

Ch 6

The network is the new city

From Uruk to Facebook, dense networks breed ideas. A mobile social network also organises crowds, as Tahrir Square showed.

Ch 7–8

From visits to streams

Care and teaching move from appointments and timetables to continuous monitoring, remote experts and feedback.

Ch 9

The latecomer advantage

A country without landlines or bank branches can skip them. In Kerala, phones ended the waste of unsold fish; in Kenya, M-Pesa made a bank of a basic handset.

Ch 10

Information energy

Data is the fuel, software the engine, and “information motion”, apps and reports, moves people to act. Unlike coal, it is never used up.

Ch 10

Technology is acid

It burns away accumulated inefficiency and exposes corruption, and this revolution will come faster than any before it.

Chapters 1–2

A computer becomes part of the person

The important change is not processor size. It is the collapse of the distance between software and daily behaviour, and it starts with a thirteen-year-old at lunch.

Chapter 1 · The Wave

The Shape of the Wave

Mobile computing is not a smaller PC but a new universal computer, and it will force every business to turn its products into software and plant that software on the customer.

A lunch in June 2010

  • The book opens in late June 2010 at New York’s Blue Water Grill. Saylor had flown his niece Lauren, about to turn thirteen, and her mother up from Sarasota, Florida. Asked what she thought of the Big Apple, she talked about the iPad. For her generation, he realised, Apple was a company and the “Big Apple” was the iPad, not the city.
  • Why did she want one? She could do her schoolwork on it, play games on it and read her email on it, “and all my friends have one”. How many friends? “Hundreds.” The tablet had been on sale for about twelve weeks, and her circle had already snapped it up like candy. He calls the moment an epiphany.
  • A second scene makes the same point from lower down the age range: a child of about three in a stroller playing with an iPad, who told a Swedish woman who asked what he was doing that he was working on his music.

Different, not just smaller

  • It is easy, he writes, to fall into the trap of assuming a new technology is a smaller version of the old one. The phone is not a shrunken PC. Always on, always carried, touch-driven and full of sensors, it does different jobs, and companies and governments have begun to sense “a fundamental new dynamic afoot”.
  • The universal computer. Mobile computers will become “the standard universal computing platform on the planet”. The argument is scale: the sheer numbers in pockets, purses and backpacks will make mobile the standard channel through which people receive information and services.

Two changes for every business

  • The new platform forces two changes. Companies will replace their physical products and services with software equivalents, and they will extend their business processes so that they live on their customers’ mobile computers.
  • The chapter previews the list of objects in pockets and purses that become software: keys, wallets, credit cards, calendars, cameras, recorders, maps and mirrors. Money, credit and loyalty programmes will all become software on your phone; a doctor in Bangalore will one day examine you through the screen for $10, reading your temperature, blood pressure, heart rate and an EKG from sensors attached to the device. Later chapters take these items up one by one.
  • Planting the software on your customer. Every consumer-facing company will have to establish “a direct application linkage” with its customers, its own app on their phone. That link shortens the value chain by cutting out middlemen in retail, distribution and service, and it allows the long-sought one-to-one marketing relationship with each customer. The prize is a place among the app icons on the first two pages of people’s phone screens; first movers capture the attention, the usage and the loyalty.
  • From solid to vapor. On a desktop, software exists in “solid form”, installed on and bound to a machine. With mobile technology it has “boiled off into a vapor form that surrounds us everywhere we go”, reachable at Wi-Fi watering holes and over the cell network.

Disruption, and war in cyberspace

  • Disruption ahead. Software will replace physical goods, middlemen will be less necessary, and brick-and-mortar stores will disappear. Vast amounts of capital will be driven out of the production equation. The music industry, he says, gave us a taste of the upheaval.
  • “You may not be interested in war, but war is interested in you.” He notes the line is only loosely attributed to Trotsky, and a note gives the less memorable original from a letter in In Defense of Marxism. The point is that whatever business you are in, the software war is coming to you.
  • In cyberspace there are no laws of physics to anchor strategy: no conservation of mass, momentum or energy. There will be regulations and laws, but not physics. The companies that do well there, Google, Facebook and Apple, are software companies at the core. A chart compares 2009–10 market-value growth for a “cyber economy” (Amazon, Apple, Google) against a traditional one (Best Buy, Dell, Microsoft), and a note values Google at $190 billion.
  • The threat to incumbents, he warns, comes from twenty-something software engineers “with great imagination and nothing to lose”.

Accelerating the Information Revolution

  • There have been three great economic revolutions in human history: agricultural, industrial, and now informational. Electricity was the tipping-point technology that accelerated the lumbering Industrial Revolution; mobile computing, he believes, is the tipping-point technology for the Information Revolution, the one that lets software become ubiquitous. Chapter 10 returns to this frame and builds it out.

Chapter 2 · Computers

The Evolution to Mobile Computing

Four waves of computing led here, each moving the machine closer to the individual; the fifth, the mobile Internet, arrived with the iPhone and became unmistakable with the iPad.

  • The chapter opens with a ninety-nine-year-old woman in Oregon who bought an iPad as her first computer. On a PC, enlarging text meant selecting characters and choosing a font size; on the iPad she spread two fingers. With the barest instruction she rediscovered one of her great lost joys, reading.
  • Four waves came first, each built on the last and each automating a different layer of work: the minicomputer wave automated factory processes, for instance, and the personal-computer wave automated office work with email and spreadsheets.

The first wave: the mainframe

  • The Industrial Revolution extended human muscle; the computer extended the mind. The ENIAC (Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer) entered operation three months after Hiroshima, too late for the war it was built for but the start of the mainframe age. It weighed more than thirty tons and covered more than 1,800 square feet.
  • Mainframes were monsters, each at first hand-built and unique, with output on a roll-like printout from a large electric typewriter. At the level of society they lowered costs, created jobs and yielded some new products, but they never touched most people’s lives.

The second wave: the minicomputer

  • Scientists chafed at the “priesthood” that guarded the mainframes. Digital Equipment Corporation introduced the first commercial minicomputer, about the size of a refrigerator and far cheaper, and by the 1970s minis were running ticket agencies and mass-transit systems. The industry’s drama is the subject of Tracy Kidder’s Pulitzer-winning The Soul of a New Machine.
  • Under the waves ran a chain of components: the transistor, developed at Bell Labs and made of semiconductor material; the integrated circuit; and the microprocessor, which commoditised the heart of the computer and was sold to manufacturers as a standard part. Built from discrete transistors, he points out, the 29,000 in the Intel 8088 would have needed an enormous circuit board.

The third wave: the desktop

  • In 1975 a cover story in Popular Electronics introduced the Altair, a build-it-yourself kit sold out of an Albuquerque strip mall, where “a kid named Bill Gates” was hanging around. Microsoft wrote the DOS operating system for the first IBM PC, and by 1995 Gates was the richest person on earth.
  • Apple revolutionised computers in 1984 with the Macintosh and its mouse. In 1985 its board decided Steve Jobs was not “businesslike” enough and ejected him.

The fourth wave: the Internet PC

  • The classic PC was self-sufficient: you bought programs for it at stores. The Internet turned it into a window. News and information portals appeared, you could barter and sell at the world’s biggest auction, and the online store Amazon became better known than the great river that gave it its name.

The road to mobile

  • For years engineers worked on the parts a pocket computer needs, among them flash memory (named, the book says, because its erasing speed reminded its developer of a camera flash; Intel saw its potential early) and ferroelectric RAM.
  • Early mobile. The first cell phones came out of a 1970s race between Bell Labs and Motorola. Motorola’s vice-president Martin Cooper won in 1973 and used his handset to call his rival. As texting grew popular Nokia rose to prominence, and texting pushed phones toward full keyboards. GPS joined the mix with the Nokia N95 in 2007.
  • The BlackBerry was the first smartphone blockbuster: a two-way pager in 1999, a phone from 2002, good for texting and tolerable for short emails, with 32 million customers by the end of 2009.

The fifth wave: the mobile Internet

  • Steve Jobs introduced the iPhone at Macworld in January 2007. Supply ran short and sellers on eBay and Craigslist asked as much as $12,000. It offered a striking user experience: Google Maps, YouTube, photo editing, and movies or airline tickets bought on the device, things that had never been possible on the BlackBerry. The App Store followed in July 2008, alongside a new iPhone.
  • Google rolled out Android in 2008 with a multi-touch screen, gyroscope, GPS and web access. Unlike Apple, Google made it open source, so programmers and manufacturers could modify it and it could evolve in directions Google never intended. Its Android Market passed 10 billion downloads in late 2011. In 2009, he notes, mobile Internet devices began outselling personal computers.
  • Multi-touch and more. Touch screens were old: early touch sensors date from the early 1970s and Hewlett-Packard sold the first commercial touch-screen computer in 1983. The breakthrough was the iPad, which put the iPhone’s interface on a larger screen that weighed 1.5 pounds and went anywhere. People could read books and magazines on it and use productivity apps the small screen had frustrated. He recalls urging one software maker to port his applications to the iPad because it was so easy to use.

Chapters 3–5

Objects collapse into applications

Paper, entertainment screens and wallets look like separate industries. Saylor treats them as bundles of information, identity and transactions that software can recombine, and gives each a long history before he buries it.

Chapter 3 · Paper

The Demise of Paper

Paper beat clay, papyrus and parchment because it was cheaper to copy; software now beats paper for the same reason, and every paper product, from maps to office forms, will become a live service.

Paper vanishing today

  • The chapter opens with the bankruptcy in 2009 of AbitibiBowater, a newsprint giant, whose vice-president said the company could not speculate on the future. Pulp mills, Saylor adds, have consumed some 5,000 square miles of US forest a year, all for paper. His verdict: paper is vanishing into the world of papyrus and parchment.

From clay to paper

  • The Sumerians used the earth around them, pressing wedge-shaped cuneiform into wet clay. The Epic of Gilgamesh survives on sets of tablets about three inches across, and Mesopotamians drew maps on hand-sized tablets, one from about 2500 BC.
  • Egypt improved on clay with papyrus, strips of a reed (Cyperus papyrus) whose own sap glued them together. It was cheap and light but fragile. It lasted in Egypt’s dry climate and filled the library of Alexandria, but in damp Rome it quickly degraded, which kept scribes busy recopying.
  • Parchment, charta pergamenum, was dried, de-haired and polished sheepskin; vellum was calf hide. Contracts on parchment date to 258 BC and the Dead Sea Scrolls are parchment. By the fourth century AD it had ousted papyrus in Europe, but it was costly: printing the Gutenberg Bible on vellum meant slaughtering herds of calves.
  • Paper came from China. In 1957 archaeologists found a four-inch square of it in a tomb near the ancient capital Sian (Xi’an), dated between 140 and 87 BC, and one of the oldest pieces of Chinese paper carries a map.

Gutenberg and the second-copy revolution

  • Printing was not new either. The Chinese printed sometime between 704 and 751, carving whole lines or pages into blocks for calendars, playing cards and paper money. Johannes Gensfleisch zum Gutenberg, born in Mainz between 1397 and 1403, made movable type practical in Europe, where books were still handwritten and costly. He worked on a small scale until about 1460, when some think he went blind, and died in 1468.
  • Saylor names this the second-copy revolution: what printing changed was the cost of every copy after the first. Page numbers, indexes and tables of contents followed, all of which sped up access to information.
  • The first post-Gutenberg breakthrough was desktop publishing. The copier had already made everyone a micro-publisher; the desktop made composing pages cheap. But both still ended on paper that had to be printed, stored and shipped.

The new map, book, bookstore and library

  • Maps open with a story from 227 BC. Crown Prince Tan of Yan had to make a humiliating submission to the ruler of Qin, and the gift was a district map. His assassin, Jing Ke, hid a dagger in the case with it, to be drawn as he presented the map. The map as a precious object, Saylor says, is finished: “The paper map was a fine product once, but it’s a ridiculous one today.” Google Maps and Google Earth are a world library of maps and satellite images, and with app-phones and tablets the desktop printer “is about to go the way of the typewriter”.
  • The book is changing from tablet to “magic tablet”. What is a book? Not the object. Electronic ink, first developed by Joseph Jacobson in the 1990s, made screens reflective and easy on the eyes, and with the Kindle the screen became pleasant to read.
  • The bookstore, a middleman, loses its reason to exist once books are files; the foreword has already cited Borders’ bankruptcy.
  • The library becomes everywhere and nowhere. Alexandria gathered some 700,000 scrolls; law libraries foreshadowed the change, since attorneys who once met in the firm’s library, its “public square”, now search online. Google’s book-scanning with the libraries of Harvard, Stanford, Oxford and Cornell points to a true world library “that Ptolemy could never have accomplished”.
  • The world as your library. Wikipedia is building a world encyclopedia; on a phone it leaps into the physical world. Any object can be tagged with NFC, RFID, a barcode or a GPS reading and linked to information, even a “digital tombstone”. As information becomes vapor, he writes, it will surround us and we will breathe it in.

The new newspaper and magazine

  • Printed books will linger on as luxuries, he says, but print newspapers already face extinction. Newspaper advertising income peaked at $48.7 billion in 2000 and was $22.8 billion by 2010 (see the figure). Daily circulation fell 30% from 1990 to 2010, and classified advertising fled to Craigslist, which is free for most listings. The New York Times’s publisher Arthur Sulzberger had conceded in 2010 that the paper would stop printing “sometime in the future”.
  • His advice to publishers: since the big stories are stale by the time they are printed, focus only where you can be the best. The Portland Oregonian can be the best paper for local news but never the top international one; the Dayton Daily News can report local culture and Dayton’s sports scores. USA Today, lacking prestige and a niche, “may have no tomorrow”.
  • Magazines may get a golden age of niche publications. A person with a shrewd idea and an audience no longer needs offices, a press and a production plant.

The paperless office, and saving the air

  • “Paper is to an office as krill is to a whale.” By 2010 the average US office worker used 10,000 sheets of copy paper a year. Mobile devices will turn the paperless office from hype into reality, and every standard form, at every level of government, can become software that fills and files itself.
  • The environmental case closes the chapter. In September 1994 a naturalist, David Noble, climbed into one of the 500 canyons of Australia’s Wollemi National Park and found the Wollemi pine, a relic of a lost world. Paper mills, meanwhile, release millions of pounds of chlorine dioxide, methanol, formaldehyde and hydrochloric acid into the air.

Chapter 4 · Entertainment

The New Universal Screen

One personal screen absorbs the camera, the games console, the DVD, the television schedule and the advertising around them, and each industry that sold a physical medium loses it.

  • The universal screen, he writes, is Casablanca, Chinatown, Mad Men, Arab revolts and home movies; it is Angry Birds, World of Warcraft, and chess with a stranger in Chile. Once everything is on one screen, the old media become unwanted baggage.

The disappearance of the camera

  • In 1841 a daguerreotype portrait cost about a week’s wage. In 1888 George Eastman sold the first box camera, the Kodak, for $25, and charged $10 to develop the film. He knew where the profits lay, and the slogan said so: “You press the button, we do the rest.”
  • The companies that did “the rest” have now gone to rest, Saylor writes, and iconic brands like Kodak, Pentax and Fujifilm have buckled. Meanwhile Flickr became a global photo album and passed five billion photos in 2010, and Facebook was taking 2.5 billion uploads a month. As app-phones reach the developing world, billions of new photographers will emerge.
  • A camera that is always present, he adds, has sweeping implications for crime, privacy and revolutions, themes that return in Chapters 6 and 10.

Games: the open playing field

  • Angry Birds, made by the small Finnish firm Rovio for people to play while waiting, was Apple’s best-selling app of 2010. At $0.99 it had brought in more than $8 million by the end of 2010; on Android, where it was free, Rovio said ads yielded $1 million a month. It cost about $100,000 to develop (a typical app cost $30,000 to $300,000), so anyone could take a shot. Its secret was micro-play: not one game but a sprawling basket of short ones.
  • Farmville. Silicon Valley investors repeatedly failed to back Zynga’s founder Mark Pincus. By 2011 Farmville had some 84 million monthly users, and its farmers outnumbered real ones 110 to 1. The typical social gamer was a forty-three-year-old woman; as Pincus put it, Facebook is like a cocktail party and social games give people something to do there. Zynga’s Cityville, launched in November 2010, grew faster still, spreading through social networks.
  • The long history of play. Senet, the oldest known board game, was found in an Egyptian tomb dating from 3500 BC, and Tutankhamun played it; leela, from sixteenth-century India, survives as Chutes and Ladders. The first video game differed from Farmville in almost every way: in 1962 Steve Russell and friends at MIT, readers of E. E. “Doc” Smith’s science fiction, wrote Spacewar for DEC’s PDP-1. Then came Pong, Pac-Man, Space Invaders, Dungeons & Dragons and Tetris, and a console market for eighteen-to-thirty-four-year-olds.
  • Phones were eating that market. In 2003 Nokia noticed gamers carrying both a Game Boy and a phone and built one device to do both, at twice the price of a Game Boy Advance SP. In one survey 61% of app-phone owners had played games, the top category of app. His conclusion: “as tablets grow more powerful, consoles will go the way of Pong.”

Movies: the downfall of the DVD

  • Local video-rental stores appeared in the late 1970s and were crushed by the chains, Blockbuster and Hollywood Video. Blockbuster, founded by a database expert, tailored its stock to neighbourhood tastes, and by May 2010 it was the only nationwide rental chain left.
  • Netflix looked unpromising at first: you chose a film online and it arrived in the mail. But you never had to leave home or spend twenty minutes browsing and queueing. It delivered its billionth DVD in February 2007, then moved to streaming and apps, and the DVD followed the newspaper into oblivion.

Mobile television and the new shared media

  • A Rio de Janeiro cab driver, Marcelo Mendonça Guimarães, likes to watch television on his phone. Yet Qualcomm’s mobile pay-TV service, FLO TV, launched in 2004 with the big US carriers, was shut down. Its central problem, Saylor argues, was the schedule: people wanted their programme at their convenience, anywhere, not a broadcast at a fixed hour on a small screen.
  • The television audience was greying and dwindling; by 2008 the average viewer was about fifty. What survives of shared viewing moves to the second screen: viewers sent a flood of tweets about Lost within twelve hours of its finale, many of them irate, and Super Bowls set records for tweets per second.

TV and advertising: braving a new world

  • When Libyans rose against Muammar Qaddafi in 2011, news reports were sparse at first. Then phone video let readers watch events in depth, down to the capture of a dishevelled Qaddafi himself. Print shows text and photos; the universal screen shows everything.
  • “Advertising is biologically ancient.” A flower’s scent is advertising for bees; sales notices dot the walls of Pompeii; the taverns of ancient Uruk probably had them too. In 1897 the deaf copywriter Earnest Elmo Calkins pushed his bosses to use more pictures in newspaper ads, and his successors followed for a century.
  • The phone is advertising’s ideal medium because it knows who and where you are. Advertisers such as Starbucks and L’Oréal were already running location-based campaigns, and offers can be personal: a free ticket to a music lover’s favourite band, something else for a young parent.

Chapter 5 · Wallet

A Smarter Wallet and Intelligent Money

A wallet is a bundle of money, credit, discounts, identity and keys; the phone can carry all of them as software, and software money can be smarter and safer than cash.

  • The chapter opens with Perseus approaching the Atlas Mountains through a garden of statues that had been people until they looked at Medusa. He found her sleeping, killed her, and carried the head away in a kibisis, a word scholars translate as “wallet”. A wallet holds valuables.
  • Coins went in a small pouch hung from the belt, a bowgette in Middle English, from which we get “budget”. A wallet and its contents were often a person’s means of survival.

The obsolescence of hard cash

  • The first coins probably appeared in Lydia, in today’s Turkey, around 620 BC. They had spectacular advantages over barter and commodities like cowry shells, quickened the flow of wealth, and enriched Lydia.
  • In his Travels (dated 1296), Marco Polo described Kublai Khan manufacturing paper money in such quantities that it could buy all the treasure in the world; China had used paper money since 1024. Many Europeans found the idea absurd.
  • Sweden, short of gold and silver, minted coins of copper weighing about four pounds each, far too heavy for any wallet. Johan Palmstruch, imprisoned for debt in Holland, emigrated to Sweden, founded the Stockholm Banco and lobbied for paper money; in 1661 the crown yielded. After an unfortunate start, paper money spread through Europe and led to the thin modern wallet.
  • Now cash is a cost. The US Bureau of Engraving and Printing prints billions of notes a year; merchants must stock, count and ferry coins; coins have gone from a standard of exchange to an annoyance. Casinos, which handle mountains of them, adopted “Ticket-In Ticket-Out”: the slot machine takes a bill or a ticket and prints a new ticket for your winnings. Digital cash, he says, will end the trucking of dead cash to the dump.

The new banks: a really big Apple

  • In 1317 English churches needed to move a large sum of cash to Rome. The Italian bankers who took it (the book names the Bardi and Peruzzi) sent Rome a receipt, a bill of exchange, which like a cheque was worthless to thieves, and the papacy presented it to the bank’s branch in Rome for payment. Banking, in this telling, has always been the movement of trusted information instead of metal.
  • So a technology company that already holds its customers’ card details is in an intriguing position. Apple’s iTunes account is effectively a credit card, so Apple could bypass Visa, and if it can debit the card it can credit it: “Every single iPhone could become an ATM to every other iPhone.” Such a company could become a much larger consumer bank than any bank.
  • The ATM, he predicts, will disappear along with bank branches and wallets, like the phone booth.

The credit card and the discount card

  • In 1949 Frank McNamara, head of the Hamilton Credit Company, was dining with friends when he hit on the idea of paying for the meal on credit instead of with cash. He founded the Diners Club, the first credit card accepted by a wide variety of merchants; in 1951 it handled about $35 million in transactions. MasterCard and BankAmericard (which became Visa in 1976) arrived in 1966 and now dominate. US credit-card debt stood at $800.5 billion at the end of 2010, down from $974 billion.
  • Discount cards: beyond Groupon. Coupons let a seller give a lower price only to the customers who need one, but they take effort, most go unused, and many are for things people don’t want at that moment. Loyalty cards clog wallets and are barely used. Groupon and LivingSocial send geo-targeted deals by zip code, but blanket discounts train people to wait for them. The phone can replace the whole world of paper, plastic and “Groupon” with loyalty systems that reward frequency and know what each customer actually buys.

Identity, keys and near-field communication

  • The wallet came to hold our identification, and the driver’s licence became a stand-in for the birth certificate. “Both the driver’s license and the password will vanish as forms of ID” in favour of biometrics on the phone, which he surveys one by one: fingerprints, used as identification since eighth-century Japan and Tang China, and famously in a 1905 London murder case; retina and iris scans, already used by the New York police when booking suspects and by the Mexican city of León; and voice recognition, which is not speech recognition, since it identifies the speaker rather than the words.
  • Keys. The hotel key card debuted at the Peachtree Plaza in Atlanta in 1979. Car thefts fell as smart keys spread, and the National Insurance Crime Bureau gave them the credit. A phone key goes further: you could lend a software key to a neighbour that works only while you are away. Mobile car keys made Zipcar possible (launched 2000, more than 550,000 members), and one city replaced more than a hundred government cars with it; Getaround let owners rent out their own cars and keep two-thirds of the fee.
  • Near-field communication: the key. In 1948 Bernard Silver and Norman Woodland overheard a grocery-chain executive complaining about checkout, and the barcode followed. RFID tags added radio; NFC, its short-range descendant, lets a phone talk to a terminal with a tap and no pairing. Google, Microsoft, Visa and American Express were driving it, and he predicts that “before 2020, perhaps 500 million to a billion people will have NFC” in their phones.

The elimination of robbery

  • On 13 January 2011 a pickpocket stole $5,000 from a man’s wallet. Identity thieves took $31 billion from Americans in 2009, and businesses worldwide lose up to $220 billion a year to such fraud, before counting the psychological harm and lost productivity.
  • Digital cash, he argues, knows who owns it and who should have it, and can alert the police if either is suspect. A stolen phone is a locked vault behind biometrics; a mugger’s victim can film the assailant and send the video at once. You will be able to give digital cash to a daughter that works only for a set time and only at chosen stores for school clothes.
  • Privacy fears about paying by phone get short shrift: “This anxiety is misplaced.” Companies generally benefit from respecting customers, he argues, and cases of companies abusing their access to consumers’ privacy “are so uncommon that it is hard to think of any”. Orwell’s Big Brother didn’t need customers; a company that spied would drive them away. (See what held up.)

Chapters 6–10

Services reorganise around continuous connection

The second half applies the model to social life, healthcare, education and poorer countries, and ends by setting the whole wave inside the long history of economic revolutions.

Chapter 6 · Social Networks

A Mobile Social World

Cities were humanity’s first great social network; the mobile social network is a city without walls, able to share news, rate everything, prove who you are and topple a government.

The development of mega-cities

  • The tightest social networks are genetically coded, in the behaviour of insects. Humans eventually outgrew the tribe, and the first mega-city, Uruk in Mesopotamia, held some 50,000 people by the middle of the fourth millennium BC. By 3200 BC it was the pinnacle of human economic complexity, a city entertained by lyre music that incubated one innovation after another.
  • Leaning on Edward Glaeser’s Triumph of the City, Saylor treats density as the engine: cities work because they put many minds in contact. A social network carried in every pocket does the same thing without the walls.

The rise of the social networks

  • Facebook has become the busiest website on earth, and for its users, he suggests, something close to telepathy with their friends. It reported 800 million users in 2011, where earlier networks such as Friendster had faded.
  • LinkedIn, with 100 million users by March 2011, is “the professional person’s Facebook”: it stores our résumés and business cards and lets us reach people through friends of friends. Twitter began with its famous length limit, and its number of users more than doubled. Brands gather followers too: Lady Gaga had 46 million on Facebook, Coca-Cola 37 million, Starbucks 26 million.

The evolving social phenomenon: the Arab Spring

  • On 3 March 2010 a street vendor, Abdesslem Trimech, set himself on fire in Monastir, Tunisia, and almost nobody noticed. On 17 December 2010 another vendor, Mohamed Bouazizi, was selling vegetables from his cart in Sidi Bouzid when a policewoman asked to see his licence; after his protests were ignored he too set himself alight. This time the images spread through personal networks and across the Arab world, fuelled by Facebook and Twitter feeds.
  • In Egypt, a twenty-five-year-old computer-company employee named Asmaa Mahfouz had been handing out flyers in Cairo’s Tahrir Square and was pushed around by security officers. She posted a video calling people to come; on 25 January 2011, 25,000 demonstrators came to Tahrir and to squares across the country, and they refused to leave.
  • By early February the protesters numbered hundreds of thousands. On 2 February Hosni Mubarak’s government sent in plain-clothes security officers and the violence was relayed live. The book reprints timestamped tweets from that night (“The tanks are moving…”, “Sunrise in Cairo”). The crowd held fast, and the tweets, he suggests, may have been crucial to morale. Mubarak resigned days later.
  • For decades rulers such as Mubarak and Qaddafi had cowed their citizens with censorship. Real-time information spread through mobile social networks proved powerful enough to drive revolutions, and today, he writes, neither man reigns, though the ultimate winners are not yet clear.

Crowds, trust and the cost of exposure

  • Mobile social tools also organise less weighty crowds. Flash mobs spread from Sydney to Tokyo to Toronto, with zombie walks, silent raves, subway parties and pillow fights, and “flash mob” entered the Oxford English Dictionary. On a Friday night, he imagines, a map could show the parties around you graded by how much fun they are.
  • The crowd becomes an adviser: a “voting poll” app could ask “Which of these wines is best with fish?”, and recommendations could come from the people most like you, or you could fish the crowd for one expert.
  • A social profile becomes a credential. It is hard to fake hundreds of friends, hundreds of tagged photos and years of history, so a PayPal link on a Facebook page makes it likely you are dealing with a real person, and you have more to lose than an anonymous shop down the street.
  • The same exposure cuts the other way. In September 2010 a Duke student’s mock thesis about her private life, sent to three people, went around the world and wrecked reputations, something that “wouldn’t have happened twenty years ago”. In the Boston “Craigslist Killer” case of 2009, police traced the suspect through hotel cameras and email sent from his BlackBerry. Who, Saylor asks, would dare commit a crime when he can be placed at the crime scene?
  • His conclusion is that privacy and social networking together will drive new changes in society, a theme he returns to in Chapter 10.

Chapter 7 · Medicine

The New Landscape of Global Healthcare

Medicine is advanced in its treatments and primitive in its information; mobile records, sensors and remote specialists can cut its costs and carry care to places that have none.

  • Americans spend $6,102 a head on healthcare each year, about three times as much as the United Kingdom’s $2,083, yet US life expectancy is below that of most European nations (see the figure).

The paper swamp: medical records

  • Modern medicine, he writes, is like a city with gleaming towers and large, primitive slums. Stem-cell therapy can restore sight, yet records are a backwater. The Institute of Medicine concluded as early as 1991 that electronic records were essential, but by 2010 only 20% of US doctors had them. Even trucking was more digitised.
  • A record holds vital signs, medications, prescriptions, allergies, referrals, results including X-rays, insurance and payment details. Because a patient now sees more than one doctor, more people have to read each record, and paper records take up space, cannot be duplicated for an emergency without a copier, and invite errors when handwritten. A chart on a tablet goes wherever the doctor goes, and can be written on while keeping eye contact with the patient.

Telemedicine

  • Around 400 BC Hippocrates told doctors to put an ear to the patient’s chest. In 1816 the French physician René Laennec revived the idea but faced a problem of propriety with women patients, and after much testing he made a stethoscope that was simply a wooden tube. It made the assessment objective and became the symbol of medicine. A digital version can show the patient a waveform. “Why would I listen to ‘lub dub’,” asks the cardiologist Eric Topol of Scripps Health in San Diego, “when I can see everything?”
  • Telemedicine isn’t new. Medieval Europe lit bonfires to relay warnings of plague. In the 1960s a link joined the Nebraska Psychiatric Institute in Omaha to Norfolk State Hospital, 112 miles away, and in 1967 the first complete video system tied Boston’s Logan Airport to Massachusetts General Hospital. What is new is telemedicine that is ubiquitous and affordable.
  • Chronic disease is where it pays. Seventy-four million Americans have hypertension, and some 10% of patients generate about 70% of healthcare costs, most of them with chronic illness. Wristband sensors already track vital signs and alert doctors; an app for joggers read a sensor slotted into the shoe; the vital signs of the elderly can be watched constantly so that their homes are safer. Bill Gates, asked about mobile health, says he thinks first about patient reminders.

The hospital as a network

  • The hospital began inseparable from the temple, and the book traces it through the medieval Hôtels-Dieu of Lyon (founded 542) and Paris (660). The modern hospital becomes a network whose specialists need not share its building: a radiologist can read your scan from Bangalore, and many teleradiologists already do. “Remote” means anywhere.

Portals for the deaf and blind

  • The telephone was once more or less useless to the world’s twenty-five million deaf people; a mobile device can vibrate or flash for calls, act as an audio sensor that warns of sounds, and carry text and video. For blind and visually impaired people it can speak, drive Wi-Fi Braille displays (blind testers tended to prefer Braille to speech), and use crowd-sourcing to enrich maps of the world around them.

Global medical care

  • People have long travelled for health, from the Adirondacks to the Alps. Rising costs, low airfares and the global spread of medical technology drive today’s medical tourism, for surgery such as heart-valve replacement at a fraction of the US price.
  • Yet medical tourism will become increasingly unnecessary, he argues, as the doctor comes to the patient through the screen: the $10 consultation with a physician in Bangalore that Chapter 1 promised. Medical service centres will arise in places such as Brazil and China to serve the world, creating fierce price competition.

Third World Prometheus

  • “Because it’s there,” George Mallory said of Everest. Whatever the level of healthcare in the United States, in the poorest regions mobile technology is a Prometheus, and it bestows a greater gift than fire: “It confers eyes, ears, voice, reach, and knowledge.” It can bring a doctor’s presence to sick farmers in isolated villages and let health authorities watch whole populations for outbreaks.
  • The world’s leading killers, he notes, are chronic: heart disease, emphysema, stroke, cancer and diabetes, exactly the conditions continuous monitoring suits. US life expectancy rose from forty-seven in 1900 to sixty-eight in 1950; mobile medicine, he predicts, will spin the cycle faster, and we will live longer, healthier and better.

Chapter 8 · Education

Remaking Education for Everyone

Schools still run on a Sumerian model; tablets can replace the textbook, make learning active, bring the best teachers to every room and break the rising curve of college costs.

Education matters

  • Formal education is almost as old as writing, and it has always clung to tradition: the Akkadians taught in Sumerian long after the language died, and the Romans taught in Greek. We have used the original Sumerian model for 4,500 years, he says, and it is ready to evolve.

The new textbook

  • “Your future is all used up,” Marlene Dietrich tells Orson Welles in Touch of Evil, and Saylor says the same of the printed textbook. A tablet can hold every textbook and every basic reference manual, kept current, and much of the content is already in the public domain.
  • Roslyn High School on Long Island launched one of the early iPad programmes. Extrapolating from Roslyn’s numbers, he estimates some 276 billion paper copies nationwide a year: schools are paying for “a vast river of paper they don’t need”.

Active learning and virtual worlds

  • At Roslyn, students reported that the devices changed how they worked, and one English teacher began giving small assignments during class. Right after a lecture, students recall about 70% of what was said in the first ten minutes and 20% of the last ten, so fifty-minute periods are inefficient (see the figure). Active learning boosts interest and recall, and tablets, whose screens react to every touch, are made for it.
  • Game playing is active learning too, and old: Senet in Egypt, leela in India, boards in ancient Athens. Virtual worlds go further. Colleges teach courses inside Second Life; one university was building a game called Arden set in the world of Shakespeare; Edusim offers 3D worlds for the classroom. With tagged and augmented reality, a zoo becomes a museum and students can wave a phone at the sun bears to learn about them.

The digital school office and Nobel laureates at the lectern

  • Tablets make the classroom cheaper to run: students take multiple-choice tests and see scores at once, and teachers learn immediately which topics the class hasn’t understood.
  • There are more than 14,000 high schools in the country, he writes; schools have long had trouble finding good teachers for subjects such as physics, and will have more trouble finding them for newly important ones such as Chinese. Mobile technology can bring the nation’s best teachers and top experts into every classroom: Stephen Hawking on cosmology, Ron Howard on film-making, Bill Clinton on politics. At Yale, well-known professors such as the economist Robert Shiller already lecture on free Internet video.

One-to-one learning

  • Alexander the Great was tutored by the best-known scholar of his time, Aristotle. “Soon all students will have their Aristotles”: software that paces itself to each learner, and tutors reachable from anywhere.
  • Start early enough and the gains compound. A toddler in Sri Lanka can start learning English on a tablet, grow up with both languages processed in one hemisphere of the brain, and be connected to other youngsters around the world. (Sri Lanka, newly independent, had replaced English as its official language in 1956.)

Breaking the curve of college costs

  • His own story: his father was an enlisted man who rose to chief master sergeant, and without Air Force financial aid MIT, at $10,000 a year, would have been out of reach. Since the late 1950s, tuition has risen almost twice as fast as inflation. Princeton costs about $55,000 a year for tuition, room, board and books, $220,000 over four years.
  • Higher education, he argues, will see the same transformation as the other industries in the book. Against a payroll he puts at $152 billion a year, the open courseware of MIT and Yale is free. The drop in college costs will usher more people into higher education, and students “won’t have to go into hock to gain a professional’s synapses”.

Global education

  • When Singapore became independent in 1965 it was a seedy, low-slung port with a fine deep-water harbour on the Strait of Malacca but no resources; it had to import its water, food and energy, and its income per head was a fifth of America’s. Lee Kuan Yew saw from the start that education was the precondition of everything. Singapore now has the fifth-highest GDP per head in the world; the United States is eleventh.
  • India’s first prime minister, Jawaharlal Nehru, gave his country a “glittering gift” in five world-class Indian Institutes of Technology. But much of the poor world’s education is corrupt: in Tajikistan students may pay a $2,000 bribe to enter college, and one professor in Georgia handed students a price list. Education itself tends to undermine corruption, he adds, as America’s own Progressive movement showed.
  • His arithmetic of the possible: with a shared tablet and open courseware, “we can create an engineer for $200 a year”, about the price of one textbook in the United States.

Chapter 9 · Developing World

Bootstrapping the Developing World

Poor countries can leap over the landlines, bank branches and bureaucracies they never built; the phone makes markets efficient, cuts out middlemen, brings banking and fights corruption.

Zinder, Niger

  • Zinder was once an outpost on a lonely route across the Sahara; now it is a city of 200,000 in southern Niger, one of the poorest nations on earth. It has seen sultanates rise and fall; its water teems with parasites, and the province has a 62.7% rate of a waterborne eye disease.
  • For grain farmers the trip to market takes two to four hours, and if they don’t get the prices they need the trip is pointless. In Zinder, as across rural Africa, communication has been scarce and slow. Literacy is low (28.7% in Niger, 23.6% in Burkina Faso, 24.0% in Mali), so these remain oral worlds where a voice call is the natural tool.

Instant Internet infrastructure

  • A single 3G or 4G transmitter serves a whole area with no wires to each home. India and China added 300 million new mobile subscribers in 2010, about the population of the United States. Africa had about 137,000 cell towers in 2010, and in most of Africa income per head is under $1,000 a year. Many people there have phones but no electricity.

Market efficiency, and the middleman

  • Kerala. In the dark hours of the morning, sardine fishermen in the Indian state of Kerala put to sea. Before phones, a boat could visit only one market, because the fish were perishable and the markets closed at 8 a.m.; some markets were glutted and dumped fish while others ran short. Phones reached Kerala in 1997 and, since most people live near the coast, towers went up along the shore. By 2001, 60% of boats had phones. Prices across markets converged, waste disappeared, and consumers saved about 4% on fish.
  • “My phone rings sixty or seventy times before I get to port,” a captain of a seventy-four-foot boat said in 2006.
  • In Niger, research by Jenny Aker (with Isaac Mbiti) found that phones let grain traders search more markets and narrowed price differences. Kenya was once a mosaic of isolated markets; now farmers track their local markets by phone. Phones eliminate middleman costs: the farmer can sell direct, and the buyer can compare, which Saylor calls, for consumers in rich countries, “hijacked retail”, browsing in one shop and buying from another.

Financial services, and corruption

  • Lehman Brothers’ collapse on 15 September 2008 froze credit in rich countries, but the Third World has always been a credit desert. Worldwide, 53% of adults are unbanked, against 8% in developed countries (the book prints “2.46 million”; it means about 2.5 billion people). In India, only 18.3 million people had a credit card.
  • Muhammad Yunus opened the Grameen Bank in Bangladesh in 1983; it has lent some $8 billion and claims a 98.6% repayment rate, because borrowing groups create social pressure to repay. But microfinance solves only part of the problem. In a Sri Lankan experiment, researchers gave $100 each to a random sample of small businesses and found returns far above the 12–20% a year that banks charged, so the barrier was access, not opportunity.
  • M-Pesa. Kenya’s M-Pesa (“M” for mobile, pesa for money in Swahili) turned the basic handset into a bank account. There were fourteen million accounts by 2011; “M-Pesa” had become a verb (“I’ll M-Pesa it”), and the average transaction was tiny, about one-seventieth of the average cheque. Perhaps wary of the central bank, it paid no interest and made no loans. In south-west Kenya, Kilimo Salama (“safe farming” in Swahili) was insuring small farmers by phone against bad weather.
  • Corruption. In 2002 a reporter for The Economist rode for four days through Cameroon in a sixty-ton truck carrying 30,000 bottles of Guinness; police stopped it forty-seven times, usually at roadblocks made of tyres, and by the end had taken a third of the cargo. Corruption correlates inversely with growth. Software removes the discretionary clerk: in 1998 the Indian state of Karnataka launched Bhoomi to computerise land records, and within a decade it held twenty million holdings.

The new globalisation and the latecomer advantage

  • From 1750 to about 1950 the economies of the First World pulled away. The road from Third World to First has been paved with exports, as Japan showed, but a rising nation also needs technology. Now globalisation itself will evolve: services, from medicine to teaching, can be exported through the screen.
  • Economists call the absence of antiquated infrastructure the “latecomer advantage”. Mittal Steel built a giant by buying obsolete state-owned mills across the developing world and modernising them. In 1900 much of rural America had no electricity; today’s market entrants, Saylor says, have the greatest latecomer advantage in history. The chapter ends with his summary: a person in rural Africa will have the same information as a person in lower Manhattan.

Chapter 10 · New World

Human Energy Unleashed

Each economic revolution harnessed a new form of energy and freed human effort for new work; the information revolution harnesses “information energy”, mobile computing is its tipping point, and it will come faster than any before.

Three revolutions

  • Agricultural. Humans harnessed bio-energy by domesticating plants and animals. The food surplus freed human energy: hunter-gatherers became farmers, builders, craftsmen and shopkeepers, and the surplus laid the foundation for cities and, eventually, the city-states of Greece and Rome.
  • Industrial. Before steam, mechanical power came only from something already moving: water, wind or a horse. Steam converted one form of energy, heat from burning coal or wood, into another, mechanical motion. Steam engines crossed continents, steam shovels dug canals between oceans, and steam pumps drained coal mines to feed more steam engines. Then electricity became “the universal power platform” and the tipping-point technology that carried the lumbering Industrial Revolution past the limits of steam.
  • Reassigning human energy. In 1840, 69% of the US labour force worked on farms; by 2000 just 1.9% did, and farms still produced more than ever. Each wave of farm automation moved farmhands into manufacturing, and each wave of factory automation moved workers into services. The world is far behind: in 2010, 36.7% of the global labour force, 1.18 billion of 3.23 billion workers, still worked in agriculture, and in India the share was 52% (see the figure).

A new force: information energy

  • The printing press started the Information Revolution; computing from the 1960s accelerated it. Like the revolutions before it, it has energy at its core: it is about harnessing “information energy”, which he admits is not a common phrase. His analogy: coal is the fuel a steam engine turns into the mechanical motion that drives a steamboat; data is the fuel software turns into “information motion”, apps and reports, that drives people to a course of action.
  • Unlike physical energy, information energy is not conserved: it can be consumed but not depleted, and used over and over. Nowhere does it matter more than in services such as banking and insurance, where the product is information.

The economics, the speed and the privacy

  • The economies of revolutions. For most of history, world income per head barely grew; automation has since created great wealth. He acknowledges the pain of transition, citing the unrest of 2010 and US unemployment of 9.6%, but argues that if displaced service workers move on to new services, as farmhands once moved to factories, mobile computing will be far more a benefit than a problem.
  • Change will come fast. “Each revolution comes faster than the previous one.” Agriculture took millennia to spread and industry centuries; this one is spreading in years, and by 2015 there will be one mobile device for every person on earth.
  • Privacy in the new world. Henri Cartier-Bresson won fame for candid street photographs; now anyone with a phone can take them, and share them. Eyes in Sri Lanka or Kazakhstan could follow your day, and someone could set up a sensor to listen outside your house. We need to re-examine the basic principles of privacy and craft “a new theory for privacy and dignity for the individual”, and governments, he warns, are traditionally slow to grasp such things.
  • New world, new rules. Each revolution created an upheaval that needed new rules and new cultural habits, and each disrupted social and political structures; the industrial one helped move societies from monarchy and aristocracy toward democracy. The information revolution will need its own rules.

Final word

  • The projections in the book, he says, are logical extensions of today’s technology, propelled by basic economics. Then the book’s closing image: technology is acid. Unleash it and it burns away accumulated inefficiencies, exposes corruption so that everyone can see it, and etches away the unnecessary, leaving only what is durable enough to withstand it.

“Mobile technology is very powerful acid. It has been unleashed and it will change everything.”Chapter 10, the last lines

Fourteen years on

What held up

The book makes many datable calls, usually in a single confident sentence. Here is how the main ones look from 2026. The quoted predictions are the book’s; the verdicts are our judgement against the public record.

Held

“Mobile computers will become the standard universal computing platform on the planet” 1

For most of the world’s people the phone is the computer, and for billions it is the only one they have used.

Held

By 2015, one mobile device per person 10

Mobile subscriptions roughly matched world population around 2015, by the telecom union’s count. Subscriptions are not people, but the scale arrived on time.

Held

Before 2020, 500 million to a billion people with NFC 5

NFC became standard in smartphones; the iPhone added it with Apple Pay in 2014. Well over a billion people could tap to pay before 2020.

Held

The camera, the DVD and the scheduled broadcast lose to the phone 4

Kodak filed for bankruptcy as the book went to press; compact-camera sales fell by more than nine-tenths from their 2010 peak; streaming on demand beat both the DVD and mobile TV on a schedule.

Held

Mobile money in the developing world 9

Mobile-money accounts passed a billion worldwide in the 2010s, and India built a phone-based payment system (UPI) handling more than ten billion payments a month by 2024.

Held

“Every single iPhone could become an ATM to every other iPhone” 5

Phone-to-phone payments became ordinary: Venmo, Zelle, Apple Cash and their equivalents elsewhere.

Held

Print newspapers face extinction 3

US newspaper ad income kept falling, to under $10 billion by 2020 on Pew Research Center estimates, and more than 2,500 papers closed between 2005 and 2022. Many big titles survived by selling digital subscriptions, a model the chapter barely considers.

Partly

Medical records go digital; care goes remote 7

US doctors did move to electronic records, pushed mainly by federal subsidies from 2009 rather than by phones. Telemedicine exploded in 2020, but licensing kept it mostly within national borders, and the $10 doctor in Bangalore did not arrive.

Partly

“Both the driver’s license and the password will vanish as forms of ID” 5

Fingerprint and face unlock became standard, passkeys arrived in 2022, and some US states put licences in phone wallets from 2022. The plastic card and the password are still everywhere.

Partly

ATMs and bank branches disappear; Apple becomes a bank 5

Branches closed by the thousand in the US and Britain, and Apple launched a credit card (2019) and a savings account (2023). But its card runs on Mastercard with a bank partner, cash machines remain, and the card networks were not bypassed.

Partly

Social networks as engines of democratisation 6

Right about the speed of mobilisation; wrong about where it led. Egypt’s army took power again in 2013, Libya fell into civil war, and governments learned to shut down or flood the same networks.

Missed

“Consoles will go the way of Pong” 4

Mobile became the largest part of the games market by revenue, but consoles thrived: Nintendo’s Switch alone sold well over 140 million units.

Missed

The desktop printer “goes the way of the typewriter”; printed books become luxuries 3

Printers are a shrinking but still multi-billion-dollar business, and e-books levelled off well below half of book sales within a few years of the book; print remained the main format.

Missed

Brick-and-mortar stores disappear 1

Online sales grew to about a sixth of US retail by the mid-2020s. The shops shrank; they did not vanish.

Missed

College costs fall and more people go to university 8

Free courses arrived (2012 was called the year of the MOOC), but completion stayed low, published prices kept rising through the 2010s, and US student debt passed $1.6 trillion.

Missed

Privacy worries about phone payments are “misplaced” 5

The Cambridge Analytica scandal (2018), Europe’s GDPR (2018) and a $5 billion US fine on Facebook (2019) made data abuse one of the defining issues of the decade. His own Chapter 10, which calls for a new theory of privacy, is the better guide.

The other half

What the forecast could not see

The book predicted the capability accurately and the arrangement around it barely at all. These sections are not in the book; they are what a reader now has to supply, because each one decided who the wave was good for.

Blind spot 1

Who Pays, and So Who Is the Customer

The book describes services becoming free without asking what makes them free.

  • Software copies cost almost nothing, so prices fall toward zero. True, and only half a sentence: a zero price is a business model in which the user is not the payer, and by 2012 it was already the industry’s biggest.
  • When the payer is an advertiser, what gets optimised is not your task but your predictability: how reliably attention can be held and intent inferred. Chapter 4 celebrates the phone as advertising’s ideal medium because it knows who and where you are; it does not follow that thought to the business built on it.
  • The evidence was in plain sight: the dominant search company and the dominant social network were advertising businesses, the second going public the year the book came out.
  • What followed proves the point. In 2021 the largest handset maker made apps ask permission before tracking users across other companies’ services, and Meta estimated the change would cost it about $10 billion in 2022. No hardware changed.

Blind spot 2

The Toll Booth in the Middle

Dematerialising a product moves it onto somebody’s platform, and the platform sets the terms.

  • Chapter 1 promises that a direct app on the customer’s phone cuts out the middleman. It did, and a new middleman appeared: nearly all mobile software passes through two app stores, each able to set a commission, impose rules or remove a business.
  • For about a decade the standard commission was thirty per cent, on goods the book rightly says cost almost nothing to copy. It has been trimmed for small developers since 2021 and partly pried open by European regulation and American litigation, but the shape holds.
  • Every dematerialisation chapter assumes the value released goes to users and makers. Much of it went to the layer underneath (see the figure). The book’s own logic predicts this, since whoever controls the scarce layer takes the surplus, but it never runs the argument.

Blind spot 3

The Attention Bill

What makes the device useful all day makes it profitable to keep you on it all day.

  • Every property the early chapters praise (always carried, always connected, personal, full of sensors) is equally a way to interrupt, to measure response and to iterate on whatever holds attention. Lauren’s “hundreds” of friends in Chapter 1 are the start of that story as well as the start of the book.
  • Heavy, fragmented phone use is not in dispute, nor that feeds are tuned for engagement. Whether this caused the worsening of adolescent mental health seen in several countries from the early 2010s is argued by serious researchers on both sides, on mostly correlational evidence (see debates). Hold the mechanism as clear and the size as unsettled.
  • Chapter 6 expected everyone to gain a voice, and was right. It did not expect that the same distribution, ranked by response, would carry fabrications faster than corrections.

Blind spot 4

What Refused to Dematerialise

Software ate the artefact and left the supply chain, the building and the waste where they were.

  • Chapter 3 counts the forests and fumes paper costs. The cloud has its own bill: photos and maps live in data centres drawing continuous power, on the order of one to two per cent of world electricity and rising fast with machine learning, which the book did not foresee at all.
  • The phone is cobalt, lithium, copper and rare earths, replaced every few years. Electronic waste passed sixty million tonnes a year in the early 2020s and grows faster than it is recycled.
  • Retail is the counter-example to “brick-and-mortar stores will disappear”: the shop became software, and the result was one of the largest physical build-outs in commercial history, in warehouses, delivery fleets and new kinds of work (see the figure).

Coda

The Author’s Own Sequel

He pushed the thesis one step further than the book, and staked a public company on it.

  • The book argues that anything whose value is informational will move into software, one way. From August 2020 the software company Saylor led began converting its treasury into bitcoin, and it kept buying, with borrowed and newly raised money, until it held more than half a million coins in 2025 and the holding, not the software, was why people watched the share price. The company renamed itself Strategy in 2025.
  • Chapter 5 already has the seed: money as information, with the heavy tokens falling away. But it expected the phone to make existing money intelligent; the sequel bets on a new money altogether.
  • The move shows the thesis is held sincerely. It also shows it working without the discipline that made the 2012 chapters good: those predicted what a technology would make possible, while this predicts what people will choose to hold value in, which is about institutions, trust and politics, where the book’s method is weakest.

Context

Saylor and other forecasters

The book cites journalism and a few scholars, and does not engage the older theorists whose arguments it echoes. Here it is set beside both.

Context

Where it has been argued with

Did phones make the Arab Spring?

Clay Shirky’s “The Political Power of Social Media” (Foreign Affairs, 2011) argued that networked tools strengthen civil society; Malcolm Gladwell’s “Small Change” (The New Yorker, 2010) that weak online ties cannot carry high-risk activism; Morozov that regimes would use the tools too. The outcomes, from Egypt’s 2013 coup to the Libyan and Syrian wars, sit closer to the sceptics than to Chapter 6, though the speed of mobilisation Saylor describes was real.

Does mobile money reduce poverty?

Tavneet Suri and William Jack reported in Science (2016) that access to M-Pesa lifted about 2% of Kenyan households out of extreme poverty, strong support for Chapter 9. Milford Bateman and colleagues contested the method and the size of the effect in 2019. The consensus that mobile money helps households manage risk is firmer than the estimate of how much it cuts poverty.

Where is the productivity?

Robert Solow’s 1987 quip that the computer age shows up everywhere but the productivity statistics has survived the smartphone: US productivity growth after 2005 was slower than before. Gordon (2016) reads this as the information revolution being smaller than the industrial one; Erik Brynjolfsson and Andrew McAfee (The Second Machine Age, 2014) as a lag before the gains arrive. Chapter 10 assumes the optimists’ answer.

Can free courses replace college?

2012, the book’s own year, was hailed as the year of the MOOC. A 2019 study in Science by Justin Reich and José Ruipérez-Valiente found completion rates in the low single digits and most learners already well educated and in rich countries. Access to content, it turned out, is not the same as completion, credentials or cost.

The attention bill

Jonathan Haidt’s The Anxious Generation (2024) blames smartphones and social media for the rise in adolescent anxiety and depression from about 2012. Candice Odgers’s review in Nature (2024) and others argue the evidence is mostly correlational and the effects small. The book, written just before the period in question, does not raise it.

Sourcing, and some slips

The notes run to hundreds of press and web citations, which makes the book a good record of how 2011 looked but thin on research. Some errors a careful reader will catch: Apple was founded in 1976, not 1978; the first microprocessor was Intel’s 4004 of 1971, not a “4040” of 1975; the transistor’s Nobel Prize came in 1956, not 1953; the unbanked are about 2.5 billion, not million; and a fall from $48.7 billion to $22.8 billion is 53%, not the 70% the text says.

Words that do a lot of work

Glossary

Mobile wave
The fifth wave of computing, after mainframe, minicomputer, desktop and Internet PC: the networked computer carried by the person.
Universal computer
Saylor’s claim that the phone and tablet become the standard computing platform on the planet, by sheer numbers.
Direct application linkage
A company’s own app on the customer’s phone, cutting out retail, distribution and service middlemen.
One-to-one marketing
A relationship with each customer individually, made possible by that app.
Solid and vapor software
Software installed on one machine versus software that surrounds you wherever you are.
Second-copy revolution
His name for printing: what changed was the cost of each copy after the first.
Universal screen
One personal screen that absorbs camera, console, DVD, television and advertising.
Micro-play
Games built from many short sessions, the secret he finds in Angry Birds.
Intelligent money
Digital cash that knows who owns it and who may spend it, and can carry limits and rules.
Bill of exchange
A medieval banker’s receipt, worthless to thieves; his model for money as information.
TITO
“Ticket-In Ticket-Out”, the casino industry’s escape from handling coins.
RFID and NFC
Radio tags, and the short-range version that lets a phone pay or open a door with a tap.
Mobile ID
Identity proved by the phone and biometrics (fingerprint, iris, voice) instead of a licence or password.
Mega-city
Uruk and its successors: dense human networks that incubate invention.
Flash mob
A crowd convened in minutes by phone, from pillow fights to protests.
Telemedicine
Care and specialist review delivered at a distance; old in principle, newly cheap and everywhere.
Third World Prometheus
The phone as a gift greater than fire to poor regions: eyes, ears, voice, reach and knowledge.
Latecomer advantage
The benefit of having no obsolete infrastructure to replace.
Credit desert
His term for poor countries, where most adults have no bank account.
M-Pesa
Kenya’s mobile-money system on basic handsets: “M” for mobile, pesa for money.
Hijacked retail
Browsing in one shop and buying from another on the phone.
Tipping-point technology
The technology that accelerates a revolution: electricity for industry, mobile computing for information.
Information energy
Useful information that drives people to act; unlike physical energy, it is never used up.
Information motion
What software makes from data, apps and reports, as a steam engine makes motion from coal.
Technology is acid
The closing image: it burns away inefficiency and leaves what is durable.

Using it

Reading it

It is a quick read, about 240 pages of short sections. Read it as a 2012 document: its value now is seeing which parts became ordinary and which assumptions failed. If you have an evening:

  1. Chapters 1 and 10

    The frame: the lunch with Lauren, the three revolutions, information energy and the acid.

  2. Chapters 3–5

    Paper, the universal screen and the wallet: the dematerialisation argument at its clearest, with the best history.

  3. Chapter 9

    Kerala, Niger and M-Pesa: the chapter whose claims rest on real measurement, and the one that aged best.

  4. For each chapter, ask who pays

    The question the book never asks is the one that explains what got built.

Read alongside

  • The Beginning of Infinity, summarised on this site: optimism about knowledge, argued without a product roadmap.
  • Why Nations Fail, summarised on this site: why a new technology’s gains depend on who controls the institutions around it, the missing half of Chapters 6 and 9.
  • The Book of Elon, summarised on this site: a newer forecast from inside the industry, not yet scoreable.
  • Nicholas Negroponte, Being Digital (1995): the bits-not-atoms argument this book inherits.
  • Tim Wu, The Master Switch (2010): why open platforms tend to close, the toll booth the book missed.
