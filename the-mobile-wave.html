The argument in one pass

The first computing waves put machines in organisations, then offices, then homes. The mobile wave puts a networked computer on the person. That shift changes both scale and intimacy.

Chapter 01

The Wave: The Shape of the Wave

Mobile computing is the delivery mechanism for a wider information revolution.

From information technology to information energy

Saylor places mobile computing inside a long sequence of civilisation-scale changes. Agriculture reorganised food and settlement; industry reorganised mechanical energy and production; the information revolution reorganises decisions, coordination and the use of human attention.

His key unit is not the gadget but information energy: useful information arriving where and when it can change an action. A price in a farmer’s hand, a diagnosis at a bedside or a map during a journey has more economic force than the same data stored in a distant office.

Why mobile changes the slope

The desktop web reached people mainly while they were seated at a connected computer. The smartphone stays with its owner, knows something about context and can deliver software throughout the waking week. This multiplies both the population reached and the number of moments in which computing can intervene.

A shared operating system, app model and wireless network also compress distribution. One software improvement can reach millions without constructing a new branch, printing a new edition or installing a specialised machine at every site. That leverage is why Saylor expects the wave to remake institutions rather than merely add convenience.

Scale

Billions of users

The relevant market expands from trained office workers to children, older adults and people in countries with little fixed computing infrastructure.

Intensity

All-day availability

A carried computer participates in transactions and decisions that a desktop never encounters.

Retrospective lens: The category prediction was strong, but “information energy” can power manipulation as well as productivity. Reach magnifies the incentives of whoever controls the software and feed.

Chapter 02

Computers: The Evolution to Mobile Computing

Each wave moves computation closer to the individual and expands the population that can use it.

Five successive expansions of access

Mainframes were room-sized capital equipment shared by large institutions and operated by specialists. Minicomputers lowered cost enough for departments and smaller organisations. The personal-computer wave put processing on an individual desk and let packaged software address millions of workers.

The Internet connected those machines, turning the PC into a window onto remote information and services. But it remained anchored to offices, desks, keyboards and deliberate sessions. The mobile wave detaches the networked computer from place.

A new interface and a new design subject

Touch removes layers of keyboard and mouse convention. Cameras, microphones, GPS and motion sensors make the device aware of the surrounding world. Batteries and wireless networks allow it to remain useful while the owner moves.

The design subject consequently changes from “the worker at a terminal” to “the person in context.” Software can respond to identity, location, history and nearby objects. The device is simultaneously a computer, sensor bundle, communication channel and authentication token.

Economic pattern

Lower cost, larger audience

Each wave reduces the infrastructure and expertise required per user while expanding the range of viable applications.

Fifth-wave novelty

Persistent context

The computer is no longer waiting at a destination; it accompanies the person and observes the situation in which a decision occurs.

What the five-wave story simplifies: Earlier forms do not vanish. Mainframes survive as cloud data centres, PCs remain superior for many creative tasks, and mobile depends on the fixed infrastructure it appears to transcend.

Chapter 03

Paper: The Demise of Paper

Paper persists because it is cheap and universal; mobile wins when software makes the document active.

Each medium lowers a different constraint

The historical arc begins with durable but heavy clay tablets, moves through papyrus and parchment, and reaches paper as a cheap, portable surface. Gutenberg’s movable-type system dramatically lowered the cost of reproducing consistent pages, helping books and literacy spread beyond a narrow elite.

Desktop publishing lowered composition costs but still produced physical objects that had to be printed, warehoused and transported. A mobile document eliminates much of that chain and can be delivered globally the moment it changes.

The page becomes active

Maps are the clearest example. A paper map represents a place at one printing date; a mobile map locates the user, searches destinations, calculates routes, reports traffic and updates its database. It replaces not just the sheet but the work formerly performed around the sheet.

Books, manuals, tickets, receipts, newspapers and forms follow the same transition when search, links, translation, personalisation and live updates matter. The strongest digital product is therefore not a scan of paper. It is a service whose output happens to include readable pages.

Papyrus, parchment and paper

Retrospective lens: Paper declined most where information is time-sensitive or transactional. It remains valuable for durability, ownership, deep reading, legal convention and situations where power or connectivity cannot be assumed.

Chapter 04

Entertainment: The New Universal Screen

One personal screen absorbs television, cinema, music, games and the advertising systems around them.

Camera, console and cinema converge

Digitisation already separated photographs, music and video from physical media. Mobile completes the loop by combining capture, editing, playback and distribution in one carried device. An event can be recorded and reach a global audience before a traditional broadcaster arrives.

Casual games such as Angry Birds and social games such as FarmVille illustrated a broader audience than the dedicated console market. App stores lowered distribution barriers, while free-to-play and in-app purchases changed how games were financed.

On demand displaces the schedule

Streaming services replace the trip to a video store and weaken the fixed broadcast timetable. Saylor uses failed scheduled mobile-TV services as evidence that people did not merely want television on a smaller screen; they wanted control over time and selection.

The universal screen is also a measurement surface. Every view, pause, share and purchase can feed recommendation and advertising systems. Entertainment becomes more responsive and participatory, but discovery shifts from channel owners toward platform algorithms.

Convergence

One device, many media roles

The same screen records, edits, distributes, displays, recommends and monetises content.

Business-model shift

From audience estimate to event stream

Platforms observe individual behaviour in real time and sell targeting, subscriptions and in-app transactions.

What persisted: Mobile became a universal control and viewing surface, but cinemas, televisions, consoles and professional cameras survived where immersion, ergonomics or specialised quality matter.

Chapter 05

Wallet: A Smarter Wallet and Intelligent Money

The wallet is a bundle of identity, payment, access and memory — all functions a trusted device can perform.

The wallet was always a software bundle in leather

Saylor opens with the ancient kibisis carried by Perseus and follows the container as coins, paper money, identity cards, credit and access credentials accumulate. The modern wallet is less one object than a collection of social permissions.

A trusted mobile device can unbundle and recombine those functions. Payment cards, tickets, coupons, keys, licences and transaction history become software objects selected at the moment they are needed.

Authentication makes money programmable

Near-field communication allows a short-range exchange with a terminal; biometrics can connect the device to a person; cloud services can check risk and settle the transaction. A payment can automatically attach loyalty, receipts, spending limits or purpose restrictions.

Saylor imagines technology firms competing with banks because the most valuable customer relationship may belong to the interface that authenticates identity and organises daily transactions. The convenience is powerful, but it also makes operating-system access, payment rules and account suspension matters of civic importance.

Wallet functions

Identity + value + access

A physical container holds proofs and permissions that software can authenticate, update and invoke contextually.

Intelligent money

Rules travel with payment

Time, location, merchant category, allowance, discount and receipt can become part of one transaction.

Retrospective lens: Mobile payments and digital tickets landed; the complete wallet arrived more slowly because governments, banks and merchants must agree on standards, liability and identity. Centralisation also creates new forms of exclusion and surveillance.

Chapter 06

Social Networks: A Mobile Social World

A social network carried everywhere becomes an operating layer for identity, coordination and public events.

From destination to ambient layer

Desktop social networks asked the user to visit a site. Mobile notifications, cameras and location make the network accompany the user. Messages, photographs and reactions are produced from inside the event and received while the recipient is moving through another.

Saylor traces a path from cities and mass media toward platforms such as Facebook, Twitter and LinkedIn. The key change is many-to-many coordination: ordinary participants become publishers, organisers and live witnesses rather than only an audience.

Coordination at political speed

The Arab Spring provides the chapter’s emblematic case. Images and calls to action circulated rapidly across personal networks, lowering the cost of finding allies and coordinating gatherings. The same structure lets groups form around commerce, emergencies, hobbies and professional identity.

Mobile and social reinforce one another: a more useful network motivates more device use, and a carried device supplies more timely content to the network. That positive feedback can create extraordinary utility—and platform concentration once everyone’s relationships depend on the same graph.

New capability

Broadcast from the scene

The witness no longer needs a studio or newsroom to distribute text, images and video globally.

New institution

Social coordination system

Identity, messaging, group formation, reputation and event organisation become one software layer.

Retrospective lens: The book emphasises collective empowerment. The same architecture also optimises outrage, accelerates falsehood, enables surveillance and grants feed operators immense power over public attention.

Chapter 07

Medicine: The New Landscape of Global Healthcare

Healthcare shifts from episodic visits toward continuous measurement, remote expertise and patient-held information.

Healthcare has an information bottleneck

Many clinical interactions consist of collecting symptoms, consulting records, interpreting measurements, giving instructions and checking what happened next. Mobile can move parts of that information loop outside the clinic without pretending that the physical work of medicine disappears.

Telemedicine extends scarce expertise across distance. Reminders and decision support can improve adherence. A patient-held record can reduce fragmentation when the person moves among clinics, pharmacies and hospitals.

From episodic snapshots to streams

Traditional care often measures a person during a brief appointment. Connected sensors can follow heart rate, activity, glucose, sleep or symptoms between visits, making trends visible before the next scheduled encounter.

The operating model becomes triage: software handles routine monitoring and routes anomalies toward nurses or physicians. The economic promise is scale, but safe scale requires validation, escalation rules, secure records and clarity about who is responsible when an algorithm is wrong.

Access layer

Remote expertise

Communication moves advice and specialist review to patients who cannot easily reach the facility.

Measurement layer

Continuous monitoring

Sensors reveal patterns and exceptions that an occasional office measurement can miss.

Retrospective lens: Telehealth and wearables expanded, but healthcare does not dematerialise like music. Diagnosis, trust, procedures, regulation and unequal ability to act on advice keep the physical system central.

Chapter 08

Education: Remaking Education for Everyone

When instruction travels with the learner, education can separate from the timetable and the classroom.

Separate learning from one room and one pace

A tablet can carry textbooks, lectures, simulations, dictionaries and practice systems at a fraction of the distribution cost of a physical library. Content can be translated and updated without waiting for a new print cycle.

Recorded instruction makes repetition cheap. A learner can pause, replay and study outside school hours, while a global course can reach people excluded by geography or tuition. Saylor’s strongest claim is that high-quality instruction can become a software service with very low marginal delivery cost.

The real product is feedback

A video library reproduces broadcast education. A mobile learning system can test understanding, detect which prerequisite is missing, adjust the next exercise and connect the learner to a teacher or peer at the difficult moment.

This changes the teacher’s potential role from repeating one lecture toward diagnosing, motivating and mentoring. But access to content is not the same as completion or mastery. Good pedagogy, social support, language, time and a credible pathway to opportunity remain decisive.

Cost change

Global replication

Once produced, a lesson can reach another learner without printing another book or building another lecture hall.

Pedagogical change

Adaptive practice

The system can respond to the learner’s errors instead of moving an entire class through one timetable.

Retrospective lens: Mobile transformed access more than institutions. Completion, credential value, motivation and unequal home conditions explain why abundant lessons did not automatically produce abundant education.

Chapter 09

Developing World: Bootstrapping the Developing World

Countries can leap over missing fixed infrastructure when services arrive through inexpensive wireless devices.

Deliver the function without copying the building

A country with few landlines need not wire every home before enabling communication; it can build cellular coverage. The same logic applies to banking branches, information desks and parts of education or healthcare. Wireless service can leap over a missing layer of fixed infrastructure.

Mobile money is the clearest mechanism. An account linked to a basic handset lets people store value, transfer funds and pay merchants without a nearby bank. Transaction histories can support credit and reduce the cost of serving small or remote customers.

Information changes bargaining power

A farmer or trader with current market prices can choose where to sell instead of accepting the only local offer. Digital identity and records can make a person legible to institutions that previously could not verify them.

Leapfrogging is strongest where incumbents and legacy systems are weak. Yet the phone complements rather than replaces electricity, radio spectrum, roads, literacy, legal trust and competition. A monopoly network or exclusionary identity system can reproduce the old bottleneck in software.

Leapfrog

Cell tower before landline

Shared wireless infrastructure can deliver population-scale communication without duplicating the rich world’s historical build sequence.

Economic example

Mobile money and market prices

Payment and information reduce the distance between rural participants and wider markets.

Pressure point: “Developing world” is too broad for one trajectory. Institutions, language, gender, affordability and state control shape who benefits and whether dependency shifts from local gatekeepers to global platforms.

Chapter 10

New World: Human Energy Unleashed

When information, coordination and transactions become immediate, human effort can move from friction toward problem-solving.

Technology as acid

Saylor’s closing metaphor is that technology burns away accumulated inefficiency. Paper handoffs, scheduled broadcasts, retail inventory, cash reconciliation and information monopolies become visible costs once a mobile alternative coordinates the same function instantly.

Incumbents often mistake the device for a new channel and place an app beside the existing organisation. Native competitors redesign the workflow around identity, real-time data and software distribution, removing the branch, schedule or intermediary from the core process.

Human energy moves—and so does power

Automation destroys some jobs and tasks while lowering the cost of forming new services. Saylor compares this with earlier productivity shifts: fewer people are required for routine coordination, releasing effort for work that was previously uneconomic or impossible.

The optimistic endpoint is billions of people able to learn, transact and organise at very low marginal cost. A retrospective reading adds that the released value is not automatically shared. App stores, cloud platforms, data owners and identity providers can become the new toll collectors.

Incumbent response

Digitise the old workflow

Adds a mobile interface while preserving the organisation’s branch structure, handoffs and economics.

Native response

Rebuild around software

Starts with persistent identity, real-time coordination and global distribution, then adds physical operations only where necessary.

Whole-book connection: Mobile matters when it combines a carried computer, shared software platform, sensor context and network effects to replace an institution’s coordination mechanism—not merely one of its objects.

The other half

What the forecast could not see

The book predicted the capability accurately and the arrangement around it barely at all. These four chapters are not in it — they are what a reader in the mobile-first era has to supply, because each one turned out to determine who the wave was actually good for.

Blind spot 01

Who pays, and therefore who is the customer

The book describes services becoming free without asking what makes them free, and the answer determined almost everything that followed.

The missing mechanism

Saylor’s central economic observation is that software replication costs approximately nothing, so the price of a dematerialised good collapses toward zero. That is correct and it is only half a sentence. A price of zero is not an absence of a business model; it is a business model in which the user is not the payer, and by 2012 that model was already the largest in the industry. The book notices the free map and not the advertising auction that pays for it.

The consequence is structural rather than moral. When the payer is an advertiser, the product being optimised is not the user’s task but the user’s predictability — how reliably attention can be captured and how precisely intent can be inferred. Every design decision downstream inherits that objective. A book about mobile computing that never names the auction is missing the force that shaped what got built.

How visible this already was

This is not hindsight applied unfairly. The dominant search company had been an advertising business since 2000, the dominant social network since 2007, and both were public companies filing accounts that said so plainly; the second of them went public the same year the book appeared. The information was on the record. The forecast simply treated the capability layer as the whole story, which is the characteristic error of technologist futurism — and the reason its predictions about capability landed while its predictions about consequence did not.

What followed

The decade’s most consequential mobile policy change was not a device or a network but a privacy setting: when the largest handset maker required apps to ask permission before tracking users across other companies’ services, one advertising business publicly estimated the cost to its revenue in the billions within a year. Nothing about the hardware changed. A rule about who may observe whom moved more value than any product launch in the same period — which is a precise measure of how much of this industry’s economics the book left out.

The test for any technology forecast: it should name the payer. A prediction that a capability will become free and universal is incomplete until it says who funds the freeness, because that party’s interests, not the technology’s properties, decide what the thing becomes.

Blind spot 02

The toll booth in the middle

Dematerialising a product moves it onto somebody’s platform, and the platform sets the terms.

From distribution to permission

The book celebrates the collapse of distribution: one software update reaches millions with no branches, no print run, no installed machines. True — but distribution did not disappear, it consolidated. It moved into two application stores through which essentially all mobile software must pass, each able to set a commission, impose rules, and remove a business from the market entirely. The old distribution cost was paid to printers, shippers and retailers, many of them substitutable. The new one is paid to a duopoly that also owns the operating system, the payment rail and the default applications competing with yours.

For a decade the standard commission was around thirty per cent of revenue — a rate that would be extraordinary in almost any physical retail channel, applied to a good whose replication cost the book correctly says is near zero. It has since been trimmed for small developers and partially pried open by European regulation and by litigation, but the shape holds: the entity that owns the platform captures a large, non-negotiable share of everything dematerialised onto it.

Why this is the deepest miss

Every one of the book’s dematerialisation chapters implicitly assumes the value released by removing the physical object accrues to the user and the maker. Mostly it did not. It accrued to the layer underneath. Newspapers dematerialised and the advertising revenue went to search and social; music dematerialised and the margin went to streaming platforms; retail dematerialised and the terms went to marketplaces. The book’s own logic predicts this — whoever controls the scarce layer captures the surplus, and the scarce layer in a world of free replication is access to the customer — but it never runs the argument.

Blind spot 03

The attention bill

The same properties that make the device useful all day make it profitable to keep you on it all day.

The symmetry the book misses

Every capability listed approvingly in the early chapters — always carried, always connected, personal, sensor-rich — is equally a capability to interrupt, to measure response and to iterate on what holds attention. The book treats these as neutral properties that institutions will use for medicine, education and commerce. They were also the raw material of a decade of optimisation against a metric that has no natural stopping point, in an industry paid by the impression.

What followed is genuinely contested and should be reported as such. That adults report heavy, fragmented phone use is not in dispute; that recommendation systems are tuned for engagement is not in dispute; whether this caused the deterioration in adolescent mental health recorded across several countries from the early 2010s is actively argued, with serious researchers on both sides and the evidence mostly correlational. A reader should hold the mechanism as clear and the magnitude as unsettled. What is not defensible is the book’s framing, in which continuous connection has an upside column and no downside column at all.

Information disorder

The chapter on human energy unleashed anticipates coordination, publishing and political voice reaching everyone, and it was right. It did not anticipate that the same distribution, optimised by the same metric, would carry fabrications faster than corrections, or that a feed ranked by response would systematically favour the emotionally activating over the accurate. This is the book’s optimism in its purest form: it models the removal of a constraint and not the incentive that rushes into the space.

Blind spot 04

What refused to dematerialise

Software ate the artefact and left the supply chain, the building and the waste stream exactly where they were.

The cloud is a building

The book’s rhetorical move is to show a shelf of physical objects — camera, map, torch, ticket, ledger, record collection — collapsing into one device, and it is a genuinely good demonstration. What it omits is that the collapse relocates rather than abolishes. The photograph still exists; it lives in a warehouse of servers drawing continuous power. The map is rendered by a data centre. Global data-centre electricity consumption is on the order of a per cent or more of world demand and has been climbing since, sharply so with the arrival of large-scale machine learning, which the book did not foresee at all.

The device itself is the same story. A phone is cobalt, lithium, copper, rare earths and a supply chain that includes artisanal mining, and the fleet is replaced every few years by design. Electronic waste passed sixty million tonnes a year in the early 2020s and grows faster than it is recycled. None of this contradicts the book’s thesis; all of it is missing from its accounting, which is written as though information were weightless because its marginal copy is.

The counter-example the book uses against itself

Retail is the clearest case. Commerce dematerialised exactly as predicted — the catalogue, the cheque, the queue and eventually the shop all became software — and the result was one of the largest physical build-outs in commercial history: warehouses, sortation centres, delivery fleets, and a labour force doing work that did not exist before. The interface dematerialised. The logistics materialised, at greater scale than the shops they replaced. Any forecast that counts only the disappearing object will get the second half of this backwards.

Coda

The author’s own sequel

He followed the thesis one step further than the book does, and staked a public company on it.

Dematerialising the balance sheet

The book argues that any good whose value is informational will migrate into software, and that the migration is one-way. In 2020 the software company Saylor led began converting its corporate treasury into bitcoin, and continued until the holding, rather than the software business, was the reason anyone looked at the share price. Whatever one thinks of the trade, it is the same argument applied to money itself: if value can be represented as information, it eventually will be, and the physical representation is the legacy artefact.

For a reader of this book the episode is useful in two ways. It confirms that the dematerialisation thesis is held sincerely and pushed to its conclusion rather than deployed as marketing. And it shows the thesis operating without the discipline that made the 2012 chapters good: those predicted what a technology would make possible, which is a claim about capability; this predicts what people will choose to hold value in, which is a claim about institutions, trust and politics — the category where, as the rest of this summary keeps finding, the method is weakest.

Read the coda as evidence, not endorsement: it is a live test of whether the book’s central intuition generalises past the point where the constraint is physical. That test is still running, and this summary takes no position on it.
