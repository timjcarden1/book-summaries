The Mobile Wave

How Mobile Intelligence Will Change Everything

A smartphone is not a smaller desktop. It is a personal computer that never leaves you, and it will absorb whole categories of objects and reorganise the institutions around them.

The book’s argument, in our words

Michael J. Saylor · 2012

The whole book in one breath

Every computing wave moved the machine closer to the person and multiplied who could use it; this one puts a networked, sensor-laden computer in everyone’s pocket all day. Once it is always there, it can replace paper, screens, wallets and service counters, and each replacement removes friction, creates data and turns a static product into a service that keeps improving. Saylor follows the idea through paper, entertainment, money, social life, medicine, education and the developing world, and ends with technology as an acid that burns away inefficiency and frees human energy. Read now, the capability forecasts landed almost everywhere. What the book did not see was who would own the arrangement: who pays, who takes the toll, what the attention bill is, and what stayed stubbornly physical.

Orientation

The argument in one pass

The first computing waves put machines in organisations, then offices, then homes. The mobile wave puts a networked computer on the person, which changes both the scale and the intimacy. Once the device is persistent, personal and full of sensors, it can replace paper, screens, wallets and service counters, and every replacement removes friction, creates data, and turns a static product into a service.

Chapters 1–2

A computer becomes part of the person

The important change is not processor size. It is the collapse of the distance between software and daily behaviour.

Chapter 1

The Wave: The Shape of the Wave

Mobile computing is the delivery mechanism for a wider information revolution.

  • Saylor sets mobile in a long run of civilisation-scale changes: agriculture reorganised food and settlement, industry reorganised energy and production, and the information revolution reorganises decisions, coordination and attention.
  • His unit is information energy: information that arrives where and when it can change an action. A price in a farmer’s hand or a map during a journey has more economic force than the same data filed away.
  • The desktop web reached people only while they sat at a computer. The phone stays with its owner through the waking week, multiplying both the people reached and the moments computing can step in.
  • Shared operating systems, app stores and wireless networks compress distribution: one improvement reaches millions with no new branch, edition or machine. That leverage is why he expects institutions to be remade, not just made convenient.

Chapter 2

Computers: The Evolution to Mobile Computing

Each wave moves computation closer to the individual and widens who can use it.

  • Mainframes were shared capital equipment run by specialists; minicomputers reached departments; the PC reached every desk; the internet connected the desks but stayed tied to them (see the figure).
  • Touch removes layers of convention. Cameras, microphones, GPS and motion sensors make the device aware of its surroundings; batteries and wireless keep it useful on the move.
  • The design subject changes from “the worker at a terminal” to “the person in context.” The device is a computer, a sensor bundle, a communication channel and a key to your identity at once.

Chapters 3–5

Objects collapse into applications

Paper, entertainment screens and wallets look like separate industries. Saylor treats them as bundles of information, identity and transactions that software can recombine.

Chapter 3

Paper: The Demise of Paper

Paper survives because it is cheap and universal; mobile wins when software makes the document active.

  • The arc runs from clay tablets through papyrus and parchment to paper; Gutenberg made consistent pages cheap and spread literacy beyond an elite. Media change when cost, climate, scarcity or control make a substitute more useful.
  • Desktop publishing cut the cost of composing pages but still printed, stored and shipped objects. A mobile document skips that chain and is delivered worldwide the moment it changes.
  • Maps are the clearest case: a paper map shows a place as of one printing; a phone map finds you, searches, routes, reports traffic and updates itself. Books, manuals, tickets, receipts, newspapers and forms follow when search, links and live updates matter.

Chapter 4

Entertainment: The New Universal Screen

One personal screen absorbs television, cinema, music, games and the advertising around them.

  • The phone combines capture, editing, playback and distribution, so an event can reach a global audience before a broadcaster arrives.
  • Casual and social games showed an audience far wider than consoles; app stores lowered the barriers, and free-to-play with in-app purchases changed how games were paid for.
  • Streaming beats the fixed schedule. Saylor reads the failure of scheduled mobile TV as proof that people wanted control of time and choice, not television on a smaller screen.
  • The screen also measures: every view, pause and share feeds recommendation and advertising, and discovery moves from channel owners to platform algorithms.

Chapter 5

Wallet: A Smarter Wallet and Intelligent Money

A wallet is a bundle of identity, payment, access and memory, all things a trusted device can do.

  • From Perseus’s bag in myth to coins, notes, ID cards, credit and access passes, the wallet has always been less an object than a collection of social permissions.
  • A trusted phone can unbundle and recombine them: cards, tickets, coupons, keys and licences become software objects picked at the moment of need.
  • Short-range radio talks to the terminal, biometrics tie the device to a person, and the cloud checks risk and settles. A payment can carry loyalty, receipts, spending limits or rules about what it may buy.
  • He expects technology firms to compete with banks, because the most valuable relationship may belong to whoever authenticates you. Which also makes operating-system access, payment rules and account suspension matters of civic importance.

Chapters 6–10

Services reorganise around continuous connection

The second half applies the model to social life, healthcare, education, poorer countries, and finally the economy as a whole.

Chapter 6

Social Networks: A Mobile Social World

A social network carried everywhere becomes an operating layer for identity, coordination and public events.

  • Desktop social networks were places you visited. On the phone, notifications, cameras and location make the network come with you, produced from inside events and read on the move.
  • The key change is many-to-many coordination: ordinary people become publishers, organisers and live witnesses, not only an audience.
  • The Arab Spring is the emblematic case: images and calls to act spread through personal networks, cutting the cost of finding allies and gathering.
  • Mobile and social feed each other, which creates great usefulness, and concentration once everyone’s relationships sit on the same graph.

Chapter 7

Medicine: The New Landscape of Global Healthcare

Care shifts from occasional visits towards continuous measurement, remote expertise and patient-held records.

  • Much of medicine is an information loop: collect symptoms, consult records, interpret, instruct, check what happened. Mobile can move parts of it outside the clinic without pretending the physical work disappears.
  • Telemedicine stretches scarce expertise; reminders improve adherence; a record the patient carries reduces fragmentation between providers.
  • Sensors can follow heart rate, activity, glucose or sleep between visits, showing trends before the next appointment. Software handles routine monitoring and routes anomalies to people (see the figure).
  • Safe scale needs validation, escalation rules, secure records, and clarity about who is responsible when an algorithm is wrong.

Chapter 8

Education: Remaking Education for Everyone

When teaching travels with the learner, education can separate from the timetable and the classroom.

  • A tablet can carry textbooks, lectures, simulations and practice at a fraction of the cost of a library, translated and updated without a print cycle.
  • Recorded teaching makes repetition cheap, and a global course reaches people excluded by geography or fees. His strongest claim: good instruction can become a software service with near-zero cost per extra learner.
  • The real product is feedback: testing understanding, finding the missing prerequisite, adjusting the next exercise, and connecting the learner to a person at the hard moment (see the figure).
  • The teacher’s role could shift from repeating a lecture to diagnosing and mentoring. But access to content is not completion or mastery.

Chapter 9

Developing World: Bootstrapping the Developing World

Countries can leap over missing fixed infrastructure when services arrive through cheap wireless devices.

  • A country with few landlines can build cell towers instead of wiring every home. The same logic applies to bank branches, information desks, and parts of schooling and healthcare.
  • Mobile money is the clearest mechanism: an account on a basic handset lets people save, send and pay without a nearby bank, and the history can support credit.
  • Current prices give a farmer or trader a choice of where to sell; digital identity makes people visible to institutions that could not verify them before.
  • The phone complements electricity, spectrum, roads, literacy and legal trust; it doesn’t replace them. A monopoly network or exclusionary ID system can rebuild the old bottleneck in software.

Chapter 10

New World: Human Energy Unleashed

When information, coordination and payment become immediate, human effort moves from friction to problem-solving.

  • Technology as acid: it burns away paper handoffs, fixed schedules, inventory, cash reconciliation and information monopolies, which become visible costs once a phone can do the same job instantly.
  • Incumbents treat the phone as a new channel and put an app beside the old organisation. Native competitors redesign the work around identity, live data and software distribution (see the figure).
  • Automation destroys some jobs while making new services cheap to start; fewer people are needed for routine coordination, freeing effort for work that wasn’t economic before.
  • The optimistic endpoint is billions of people learning, trading and organising at very low cost. Read now: the released value is not automatically shared.

The other half

What the forecast could not see

The book predicted the capability accurately and the arrangement around it barely at all. These sections are not in the book; they are what a reader now has to supply, because each one decided who the wave was good for.

Blind spot 1

Who Pays, and So Who Is the Customer

The book describes services becoming free without asking what makes them free.

  • Software copies cost almost nothing, so prices fall towards zero. True, and only half a sentence: a zero price is a business model in which the user is not the payer, and by 2012 it was already the industry’s biggest.
  • When the payer is an advertiser, what gets optimised is not your task but your predictability: how reliably attention can be held and intent inferred.
  • This was visible at the time: the dominant search company and the dominant social network were public, advertising-funded businesses, the second listing the year the book came out.
  • What followed proves the point: when the largest handset maker made apps ask permission before tracking users across other companies’ services, one advertising business estimated the cost in billions within a year. No hardware changed.

Blind spot 2

The Toll Booth in the Middle

Dematerialising a product moves it onto somebody’s platform, and the platform sets the terms.

  • Distribution didn’t disappear, it consolidated into two app stores that nearly all mobile software must pass through, each able to set a commission, impose rules, or remove a business.
  • For about a decade the standard commission was around thirty per cent, on goods the book rightly says cost almost nothing to copy. It has been trimmed for small developers and partly pried open by European regulation and litigation, but the shape holds.
  • Every dematerialisation chapter assumes the value released goes to users and makers. Much of it went to the layer underneath (see the figure). The book’s own logic predicts this, since whoever controls the scarce layer takes the surplus, but it never runs the argument.

Blind spot 3

The Attention Bill

What makes the device useful all day makes it profitable to keep you on it all day.

  • Every property the early chapters praise (always carried, always connected, personal, full of sensors) is equally a way to interrupt, measure response and iterate on whatever holds attention.
  • Heavy, fragmented phone use is not in dispute, nor that feeds are tuned for engagement. Whether this caused the worsening in adolescent mental health seen in several countries from the early 2010s is actively argued, with serious researchers on both sides and mostly correlational evidence. Hold the mechanism as clear and the size as unsettled.
  • The chapter on human energy expected everyone to gain a voice, and was right. It didn’t expect that the same distribution, ranked by response, would carry fabrications faster than corrections.

Blind spot 4

What Refused to Dematerialise

Software ate the artefact and left the supply chain, the building and the waste where they were.

  • The cloud is a building. Photos and maps live in data centres drawing continuous power, on the order of one per cent or more of world electricity and rising fast with machine learning, which the book didn’t foresee at all.
  • The phone is cobalt, lithium, copper and rare earths, replaced every few years. Electronic waste passed sixty million tonnes a year in the early 2020s and grows faster than it is recycled.
  • Retail is the counter-example: the shop became software, and the result was one of the largest physical build-outs in commercial history, in warehouses, delivery fleets and new kinds of work (see the figure).

Coda

The Author’s Own Sequel

He pushed the thesis one step further than the book, and staked a public company on it.

  • The book argues that anything whose value is informational will move into software, one way. From 2020 the software company Saylor led began converting its treasury into bitcoin, until the holding, not the software, was why people watched the share price.
  • It confirms the thesis is held sincerely. It also shows it working without the discipline that made the 2012 chapters good: those predicted what a technology would make possible; this predicts what people will choose to hold value in, which is about institutions, trust and politics, where the method is weakest.
  • Saylor recommends The Lessons of History, which is summarised on this site.

A retrospective reading

Scoring the forecast

Read now, the book is most striking where its forecast has become invisible infrastructure. Its blind spots are just as useful.

Words that do a lot of work

Glossary

Information energy
Saylor’s term for useful information arriving where and when it can change an action.
Dematerialisation
A physical product becoming a software service.
Leapfrogging
Skipping a stage of infrastructure, such as landlines or bank branches.
Mobile money
Accounts and payments run on a basic phone, without a bank branch.
Universal screen
One personal display that absorbs television, cinema, music and games.
Telemedicine
Care and specialist review delivered at a distance.
App store commission
The platform’s share of sales made through it; about thirty per cent for a decade.

Using it

Reading it

  1. Read it as a 2012 document

    Its value now is seeing which parts became ordinary and which assumptions failed.

  2. Chapters 3–5 are the core

    Paper, entertainment and the wallet show the dematerialisation argument most clearly.

  3. For each chapter, ask who pays

    The question the book never asks is the one that explains what got built.
