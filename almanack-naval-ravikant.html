The Almanack of Naval Ravikant

A Guide to Wealth and Happiness

“Seek wealth, not money or status. Wealth is having assets that earn while you sleep.”

Naval, in the 2018 tweetstorm the wealth half grew from

Assembled by Eric Jorgenson · 2020 · foreword by Tim Ferriss

The whole book in one breath

Two books share this cover and do not entirely agree. The first is a compressed account of how wealth gets made now: find the knowledge nobody can train you for, put your own name on the outcome, and multiply it with leverage that needs nobody’s permission (code and media), for long enough that compounding does the work. The second says the thing you just learned to want will not give you what you wanted from it: happiness is what is left when nothing feels missing, and desire is a contract to be unhappy until you get something. Naval knows the halves pull against each other and puts the money first anyway. None of it was written as a book. Eric Jorgenson cut a decade of tweets, podcasts and talks apart and rearranged them, with Naval’s blessing, and the result is free.

How it was made

The book Naval did not write

The most important fact about the Almanack is in a disclaimer most readers skip. Naval Ravikant did not write it. Eric Jorgenson, a product strategist with no prior connection to him, built it from transcripts, tweets and talks that were already public, then asked permission. Every idea in it had already been given away, and the ebook is free as well.

That changes how to read it. Jorgenson says plainly that the material was transcribed, cut, rearranged and re-edited for readability, and that by definition it is all out of context; he asks you to read generously. You are reading Naval compressed by an admirer, with the hedges and the dull parts removed. The form is an almanack in the line of Poor Richard’s and Poor Charlie’s: made for dipping into, not for reading straight through. Naval’s own advice in the front matter is to jump to whatever interests you and skip the rest.

The life underneath

Why the advice sounds the way it does

The book carries almost no biography, which is a pity, because the life explains the doctrine.

  • Born in Delhi in 1974, he moved to Queens as a boy into a poor, single-parent home, where the public library was his after-school care. Getting into Stuyvesant High School on an entrance exam was, he says, what saved him. Then Dartmouth.
  • He co-founded Epinions in 1999. It did well and he did not: he later sued his backers over how the company was sold, and for a while was badly thought of in Silicon Valley. The advice about reputation, accountability and long-term games comes from someone who spent years with a damaged name in a small industry.
  • He started AngelList in 2010 and was an early investor in companies including Uber and Twitter.
  • The public philosophising came late, in the mid-2010s, and grew into the 2018 “How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)” tweetstorm, which is the spine of Part I.

If you remember nothing else

Twelve things to keep

Definitions

Wealth, money and status are three things

Wealth earns while you sleep, money is the receipt, and status is a ranking someone must lose for you to gain.

Specific knowledge

What you can’t be trained for

If society can train you, it can replace you. It usually feels like play to you and looks like work to others.

Authenticity

Escape competition

If you are competing, you are probably copying. Nobody can out-compete you at being you.

Accountability

Put your name on it

Leverage is handed to people with credibility, and credibility comes from owning the outcome in public.

Equity

Own a piece

Without equity your income is tied to your hours. Salary is the minimum that gets you to do the job.

Leverage

Two levers need no permission

Labour and capital must be given to you. Code and media copy at no cost and need nobody’s say-so.

Judgment

Leverage multiplies judgment

Once leveraged, being a little more right is worth a great deal more than working a little harder.

Compounding

Long-term games, long-term people

Returns in wealth, relationships and knowledge come from compound interest, which only pays those who stay.

Luck

Three of four kinds can be built

Hustle, a prepared eye and a distinctive character each make luck more likely. Only blind luck is out of reach.

Happiness

A skill, and mostly a subtraction

It is what is there when the sense that something is missing goes away.

Desire

A contract to be unhappy

Hold one big desire at a time, and know it is where you have chosen to suffer.

Acceptance

Change it, accept it, or leave it

Most misery lives in the fourth option: wishing it were different and doing none of the three.

Part I

Wealth

About ninety-five pages in two sections. The headings are imperatives, and there is no argument building from one to the next: each is a cluster of excerpts.

Part I · Section 1

Building Wealth

Specific knowledge, accountability and leverage, applied for long enough that compounding does the rest.

  • Understand how wealth is created. Wealth is assets that earn while you sleep: a factory, a program serving customers overnight. Money is how we move time and wealth around. Status is a place in a hierarchy, and it is zero-sum. Attacks on wealth creation are often status moves dressed as ethics (see the first figure).
  • Society will pay you for giving it what it wants but doesn’t yet know how to get, at scale. Nobody gets rich renting out their time.
  • Find and build specific knowledge. Knowledge you can’t be trained for: not taught in schools, passed on by apprenticeship if at all, often highly technical or creative, and impossible to outsource or automate. The way to find it is to look at what you did effortlessly as a teenager that others noticed (see the second figure). His mother named his when he was fifteen: he would go into business.
  • Play long-term games with long-term people. The returns in life come from compounding, in money, relationships and knowledge alike. Trust makes deals cheap, so pick people you can work with for decades.
  • Take on accountability. Take business risks under your own name. Failure in public costs less than it feels (no debtors’ prison), and because most people fear it, those who accept it gain credibility and are given leverage.
  • Build or buy equity in a business. Without ownership, your inputs are tied to your outputs. Even doctors who get rich do it by owning a practice or a brand.
  • Find a position of leverage. Three kinds: labour, capital, and products that cost nothing to copy (code and media). The first two need someone’s permission; the third does not, and it is where the new fortunes are (see the third figure). One industry, worked through rung by rung, shows the three ingredients rising together (fourth figure).
  • Get paid for your judgment. Where inputs and outputs are tightly linked, wealth is almost impossible. Where they come apart, judgment is what gets paid.
  • Prioritise and focus. Set an aspirational hourly rate and stick to it. His was $5,000 an hour long before he earned anything like it: outsource whatever costs less than your rate, and ignore whatever costs more.
  • Find work that feels like play. Nobody can compete with you on something you would do for free for sixteen hours a day.
  • How to get lucky. Four kinds of luck, three of which you can build (fifth figure).
  • Be patient. The time scale is a decade or more. Don’t keep count, or you will run out of patience before the compounding arrives.

Part I · Section 2

Building Judgment

Leverage multiplies judgment, so once you have leverage, being a little more right is worth an absurd amount.

  • Judgment. Wisdom is knowing the long-term consequences of your actions; judgment is wisdom applied to outside problems. In a leveraged world, direction matters far more than speed, and hard work is overrated (though never optional).
  • How to think clearly. “Clear thinker” is a better compliment than “smart.” If you can’t rebuild an idea from basics when you need it, you have memorised it, not understood it. Big words are usually signalling.
  • What clouds thinking is desire: we see the world as we want it. You can solve a friend’s break-up in a minute and not your own, because you want nothing in theirs.
  • Shed your identity to see reality. He stopped identifying with labels after finding himself defending positions that came in a package. If all your beliefs line up in neat bundles, be suspicious.
  • Learn the skills of decision-making. If you can’t decide, the answer is no. Give the three big decisions (where you live, who you are with, what you do) far more time than most people do.
  • Collect mental models. His working set includes evolution, inversion, complexity theory, microeconomics, the principal–agent problem, compound interest, basic probability, black swans and falsifiability. He warns that without the experience underneath, models read like a book of quotes, which is how this book gets misused.
  • Learn to love to read. An hour or two a day puts you ahead of almost everyone. Read what you love until you love to read; keep many books going, skip and abandon freely; read foundations and originals before commentary. Finishing books is a vanity metric.

Part II

Happiness

About seventy-five pages in three sections. The claim underneath all of it is that happiness can be trained like fitness, within a range set by temperament.

Part II · Section 3

Learning Happiness

Happiness is what is left when the sense that something is missing goes away.

  • Happiness is learned. He describes going, over about a decade, from unhappy and uninterested in the question to mostly at peace, with money a small part of it.
  • Happiness is a choice. Decide that you want to be happy and treat it as the priority it is.
  • Happiness requires presence. The mind that runs into the past and the future is where the unhappiness comes from.
  • Happiness requires peace. What he means by happiness is peace, not joy: peace is happiness at rest. Every positive thought carries its opposite as contrast, so the goal is quiet, not a surplus of good thoughts.
  • Every desire is a chosen unhappiness. “Desire is a contract that you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want.” Not an instruction to want nothing: hold one big desire at a time and know its price (see the figure).
  • Success does not earn happiness. People who get what they chased adapt and want more.
  • Envy is the enemy of happiness. You can’t swap one feature with someone. You would have to become them entirely, reactions, family, self-image and all, and almost nobody would take that trade.
  • Happiness is built by habits. You are your habits and the five people you spend most time with. His list is plain: no alcohol, sugar, caffeine, news or social feeds; a daily workout, sunlight, meditation, time with happier people.
  • Find happiness in acceptance. In any situation you can change it, accept it, or leave it (second figure). And keep death in view: nothing lasts, so unhappiness buys nothing.

Part II · Section 4

Saving Yourself

Body first, then habits, then mind, and an explicit order so the daily negotiation never happens.

  • Choosing to be yourself. Nobody is better at being you; imitation is a losing race.
  • Choosing to care for yourself. His first priority, above happiness, family and work, is his own health. Because it is first, “I don’t have time to exercise” is never available (see the figure). On diet he is cautious: the more processed the food, the less of it, and beware fat and sugar together.
  • Meditation and mental strength. Meditation won’t give you control of your mind. It shows you how out of control it already is, which is where the relief starts. His experiment: an hour, first thing, for sixty days, until the backlog of unresolved thoughts runs dry. Cold showers taught him that most suffering is the avoidance; once you are in, it is just cold.
  • Choosing to build yourself. You become your habits. Saying you will “try” is a way of not doing it; if you can’t start, admit you don’t want it yet, and set a smaller goal you commit to in front of others.
  • Choosing to grow yourself. Keep learning, read the foundations, and teach, because teaching forces learning.
  • Choosing to free yourself. Freedom from other people’s expectations, and from your own: their expectations of you are their problem.

Part II · Section 5

Philosophy

Rational Buddhism: keep the inner technology you can test, and drop what you can’t.

  • The meaning of life. Three answers, each undercutting the last. It is personal and you must find it. There is none: you were dead for billions of years and will be again. Or, stranger: living things reverse entropy locally and speed it up overall, hurrying towards a state where all differences vanish.
  • Live by your values. His are phrased as refusals, which makes them testable: total honesty; no short-term dealing; only peer relationships; no anger, and no angry people. Good partners, colleagues and spouses are mostly people whose values match yours.
  • Rational Buddhism. Keep meditation, the analysis of desire and the awareness beneath the chattering mind, which he says he has checked for himself. Drop past lives, chakras and anything else unfalsifiable. Evolution is the explanation he leans on for why people are the way they are.
  • The present is all we have. Nobody has gone back in time or usefully predicted the future. Here the writing stops being advice and is at its best.

Bonus

What Naval Reads

About thirty-five pages of what to read next, in a book whose most repeated instruction is to read.

  • Science, history, economics: David Deutsch, The Beginning of Infinity; Yuval Noah Harari, Sapiens; Matt Ridley, The Rational Optimist; Will and Ariel Durant, The Lessons of History; Poor Charlie’s Almanack.
  • Risk and uncertainty: Nassim Taleb, especially Fooled by Randomness and Skin in the Game.
  • Philosophy and the inner life: Jiddu Krishnamurti; Marcus Aurelius, Meditations; and a run of Buddhist and Taoist writers.
  • Four of these are summarised on this site, which is the argument for reading the originals rather than the almanack of them.

Context

How it gets misread

The popular versionWhat the text says
Follow your passion and the money followsWork as hard as you can; what you work on and with whom matter more. He describes sixteen-hour days of it.
Don’t want thingsDo things and follow what you are meant to do. Hold one big desire and know its price, not zero.
Hard work no longer mattersWithout hard work you build neither judgment nor leverage. Direction beats speed; it does not replace it.
Find the right lever and get rich fastThere are no get-rich-quick schemes; those are someone else getting rich off you. The unit of time is a decade.

Where readers push back

Stress-testing it

Tim Ferriss’s foreword says it first: follow the advice only if it survives testing in your own life. These are the strongest objections; the book half-admits several of them.

A sample of one, who survived

“Wealthy in almost every parallel universe” is a claim about a distribution argued from one life. The lost fortunes and the bad years appear as colour, not as data, and the method is never shown working for people who are not Naval.

Out of context by construction

Naval warns that aphorisms without the experience behind them are just quotes, and the book’s format is a machine for producing exactly that. The reader most likely to quote “desire is a contract” is the one least likely to have paid for it.

The two halves need each other and undercut each other

Part I runs on ambition and one big goal; Part II calls desire chosen unhappiness. His bridge is sequence (get rich first), but sequencing schedules the contradiction rather than resolving it.

Advice from the ceiling

The aspirational hourly rate is excellent for someone with slack and harmful for someone without. The book says almost nothing to people whose hours and pay are still locked together, beyond “learn to sell, learn to build.”

A life optimised against obligation

Labour leverage is messy, networking is a waste, angry people are cut out, others’ expectations are their problem. Coherent, but what it optimises away is being needed, which is where much of most people’s meaning lives.

Using it

Reading it

  1. Read it the way it was built

    In short sittings, out of order, with a real problem in hand. Front to back in two evenings feels like reading a dictionary.

  2. Keep only what bit

    Take the three or four claims that landed and go to the originals for each.

  3. Hear it unedited

    The Farnam Street interview and the “How to Get Rich” series are fuller than any excerpt, and free.

  4. Then his shelf

    Deutsch, the Durants, Taleb and Marcus Aurelius, all on this site.

Read alongside

  • The Beginning of Infinity, summarised on this site: the source of his view of knowledge.
  • Meditations, summarised on this site: the notebook he calls life-changing.
  • Fooled by Randomness, summarised on this site: the corrective to “wealthy in 999 universes.”
  • Poor Charlie’s Almanack: the model for the form.
