Assembled, not written

Free, on purpose

The Almanack of Naval Ravikant A decade of tweets, podcasts and live AMAs, cut apart and rebuilt into a guide to wealth and happiness — by a stranger, with the subject's blessing and none of his royalties.

Two books share this cover, and they do not entirely get along. The first is the most compressed account of modern wealth creation anyone has written: specific knowledge, accountability and leverage, applied over a long enough horizon that compounding does the work. The second says the thing you just spent ninety pages learning to want will not deliver what you wanted from it — that happiness is the absence of desire, and desire is the engine the first half just handed you. Naval knows this. He puts the money first anyway, and says so: "Let's get them all rich. Let's get them all fit and healthy. Then, let's get them all happy."

The object

The Book Naval Did Not Write

A compilation assembled by a reader, from material already free.

The most important fact about the Almanack is structural, and it is stated in a disclaimer most readers skip. Naval Ravikant did not write this book. Eric Jorgenson — a product strategist in Kansas City with no prior relationship to him — built it out of transcripts, tweets and talks that were already public, then asked permission afterwards. Every substantive idea in it had already been given away for free, and the book itself is free too.

That matters for how you read it. A memoir is shaped by the author's sense of his own arc; this is shaped by an editor's sense of what was worth keeping. Jorgenson is explicit that the transcripts were "transcribed, edited, rearranged, and re-edited for readability," that not all sources are primary, that he cannot certify every one, and that by definition everything in this book is taken out of context. He asks you to interpret generously. This is an unusually honest disclosure and it is also a real limitation: what you are reading is Naval compressed by someone who admires him, with the hedges, the retractions and the boring parts removed.

The result reads less like a book than like a well-organised commonplace book — the almanack form the title claims, following Poor Richard's and Poor Charlie's. Jorgenson says so directly: the models were Peter Kaufman's Munger compilation, Blake Masters' notes on Peter Thiel, Peter Bevelin, Max Olson's Buffett letters. It is designed for consultation, not for reading through. Naval's own instruction, printed in the front matter, is "jump to anything that interests you and skip anything that doesn't."

How it is laid out

The book divides in two, and the division is not equal. Part I, Wealth, runs to roughly ninety-five pages across two sections — Building Wealth and Building Judgment. Part II, Happiness, runs to roughly seventy-seven across three — Learning Happiness, Saving Yourself, and Philosophy. A long bonus section catalogues what Naval reads. There is no argument developing across chapters; each is a cluster of excerpts around a heading, and the headings are imperatives.

The life underneath

Almost no biography appears in the book itself, which is a loss, because the shape of the life makes the advice legible. Naval was born in Delhi in 1974 and moved to Queens at nine, into a single-parent household that he describes as poor: his father, a pharmacist in India, could not have the degree recognised and worked in a hardware store; the family split. The library was after-school care. He got into Stuyvesant on an exam — "one of those intelligence lottery situations where you can break in with instant validation" — and that admission, he says flatly, saved his life.

What follows is not a smooth ascent. He was, in his telling, embarrassed into starting his first company in 1996 after telling everyone he was about to. Epinions did well; he did not, and sued his backers — a New York Times story in 2005 — and was known in the Valley for a period as "radioactive mud." The first fortune he lost in the stock market; the second he was, he says, cheated out of. AngelList came in 2010, the year he also put money into Uber. The philosophical public persona is late: he dates it to 2014–15, and says people in the industry messaged him through back channels to tell him he was ending his career.

Part I

Wealth: Assets That Earn While You Sleep

A vocabulary problem first, then an engineering one.

The engine of the whole first half is a distinction most people never make. Wealth is assets that earn while you sleep. Money is how we transfer time and wealth. Status is your place in the social hierarchy. Naval's claim is that most people say "money" when they mean one of the other two, and that conflating them is why they never get any of them.

Wealth is a factory, a robot, a computer program running at night serving customers you never meet, a business earning while you are on holiday. Money is the IOU society hands you for value already delivered — "social credits," the ability to have credits and debits on other people's time. Status is a ranking, and a ranking is zero-sum: for number three to become number two, number two has to move. Naval's sharpest sociological observation follows from this. When someone attacks wealth creation, they are usually not making an economic argument; they are making a status move, and status moves are cheaper than building anything.

"Wealth creation is an evolutionarily recent positive-sum game. Status is an old zero-sum game. Those attacking wealth creation are often just seeking status." Tweet, quoted p. 73
Productize yourself

Naval compresses the entire method into two words, and the compression is the point: it is designed to be recallable under pressure. "Productize" carries leverage and specific knowledge. "Yourself" carries uniqueness, accountability, and specific knowledge again. Everything else in Part I is an expansion of one of those four terms.

Specific knowledge

This is the book's best original coinage, and the definition is negative: specific knowledge is knowledge you cannot be trained for. If society can train you, it can train someone else and replace you. It is not credentialled, not taught in schools, and when it is transmitted at all it goes by apprenticeship. It is often highly technical or highly creative. It cannot be outsourced and cannot be automated — and the corollary Naval states bluntly is that if they can train you to do it, eventually they will train a computer to do it.

The tests he offers for finding your own are unusually concrete. What were you doing as a kid or a teenager almost effortlessly — something you did not consider a skill but people around you noticed? His examples are deliberately unimpressive: a knack for sales; picking up any instrument; an obsessive personality that dives in and remembers; reading science fiction and absorbing information fast; playing so many games that game theory is intuitive; gossiping and digging into a friend network, which he says makes a good journalist. The first person to name his own was his mother, from the kitchen, when he was fifteen and announcing he would be an astrophysicist: "No, you're going to go into business."

Accountability, equity, and the risk of ruin

Leverage is mostly given rather than taken — capital has to be handed to you, labour has to choose to follow you — and what makes people hand it over is credibility carried under your own name. Hence the instruction to take business risks under your own name and to accept the humiliation case. "In the old days, the captain was expected to go down with the ship." The modern downside, he argues, is smaller than it feels: no debtors' prison, bankruptcy discharges, and ecosystems that forgive honest failure. The social wiring against public failure is stronger than the actual penalty, which is precisely why the people willing to fail publicly gain power.

From this follows the equity argument, stated with unusual flatness: if you do not own a piece of a business, you have no path to financial freedom. Without ownership your inputs are tied to your outputs — you sleep, you stop earning. Even doctors who get properly rich do it by opening a practice, building a brand, or owning a procedure. Salary is defined as the minimum that gets you to do the job, and it can be a high minimum and still not be wealth. Equity is the upside; debt is the downside; own the upside.

The one non-negotiable Avoid the risk of ruin. Stay out of jail — "it's never worth it to wear an orange jumpsuit." Stay out of total catastrophic financial loss; don't gamble everything on one go. Watch your health. Everything else in Part I is an argument for taking more risk; this is the boundary condition that makes the rest survivable, and it is easy to skim past.
The multiplier

Leverage, and the Two Kinds of Permission

"Forget rich versus poor, white-collar versus blue. It's now leveraged versus un-leveraged."

Fortunes require leverage, and there are three sources of it: labour, capital, and products with no marginal cost of replication — code and media. The distinction that carries the argument is not between them by size but by permission. Labour and capital are permissioned: somebody has to follow you, somebody has to fund you. Code and media are permissionless. You need a computer and nobody's consent.

Naval's verdict on labour is harsher than most business writing allows: the oldest form, and the worst. "Managing other people is incredibly messy. It requires tremendous leadership skills. You're one short hop from a mutiny." Labour leverage will impress your parents; don't waste your life chasing it. Capital he rates higher and calls trickier — the dominant form of the last century, which is why the richest people outside technology are bankers and financiers, and why the CEO job at large old companies is really a financial job. It scales beautifully: managing more capital is far easier than managing more people.

The third form is the one he thinks is new. It began with the printing press, accelerated through broadcast, and detonated with the internet. Every great software developer now has an army of robots working overnight; the army is "freely available — it's just packed in data centres for heat and space efficiency." The instruction for people who cannot code is the same instruction in a different medium: write books and blogs, record videos and podcasts. The new fortunes — Bezos, Zuckerberg, Page, Brin, Gates, Jobs, and, at a different scale, Joe Rogan and PewDiePie — are all code-and-media fortunes.

The ladder, worked through in one industry

The most useful passage in the book is a five-rung worked example in real estate, because it makes the three ingredients — accountability, leverage, specific knowledge — move together rather than sit as slogans. Each rung takes on more of all three, and the compensation is not linear.

Inputs, outputs, and where they come apart

The underlying variable in the ladder is the coupling between hours in and value out. Where they are tightly coupled — a repair labourer, customer service — no amount of effort produces wealth; you are selling time at a rate that is set elsewhere. Where they come apart — a leveraged worker can out-produce a non-leveraged one by a factor of a thousand or ten thousand — judgment matters far more than hours, and the whole apparatus of visible busyness stops paying.

"Forty hour work weeks are a relic of the Industrial Age. Knowledge workers function like athletes — train and sprint, then rest and reassess." Tweet, quoted p. 62
Part I, cont.

Judgment: The Thing Leverage Multiplies

"There's no shortcut to smart."

Leverage is a force multiplier, and what it multiplies is judgment — which means that once you are leveraged, being slightly more right becomes worth an absurd amount. Naval's definition is tight: wisdom is knowing the long-term consequences of your actions; judgment is wisdom applied to external problems. Hard work is overrated in the modern economy. Direction dominates speed, and it dominates more the more leverage you carry.

Clear thinking, and the ego tax

"Clear thinker" is a better compliment than "smart." The test Naval offers is Feynman's: real knowledge is intrinsic and built from the ground up, and if you cannot re-derive a concept from basics when you need it, you are memorising, not knowing. Big words and advanced concepts are usually signalling; the advanced parts of any field are also its least proven parts. Better to be 9/10 on foundations than to be deep in something you cannot rebuild.

The obstacle to clear thinking, in his account, is not intelligence but desire. "The number one thing clouding us from being able to see reality is we have preconceived notions of the way it should be." He defines a moment of suffering as the moment you see things exactly as they are — and then makes the useful inversion: that is also the only moment from which progress is possible, because progress starts with the truth. The everyday version is the friend paradox. You can solve your friend's breakup in a minute and cannot solve your own, because you have no desire in theirs.

His own countermeasure When something in the business is going badly, acknowledge it publicly, and specifically in front of co-founders and colleagues. "If I'm not hiding it from anybody, I'm not going to delude myself." This is the one place where the book converts a psychological insight into an enforceable rule, and it is worth more than several pages of the surrounding material.

The identity material follows the same logic. Egos get built in the first two decades by environment and parents; we spend the rest of life trying to make them happy, and interpreting new information through them. Naval says he stopped self-identifying at almost any level — he used to call himself a libertarian and found himself defending positions he had not thought through, because they came in the package. "If all your beliefs line up into neat little bundles, you should be highly suspicious." To be honest, speak without identity.

The deck of models

Naval treats aphorisms as pointers rather than content: compact addresses for knowledge you have already paid for in experience. "If you don't have the underlying experience, then it just reads like a collection of quotes." That caveat is the most important sentence in the judgment section, and it is a warning about how the Almanack itself will be misused.

Time, and the four kinds of luck

Two of the book's most quoted practical devices sit here. The first is the aspirational hourly rate: pick a number that feels absurdly high, then enforce it. Naval used $5,000/hour long before he had money, and reckons in hindsight he was worth about $1,000. The rule is symmetric — if fixing something costs less than your rate, outsource it; if it costs more, ignore it. He gives the unglamorous version too, arguing with electricians and returning broken speakers, and admits he still failed at it regularly.

The second is the taxonomy of luck, worked out with his co-founder Nivi, and it is the mechanism behind the title of his most famous piece — How to Get Rich (Without Getting Lucky). The point is not that luck is irrelevant. It is that three of the four kinds are things you can build.

Reading, which he treats as the master skill

Naval's reading advice is the most personally revealing material in the book, partly because it is the least strategic. Books were his only friends as an immigrant kid in Queens; he read comics and what he calls mental junk food, and credits the fact that nobody told him what to read. The strategic claim is that reading one to two hours a day puts you in the top fraction of a percent, and accounts for whatever success and intelligence he has. The tactical claim is that the way most adults read is broken.

The breakage is a conflict between two trainings: a shredded attention span from feeds, and a school-instilled obligation to finish every book. Together they produce the person stuck on page 332 who has stopped reading entirely. His hack is to treat books as blog posts — buy every one mentioned, keep ten to twenty in flight, start in the middle, abandon without guilt. "The number of books completed is a vanity metric."

Part II

Happiness: A Skill, and Mostly a Subtraction

"The three big ones in life are wealth, health, and happiness. We pursue them in that order, but their importance is reverse."

Naval's most credible claim about happiness is a before-and-after. Ten years ago he would have rated himself 2 or 3 out of 10, and would have dismissed the question as uninteresting. He now says 9, and says money is a very small piece of it. The whole of Part II rests on treating happiness as a trainable skill rather than a temperament — like nutrition, like fitness — with a genetic range you cannot escape and a large conditioned component you can.

His working definition is subtractive and worth stating precisely: happiness is what is there when you remove the sense that something is missing. Not positive thinking — he argues every positive thought contains its negative as contrast, which is duality, which is the Tao Te Ching's point long before it was his. Happiness is the absence of desire, especially desire for external things, and it shows up as internal silence. He is careful to distinguish it from joy: "You'll notice when I say happiness, I mean peace." Peace is happiness at rest; happiness is peace in motion.

Desire as a contract

The line most people take from the book is this: desire is a contract you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want. It is not an instruction to want nothing — Naval is explicit that you are a living creature, you locally reverse entropy, you should self-actualise and do things. It is an instruction to count. Hold one big desire at a time, know that it is the axis of your suffering, and recognise that you have chosen where to be unhappy.

Envy, and the single-player game

The envy passage is the most personally useful thing in Part II. Jealousy was hard for him, and what dissolved it was noticing that the swap is not itemisable. You cannot take his body, her money, his personality. You would have to be that person entirely — their reactions, their family, their self-image, their happiness level, 24/7. Once the trade is stated as a wholesale swap, almost nobody accepts it, and the envy has nowhere to stand.

Underneath sits the game distinction. Looking good, making money, buying a house, politics, academia, status — all multiplayer, all externally scored. Health, meditation, yoga, reading, happiness — single-player, no external validation, no scoreboard. He argues we have become so socially driven that we no longer know how to win the single-player games, and that this is exactly why yoga and meditation are so hard to sustain: they have no extrinsic value at all.

The habits, itemised

Where the philosophy is abstract, the practices are not, and Naval lists them plainly enough to be audited. The unifying logic is that you are a combination of your habits and the people you spend the most time with — the "five chimps theory," that you can predict a chimp's behaviour from the five it hangs around. He treats friend selection as a happiness intervention, which is uncomfortable and probably correct.

Acceptance, and the death hack

The operational core of the happiness section is a three-way test. In any situation you can change it, accept it, or leave it. What is not on the list — and what Naval says is responsible for most of our misery — is the fourth thing everyone actually does: sit around wishing it were different while doing none of the three. His most-used private word, he says, is a single one: accept.

Underneath acceptance he puts something bleaker, and states it without softening: embrace death. There is no legacy. Our works will be dust, our civilisations dust, our planet dust. He uses the Sumerians as the test — name one — and follows it with the thought that in a hundred thousand years, "Americans" will land the same way. The move is meant to be liberating rather than nihilistic: if the whole thing is a firefly blink, unhappiness buys nobody anything, and the remaining question is only how you interpret the time.

"You're going to die one day, and none of this is going to matter. So enjoy yourself. Do something positive. Project some love. Make someone happy. Laugh a little bit. Appreciate the moment. And do your work." p. 155
Part II, cont.

Saving Yourself: Body, Habits, Mind

"Doctors won't make you healthy. Nutritionists won't make you slim. Gurus won't make you calm."

The section opens with a priority ordering that most people would not say out loud: Naval's number one priority in life, above his happiness, above his family, above his work, is his own health. Physical first, then mental, then spiritual, then his family's health, then his family's wellbeing, and only then the world. The ordering exists to kill a specific excuse. Because health is genuinely first, he can never say he does not have time to exercise.

Diet, briefly and cautiously

He hedges heavily here and is right to: "diet and nutrition are like politics — everybody thinks they're an expert," and identity gets wrapped up in what people already eat. The one mechanism he commits to is the interaction rather than either macronutrient alone. Dietary fat drives satiety; dietary sugar drives hunger; the sugar effect dominates. Which is why every dessert is fat and carbohydrate together, why the combination barely occurs in nature outside tropical fruit, and why any sensible diet avoids it. The rest is heuristics: the more processed the food, the less of it; quality control leads to quantity control; subtract before you add; ditch the extremists and anything invented in the last few hundred years.

Meditation, and the sixty-day experiment

Naval is unusually specific about meditation, and unusually deflationary about what it does. It will not give you control of your mind. Its advantage is recognising how out of control the mind already is — "a monkey flinging feces, running around the room" — because you have to see the creature in operation before you feel any distaste for it, and in that separation is the liberation. He is also honest that it helps less than you would hope: "You can be a long-time meditator, but if someone says the wrong thing in the wrong way, you go back to your ego-driven self."

The adjacent lesson comes from cold showers, learned via Wim Hof and undertaken by a man who says he is constitutionally opposed to ice baths. He worked from wincing his way in to walking straight under full blast, and extracted a general principle: most of our suffering comes from avoidance. The suffering in a cold shower is almost entirely the tiptoeing. Once you are in, it is not suffering — it is just cold. Your body saying it is cold is a different event from your mind saying it is cold.

Habits, and honesty about change

His account of habit change is refreshingly unsentimental. We are thousands of habits running subconsciously with a little neocortex left over for novelty; you become your habits. But he pushes back hard on the language of trying: "Whenever we say we're going to try to do something or try to form a habit, we're wimping out." When we really want something we simply do it — we approach the person, we take the drink. So the honest move when you cannot start is not more resolve but a smaller, externally committed goal, and an admission that you do not want the change yet.

Impatience with actions, patience with results Anything that has to be done, do now — inspiration is perishable. But markets take time to adopt products, people take time to trust each other, and products take time to polish. The failure mode most people run is exactly inverted: patient about starting, impatient about outcomes.
Part II, end

Philosophy: Rational Buddhism

Eleven pages, carrying everything the previous hundred and sixty set up.

Asked for the meaning of life, Naval gives three answers rather than one, and the sequence matters because each undercuts the last. First: it is personal, nobody can hand it to you, and the important part is not the answer but sitting with the question for years. Second: there is no meaning — you were dead for ten billion years and will be dead for another seventy, anything you do will fade, and if there were an intrinsic meaning you could always ask why that, and it would be turtles all the way down. Third, and stranger: perhaps there is one, and it is unsatisfying — living systems locally reverse entropy while globally accelerating it, so everything you build hastens the heat death in which all distinctions vanish and we become one thing.

"Rational Buddhism" is his name for the reconciliation. The Buddhist internal technology stays — meditation, the analysis of desire, the base layer of awareness beneath the monkey mind — because he says he has verified those for himself. What goes is anything unfalsifiable: past-life karma, chakras, levitation, superpowers. On the other pole sits evolution, which he treats as the binding explanatory principle for why humans are the way they are. "Try everything, test it for yourself, be skeptical, keep what's useful, and discard what's not."

Values, stated as refusals

His core values are all phrased as things he will not do, which is more useful than the usual positive formulation because refusals are testable. Total honesty, because dishonesty forks his mind into threads and pulls him out of the present. No short-term dealing — if you deal short-term with someone else, he will stop dealing with you. Only peer relationships, neither above nor below. No anger, and no angry people; he cuts them out without judging them, having been through it himself. And the point of the list: finding good partners, colleagues and spouses is mostly finding people whose values line up, because when they line up the small things stop mattering.

The section closes on the present moment, and here the writing is at its best because it stops being advice. There is nothing but this moment; nobody has gone back in time, nobody has usefully predicted the future. "You're dying and being reborn at every moment. It's up to you whether to forget or remember that." And the epigraph he chooses, from the Iliad: everything is more beautiful because we're doomed — you will never be lovelier than you are now, and we will never be here again.

"A calm mind, a fit body, and a house full of love. These things cannot be bought. They must be earned." p. 124 · the hinge between the two halves of the book
Push back

Where the Almanack Is Weakest

Naval's own instruction: consider everything, take nothing as gospel, bring your A-game.

Tim Ferriss's foreword does the reader a service by saying it first: follow his advice, but only if it survives stress-testing in your own life. What follows is that stress test. None of these are reasons not to read the book; several of them are things the book half-admits and then moves past.

01

The sample is one, and it survived

"In 1,000 parallel universes, you want to be wealthy in 999" is a claim about a distribution, argued from a single realisation of it. The book contains its own counter-evidence — the lost first fortune, the second he says he was cheated out of, the years as "radioactive mud" — and treats them as texture rather than as data. A method that produces wealth deterministically should be demonstrable in people who are not Naval, and the book never tries.

02

Everything is out of context by construction

Jorgenson says so plainly. Aphorisms are pointers to experience you already have; strip the experience and they become posters. Naval says this himself about mental models — and the format of the book is the exact machine for producing the failure he warns about. The reader most likely to quote "desire is a contract" is the reader least likely to have paid for it.

03

The two halves need each other and undermine each other

Part I runs on desire, ambition, one big goal, sixteen hours a day. Part II says desire is chosen unhappiness and the winners are those who step out of the game entirely. Naval's bridge is sequencing — get rich first, Buddha was a prince — but sequencing does not dissolve the contradiction, it schedules it. And the person who has spent thirty years becoming high-anxiety in order to succeed is, by his own account, unable to switch it off afterwards.

04

The hourly rate has a floor it does not acknowledge

"Set an absurdly high aspirational rate and outsource anything below it" is excellent advice for someone with slack and terrible advice for someone without. Naval insists he applied it before he had money; but he applied it as a Dartmouth graduate in Silicon Valley with a rising option on his time. The rule is a forecasting device disguised as a budgeting device, and the disguise makes it read as universally applicable when it is not.

05

"Life is a single-player game" is doing metaphysics in a game-theory costume

It is a genuinely useful corrective to status-chasing. It is also asserted, not argued, and it sits directly against the book's own claims that all returns come from compound relationships, that your five chimps determine your happiness, and that meeting his wife required him to have developed values worth having. The single-player frame is true of the scoreboard, not of the life.

06

Fifty pages of advice about people, almost none about being needed by them

Labour leverage is dismissed as messy. Networking is a waste of time. Angry people, unhappy people and people in conflict are cut out. Expectations others have of you are "completely their problem." Taken together this is a coherent optimisation, and what it optimises away is obligation — which is also where a great deal of most people's meaning is located. The 2008 formula leaves "good relationships" undefined for the same reason.

Practical

Reading It, and What to Read Next

The bonus section is not filler; it is the book's real bibliography.

Read it the way it was built: non-linearly, in short sittings, with a specific problem in hand. It is organised for consultation, and reading it front to back in two evenings produces the same fatigue as reading a dictionary — the density is uniform, which means nothing stands out. Jorgenson says he opens the Happiness chapter when he is feeling off and reviews sections before making an investment. That is the intended usage.

A better protocol, if you want the ideas to survive the week: take the three or four claims that actually bit, and go to the primary sources for each. The Farnam Street interview with Shane Parrish (source [4] here, and by a wide margin the most-quoted) is more nuanced than any excerpt from it. The 2019 How to Get Rich series is the wealth material in Naval's own structure. The Joe Rogan episode is where the happiness material is least edited. All of it is free, which is the whole point of the object.

If you keep only seven things
  1. Separate wealth from money from status, and check which game you are in.Wealth earns while you sleep; money is the receipt; status is a ranking someone else has to lose for you to gain. Most of the confusion and most of the resentment in this subject comes from using one word for three things.
  2. Specific knowledge is found, not built — and the test is that it feels like play.If society can train you for it, society can replace you. Look at what you were doing effortlessly at fourteen that nobody had to make you do, and ask what the scaled version of it would be.
  3. Leverage is a multiplier on judgment, and two of the three kinds need nobody's permission.Code and media replicate at zero marginal cost and require no gatekeeper. That is genuinely new, it is genuinely available, and it is the one structural claim in the book that has only got more true since 2020.
  4. Accountability under your own name is the price of the other two.Capital and labour have to be handed to you, and what makes people hand them over is a reputation you can only build by being publicly attached to outcomes — including the bad ones.
  5. Three of the four kinds of luck are manufacturable.Hustle produces surface area, skill produces sensitivity, and a distinctive character makes other people's luck come to you. Only the first kind is out of your hands, and it is the only kind worth ignoring.
  6. Desire is a contract to be unhappy until the condition is met — so sign few of them.Not zero: you are meant to do things. One at a time, consciously chosen, with the price named. And the test in the moment: is this so important that I will be unhappy unless it goes my way?
  7. Change it, accept it, or leave it — and notice the fourth thing you are actually doing.Wishing without acting is where most misery lives, and it is invisible precisely because it does not feel like a choice. Naval's most-used private word is one syllable long.
