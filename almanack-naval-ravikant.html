The Almanack of Naval Ravikant

A Guide to Wealth and Happiness

“Seek wealth, not money or status. Wealth is having assets that earn while you sleep.”

Naval, the first tweet of the “How to Get Rich” tweetstorm that opens Part I

Assembled by Eric Jorgenson · 2020 · foreword by Tim Ferriss · illustrations by Jack Butcher

The whole book in one breath

Two books share this cover and do not entirely agree. The first is a compressed account of how wealth gets made now: find the knowledge nobody can train you for, put your own name on the outcome, own equity, and multiply your judgment with leverage that needs nobody’s permission (code and media), for long enough that compounding does the work. The second says the thing you just learned to want will not give you what you wanted from it: happiness is what is there when the sense that something is missing goes away, desire is a contract to be unhappy until you get something, and peace is built from habits, meditation, acceptance and a daily workout. Naval knows the halves pull against each other and puts the money first anyway (“Let’s get you rich first”), because money buys the freedom to work on the rest. None of it was written as a book. Eric Jorgenson, a stranger who admired him, cut a decade of tweets, podcasts and talks apart and rearranged them, with Naval’s trust, and the result is free.

How it was made

The book Naval did not write

The most important fact about the Almanack is in a disclaimer most readers skip. Naval Ravikant did not write it. Eric Jorgenson, a product strategist and writer from Kansas City, built it “entirely out of transcripts, Tweets, and talks Naval has shared”. In his thanks he calls himself “a stranger from the internet” whom Naval trusted, and says the project began with a tweet and took about a thousand hours. The ebook is free at navalmanack.com, and the copyright page adds that Naval earns no money from it.

The disclaimer is candid about what that means. The transcripts have been edited for clarity and brevity, several times over; some excerpts come from other writers quoting Naval rather than from Naval; the sources cannot all be authenticated; and “by definition, everything in this book is taken out of context.” Readers are asked to check phrasing against a primary source before quoting him, and to interpret generously. The format rules follow: tweets are set as pull quotes, tweetstorms as linked chains, interviewers’ questions in bold without saying who asked, and a bracketed number marks the end of each excerpt and points to one of about eighty sources. Jorgenson calls it “a choose-your-own adventure book”: jump to whatever interests you and skip the rest.

You are reading Naval compressed and arranged by an admirer, with the hedges and the dull parts removed. Jorgenson names the books he was imitating: Poor Charlie’s Almanack (Charlie Munger’s talks, edited by Peter Kaufman), Blake Masters’s Zero to One (from Peter Thiel’s lectures), Peter Bevelin’s Seeking Wisdom, Max Olson’s edition of Buffett’s shareholder letters, and Ray Dalio’s Principles. The illustrations are by Jack Butcher of Visualize Value, who offered them.

What Tim Ferriss’s foreword says

Ferriss says he had sworn never to write forewords and made an exception for three reasons: the book is free, he had long wanted someone to compile it, and he hoped it would get Naval’s next child named Tim. He has known Naval for more than a decade and calls him one of the most courageous people he knows, not in the “run into the fire” sense but in the “tell everyone they’re focusing on the wrong fire” sense, and “as blunt as a foot to the face”. He lists the business record (co-founder of AngelList, earlier of Vast.com and Epinions, early investor in Twitter, Uber, Yammer and OpenDNS) and then says that is not why he listens. He takes Naval seriously because he questions nearly everything, thinks from first principles, tests things, is good at not fooling himself, changes his mind, laughs a lot and doesn’t take himself too seriously. His instruction to the reader: don’t parrot; follow the advice only if it holds up after stress-testing in your own life, and treat Naval as “a friendly but highly competent sparring partner”.

The life underneath

Why the advice sounds the way it does

The book gives a one-page timeline and a short background in Naval’s words, and the life explains a good deal of the doctrine.

  • He grew up in a single-parent household, a “latchkey kid” with his brother, poor immigrants. His father had been a pharmacist in India, but his degree was not accepted in the United States, so he worked in a hardware store; the family split up. His mother worked, studied and raised the boys with what he calls unconditional and unfailing love.
  • The neighbourhood in Queens was not safe, so the library was his after-school centre: he went straight there and stayed until it closed. With few friends, “my only real friends were books.” His first job, at fifteen, was delivering Indian food from the back of a van for an illegal caterer; before that he had a paper round and washed dishes.
  • Passing the entrance test for Stuyvesant High School “saved my life”: the school’s name got him into an Ivy League college and then into technology, “blue collar to white collar in one move.” At Dartmouth he studied economics and computer science and thought for a while he would do a PhD in economics.
  • He started his first company almost by accident. Working at @Home Network in 1996, he told everyone he was only there temporarily and was leaving to start a company, and was “literally embarrassed into starting my own company” when people kept asking why he was still there.
  • Epinions “did well, but I didn’t do well, so I sued some of the people involved.” It worked out for him in the end, but the timeline records that by 2005 he was being called “Radioactive Mud” in Silicon Valley. The advice about reputation, anger, accountability and long-term games comes from someone who spent years with a damaged name in a small industry, and who says the one thing he would change is the anger.
  • Until about 2013–14 his public persona was entirely startups and investing. He began talking philosophy around 2014–15, under his own name, and people in the industry messaged him that he was ending his career. At the time of the book he said he was invested in about two hundred companies, a small partner in a crypto fund, and founder and chairman of AngelList.

If you remember nothing else

Twelve things to keep

Definitions

Wealth, money and status are three things

Wealth earns while you sleep, money is how we transfer time and wealth, and status is a ranking someone must lose for you to gain.

Specific knowledge

What you can’t be trained for

If society can train you, it can train someone else and replace you. It feels like play to you and looks like work to others.

Authenticity

Escape competition

If you are competing, you are probably copying. Nobody can out-compete you at being you.

Accountability

Put your name on it

Leverage is handed to people with credibility, and credibility comes from owning the outcome in public.

Equity

Own a piece

Without ownership your inputs are tied to your outputs, and an employer pays the bare minimum it takes to get the job done.

Leverage

Two levers need no permission

Labour and capital must be given to you. Code and media copy at no cost and need nobody’s say-so.

Judgment

Leverage multiplies judgment

Right 85% of the time instead of 75%, steering a $100 billion ship, is worth almost any salary.

Compounding

Long-term games, long-term people

All the returns in life, in wealth, relationships or knowledge, come from compound interest, which pays only those who stay.

Luck

Three of four kinds can be built

Hustle, a prepared eye and a distinctive character make luck likely. Aim to be wealthy in 999 of 1,000 parallel universes.

Happiness

A skill, and mostly a subtraction

It is there when you remove the sense that something is missing. He went from 3 out of 10 to 9 by practising.

Desire

A contract to be unhappy

Hold one big desire at a time, and know it is where you have chosen to suffer.

Acceptance

Change it, accept it, or leave it

Most misery lives in the fourth option: wishing it were different and doing none of the three.

Part I

Wealth

About ninety-five pages in two sections, opening with the line “Making money is not a thing you do—it’s a skill you learn.” The headings are imperatives, and there is no argument building from one to the next: each is a cluster of excerpts around the 2018 tweetstorm.

Part I · Section 1

Building Wealth

Specific knowledge, accountability and leverage, applied with equity and judgment for long enough that compounding does the rest.

The skill, and the tweetstorm

  • He opens with a boast that is also the thesis: drop him penniless on a random street in any English-speaking country and within five or ten years he would be wealthy again, because it is a skill set anyone can develop.
  • It is not really about hard work: you can work in a restaurant eighty hours a week and not get rich. Getting rich is knowing what to do, who to do it with and when. Hard work matters and can’t be skimped, but it has to be pointed the right way, and until you know what to work on, finding out is the most important job.
  • He says he worked out the principles for himself at thirteen or fourteen and carried them for thirty years. The famous tweetstorm, “How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)”, is meant to be information-dense, concise and timeless: absorb it and work hard for ten years and “you’ll get what you want.” The book reprints it in full, some forty tweets, and the rest of the section expands them one by one.
  • Its closing tweet points to Part II: when you’re finally wealthy, you’ll realise it wasn’t what you were seeking in the first place, “but that is for another day.”

Understand how wealth is created

  • Money is how we transfer wealth: “social credits”, credits and debits of other people’s time. Society says thank you for work done in the past with an IOU and calls it money.
  • Wealth is what you actually want: assets that earn while you sleep. The factory and its robots, the program serving customers at night, money reinvested in other businesses; even a rented-out house, though that is a lower-productivity use of land.
  • Status is your place in the social hierarchy, a very old zero-sum game played “since monkey tribes”: for number three to become number two, number two has to fall. Politics and sports are status games. Wealth creation is an evolutionarily recent positive-sum game, and many people who attack it (“making money is evil”) are playing the status game, trying to look high-status by saying they don’t need money. He calls it the worse game, and advises ignoring it (see the figure).
  • Society will pay you for creating what it wants but does not yet know how to get, because if it knew, it would already be stamped out. Everything around us was once technology: oil made Rockefeller rich, cars made Ford rich. Technology is, in a line he first credits to Alan Kay and then corrects to Danny Hillis, the set of things that don’t quite work yet.
  • Then scale it: build thousands or millions, not one. Steve Jobs and his team saw that people would want a computer in their pocket with the phone’s abilities “times one hundred”, and worked out how to build and then scale it. “Technology democratizes consumption but consolidates production”: the best person in the world at anything gets to do it for everyone.

Find and build specific knowledge

  • “Specific knowledge is knowledge you cannot be trained for. If society can train you, it can train someone else and replace you.” It is found by pursuing genuine curiosity and passion rather than whatever is hot; it is taught, if at all, by apprenticeship, not school; it is often highly technical or creative, and can’t be outsourced or automated. “Specific knowledge cannot be taught, but it can be learned.”
  • Sales is his first example: there really are naturals, who learned in the school yard or negotiating with their parents, though anyone can improve by reading Robert Cialdini or doing door-to-door sales, which is brutal but fast.
  • The practical test: look at what you did almost effortlessly as a kid or teenager, something you didn’t think of as a skill but others noticed. His examples: sales, musical talent, an obsessive personality, a love of science fiction, lots of game-playing (so you grasp game theory), gossip (which might make a good journalist). It is a weird mix of DNA, upbringing and your response to it.
  • His own case: he wanted to be a scientist, and still ranks scientists at the top of humanity’s “production chain”, but what he was actually good at was making money, tinkering with technology and selling people on things. His mother named it from the kitchen when he was fifteen or sixteen: overhearing him tell a friend he would be an astrophysicist, she said, “No, you’re going to go into business.” She was right. Venture investing, which rewards learning fast and getting bored, suits him.
  • Specific knowledge often sits at the edge of knowledge, and in the domain of ideas someone 100% into a subject will outperform someone who isn’t by a lot, not a little, because compounding and leverage apply. The internet has massively broadened the space of possible careers: any niche can scale, and everyone is the best at something, being themselves. A separate tweet, not in the tweetstorm, says it shortest: “Escape competition through authenticity.” Nobody can compete with Joe Rogan, Scott Adams or Bill Watterson at being them.
  • The most important skill for getting rich is being a perpetual learner. The old model (four years of school, thirty of a profession) is gone; now you may have to master a new field in nine to twelve months, and it may be obsolete four years later. Foundations matter more than depth in fashionable things: arithmetic over calculus, plain English over poetry, persuasive speaking over digital marketing. But be deep in one or two things, usually the ones you are obsessed with.

Play long-term games with long-term people

  • “All the returns in life, whether in wealth, relationships, or knowledge, come from compound interest.” Compounding capital is only the beginning. The CEO of a public company or a manager of billions holds the job because people trust them, and they are trusted because their relationships and work compounded over years, visibly and accountably. A sterling reputation built for decades can end up thousands of times more valuable than an equally talented person’s.
  • With someone you’ve worked with for five or ten years, the little foibles are gone and negotiation becomes simple. His example is the angel investor Elad Gil, who always rounds in Naval’s favour and pays small costs out of his own pocket, so Naval sends him every deal and goes out of his way to pay for him in return.
  • “Intentions don’t matter. Actions do. That’s why being ethical is hard.”
  • “99% of effort is wasted”, in the goal sense though not the learning sense: most school papers, most dates before your spouse. The point is not cynicism but search. Find the 1% you can go all-in on for life and compound, and when you know a relationship or a class isn’t it, move on.
  • From the tweetstorm: pick business partners with high intelligence, energy and, above all, integrity; don’t partner with cynics and pessimists, whose beliefs are self-fulfilling.

Take on accountability

  • To get rich you need leverage, and labour and capital have to be given to you: someone must follow you, someone must fund you. That takes credibility, built under your own name, which is risky. Accountability cuts both ways: credit when things go well, the brunt of failure when they don’t.
  • There is no more debtors’ prison and nobody is executed for losing other people’s money, but we are still hardwired not to fail in public under our own names, so those who can gain a lot of power. The old captain went down with his ship; in business the equivalent is being last to get your capital and your time back.
  • His own risk: talking philosophy, then crypto, under his own name, against backchannel warnings that he was ending his career. And the reassurance: in good ecosystems even personal bankruptcy wipes the slate, and people forgive failure if you were honest and made a high-integrity effort. So people should take on far more accountability than they do.

Build or buy equity in a business

  • “If you don’t own a piece of a business, you don’t have a path towards financial freedom.” Paid for your time, even as a lawyer or doctor, you earn only while working: not asleep, not on holiday, not in retirement, and never nonlinearly.
  • Doctors who get really rich open a practice that builds a brand, or create a device, procedure or intellectual property. An employer carries the risk, accountability, IP and brand, “and they’re going to pay you the bare minimum they have to, to get you to do their job.”
  • Equity means owning the upside; debt means owning guaranteed revenue and the downside. You can own equity as a shareholder, through stock options at a tech company (“a fine way to start”), by founding a company or by investing. Real wealth usually comes from the last two.

Find a position of leverage

  • “We live in an age of infinite leverage”, and the rewards for genuine intellectual curiosity have never been higher. Follow curiosity rather than what is making money now; knowledge only you have comes out of passions and hobbies. Do things for their own sake, which is one definition of art; the year he made the most money was the year he worked least hard and cared least about the future.
  • Labour, other people working for you, is the oldest and most fought-over lever, and he calls it the worst: managing people is messy, and “you’re one short hop from a mutiny.” It will impress your parents; don’t waste your life chasing it.
  • Capital is trickier and more modern, the dominant lever of the last century, visible in who the richest people are: bankers, corrupt politicians who print money, and CEOs of old companies whose job is really financial. It scales well: managing more money is easier than managing more people.
  • Products with no marginal cost of replication, books, media, films and code, are the newest and most democratic lever, begun with the printing press, accelerated by broadcasting and exploding with the internet. They are permissionless. “Forget rich versus poor, white-collar versus blue. It’s now leveraged versus un-leveraged.” The last generation’s fortunes were Buffett’s, made with capital; the new ones are code and media: Bezos, Zuckerberg, Page, Brin, Gates, Jobs, Joe Rogan (he estimates $50 to $100 million a year from the podcast), PewDiePie.
  • “An army of robots is freely available—it’s just packed in data centers for heat and space efficiency. Use it.” If you can’t code, write books and blogs, record videos and podcasts.
  • A leveraged worker can out-produce an unleveraged one by a factor of a thousand or ten thousand; he says 1,000× programmers exist, naming John Carmack, Notch and Satoshi Nakamoto. One engineer writing the right little application can create half a billion dollars of value while ten engineers working ten times as hard on the wrong model waste their time. Forty-hour weeks are a relic of the Industrial Age; knowledge workers should work like athletes: train and sprint, then rest.
  • “Learn to sell. Learn to build. If you can do both, you will be unstoppable.” Building covers design, development, manufacturing, logistics, running a service; selling covers marketing, communicating, recruiting, fundraising, inspiring, PR. Avoid roles like customer service, where inputs and outputs stay closely linked.
  • The one thing to avoid is the risk of ruin: stay out of jail (“It’s never worth it to wear an orange jumpsuit”), out of physical danger and out of total loss. Don’t gamble everything on one go; take rationally optimistic bets with big upsides.

Get paid for your judgment

  • He would love to be paid purely for judgment, with a robot, capital or computer doing the work, and tracked on outputs, not inputs. Choose jobs and deals that allow that, and time management mostly takes care of itself.
  • Imagine someone demonstrably right 85% of the time instead of 75%: you would pay them $50 million, $100 million, $200 million, because 10% better judgment steering a $100 billion ship is worth it. CEOs are highly paid because of their leverage.
  • Demonstrated judgment is the asset. Warren Buffett wins because he has been accountable and right, repeatedly, in public, with a reputation for integrity, so people throw “infinite leverage” behind him and nobody asks when he gets up. “Warren Buffett spends a year deciding and a day acting. That act lasts decades.”

Prioritize and focus

  • His record: the first little fortune he made he lost at once in the stock market; the second he was cheated out of by business partners; only the third time was a charm. Even then it came slowly, a few chips at a time, never in one big payout. Now, thanks to the internet, opportunities pour “out of my ears” and time is the constraint.
  • Set an aspirational hourly rate and stick to it; it should feel absurdly high, and if it doesn’t, raise it. Long before he had money, he told himself his time was worth $5,000 an hour (looking back, he says, it was really about $1,000), which as a salary is millions a year. If a task can be outsourced for less than your rate, outsource it; if a problem costs less than your rate to leave alone, ignore it. He still argued with electricians and returned broken speakers sometimes, but made a theatrical show of throwing things out rather than returning them, and still tells his wife and mother, “I don’t do that.”
  • “If you secretly despise wealth, it will elude you.” A relative mindset breeds envy that other people can sense in any deal. Be optimistic: optimists do better in the long run.
  • For the young: spend far more time on the three big decisions, where you live, who you’re with and what you do (see the figure). And, from an old boss: “You’ll never be rich since you’re obviously smart, and someone will always offer you a job that’s just good enough.”

Find work that feels like play

  • Hunter-gatherers worked for themselves; agriculture made us hierarchical and factories extremely so. The internet is taking us back to more people working for themselves. “There are almost 7 billion people on this planet. Someday, I hope, there will be almost 7 billion companies.” He would rather be a failed entrepreneur than someone who never tried.
  • What you really want is freedom. His definition of retirement: “when you stop sacrificing today for an imaginary tomorrow.” There are three routes (see the figure).
  • Once the desperation went, making money became “more of an art” for him: he can raise money, assemble a team and launch a business in three months, for fun, and now passes on good investments whose products bore him. When younger he would have taken a sewage-trucking business if offered; he is glad no one offered.
  • “I’m just playing, for sixteen hours a day.” Competitors who are working will lose. The lust for money, though, is a bottomless pit: the desire doesn’t switch off at some number, and “the punishment for the love of money is delivered at the same time as the money.” His defence is not to upgrade his lifestyle as he earns, and to value freedom above everything.
  • Do you have to start a company? The most consistently successful people in Silicon Valley are venture capitalists and those who spot companies that have just hit product–market fit and join them to help them scale. Early promotion matters, and for someone early in their career the most important thing about a company is its alumni network.

How to get lucky

  • Why “without getting lucky”? “In 1,000 parallel universes, you want to be wealthy in 999 of them,” not in the fifty where you got lucky. He and his co-founder Babak Nivi were discussing on Twitter the four kinds of luck (see the figure); the fourth, where your character makes luck find you, becomes so deterministic that “it stops being luck.”
  • His example of the fourth: be the best deep-sea diver in the world, and when someone else blindly finds a sunken treasure ship they can’t reach, their luck becomes yours. In business, the equivalent is a reputation as a trusted, long-term dealmaker, so that strangers who want to do a deal bring it to you and give you a cut, as they do with Buffett.
  • Nivi’s line: “In a long-term game, it seems that everybody is making each other rich. And in a short-term game, it seems like everybody is making themselves rich.”
  • Business networking is “a complete waste of time.” Be a maker who makes something interesting; show and practise your craft, and the right people will find you. And a telltale: someone who talks a lot about how honest they are is probably dishonest. “Sharks eat well but live a life surrounded by sharks.” He warns a friend once about behaviour he isn’t proud of, then distances himself: “The closer you want to get to me, the better your values have to be.”

Be patient

  • Great people have great outcomes; you just have to be patient. Nearly everyone he met early in his career who struck him as super capable became extremely successful, on a longer timescale than anyone wanted.
  • “Apply specific knowledge with leverage and eventually, you will get what you deserve.” The world is efficient and immediate doesn’t work. If you keep count, you’ll run out of patience before success arrives. The most common bad advice he hears is “You’re too young”; most of history was built by young people who got the credit later.
  • “Your real résumé is just a catalog of all your suffering”: what you’ll remember on your deathbed is the hard things you did. Kids born rich have no meaning to their lives. Making money is a fine hard thing to choose.
  • But money only solves money problems. It won’t make you happy, healthy, fit or calm, and the person you become to make it, anxious, competitive, hard-working for decades, can’t switch off when it arrives. “Let’s get you rich first,” he says, because the Buddha was a prince before he went into the woods; money in a bank lets you buy the freedom the monks had to renounce everything for. His programme: “Let’s get them all rich. Let’s get them all fit and healthy. Then, let’s get them all happy.”

Part I · Section 2

Building Judgment

“There’s no shortcut to smart.” Leverage multiplies judgment, so being a little more right is worth an absurd amount, and judgment is built from clear thinking, a small ego, good models and a lot of reading.

Judgment

  • “Hard work is really overrated.” What is underrated is judgment. His definitions: wisdom is knowing the long-term consequences of your actions; judgment is wisdom applied to external problems. “In an age of leverage, one correct decision can win everything.”
  • But without hard work you develop neither judgment nor leverage. Direction matters more than speed, especially with leverage: pick the right direction, then start walking.
  • “You don’t get rich by spending your time to save money. You get rich by saving your time to make money.”

How to think clearly

  • “‘Clear thinker’ is a better compliment than ‘smart.’” Real knowledge is built from the ground up: you can’t understand trigonometry without arithmetic and geometry. Someone using lots of fancy words probably doesn’t know what they’re talking about; if you can’t explain it to a child, you don’t know it. Richard Feynman, in Six Easy Pieces, builds mathematics from counting to pre-calculus in three pages without relying on definitions.
  • If you can’t re-derive a concept from basics when you need it, you’re lost; you have only memorised it. Advanced concepts are less proven and mostly used to signal insider knowledge.
  • What clouds reality is the “monkey mind” and its preconceived notions of how things should be. One definition of suffering: the moment you see things exactly as they are, having hidden a failing business from yourself. So suffering is also a moment of truth, and progress starts from the truth. Shrink the ego and your desires about the outcome, and reality gets easier to see.
  • That is why you can solve a friend’s break-up in a minute (“get over her, she wasn’t good for you anyway”) and not your own: their desire is colliding with reality. In business, when something isn’t working, he acknowledges it publicly in front of co-founders so he can’t hide it from himself.
  • Keep empty space: a day or two a week with no meetings (“if you budget two, you’ll end up with one”), because great ideas come only after you’re bored. “A contrarian isn’t one who always objects—that’s a conformist of a different sort.” Optimistic contrarians are the rarest breed.

Shed your identity to see reality

  • Our egos are built in our first two decades by environment, parents and society, and we spend the rest of life trying to please them. We need habits, but we bundle them into an identity (“I’m Naval. This is the way I am”) and get attached. Take them apart: is this habit something I picked up as a toddler seeking attention, and does it still serve me?
  • “Any belief you took in a package (ex. Democrat, Catholic, American) is suspect and should be re-evaluated from base principles.” He used to identify as libertarian and found himself defending positions he hadn’t thought through. If all your beliefs line up in neat bundles, be highly suspicious. “To be honest, speak without identity.”
  • Suffering can force an identity change: an injured athlete, like Bruce Lee, may have to become a philosopher. “Facebook redesigns. Twitter redesigns.” Personalities and careers need redesigns too.

Learn the skills of decision-making

  • “The classical virtues are all decision-making heuristics to make one optimize for the long term rather than for the short term.” Almost all biases are time-saving heuristics; for important decisions, discard memory and identity and look at the problem. Self-serving conclusions deserve a higher bar.
  • Radical honesty means being free to say what you think and think what you say; a lie told to someone else is first a lie to yourself. He pairs it with Buffett’s rule, “praise specifically, criticize generally,” so people’s egos work for you. “It’s almost always possible to be honest and positive.”
  • Decision-making is everything: someone right 80% of the time instead of 70% will be paid hundreds of times more. If you manage $1 billion and are right 10% more often, one judgment call is worth $100 million. He recommends the Farnam Street blog for this. “The more you know, the less you diversify.”
  • If you can’t decide, the answer is no. Marry this person, take this job, buy this house, move to this city, go into business with this person? Modern life offers endless options (seven billion people, hundreds of thousands of careers) while we evolved in tribes of 150, where the one person who came along might be your only choice. These decisions lock you in for five, ten or twenty years, so say yes only when you are very sure. If you’re building a spreadsheet of pros and cons, forget it.
  • Run uphill. If two options are evenly matched, take the one more painful in the short term. Your brain is avoiding short-term pain; by definition the painful path carries the long-term gain, and compounding favours long-term gain. Working out and hard reading are the same trade.

Collect mental models

  • The brain is a memory-prediction machine, and “X happened before, so X will happen again” is a lousy way to predict. You want principles. His best models came from evolution, game theory and Charlie Munger, with Taleb and Benjamin Franklin close behind.
  • His tweets are mnemonics, pointers to principles he has the experience to back. Without that experience, a collection of models “just reads like a collection of quotes”: inspiring for a moment, then forgotten.
  • His set: evolution (one theory: civilisation exists to answer who gets to mate, since sperm is abundant and eggs are scarce); inversion (he can’t say what will work, only eliminate what won’t: success is avoiding incorrect judgments); complexity theory (which taught him the limits of prediction); microeconomics and game theory; the principal–agent problem (Julius Caesar: “If you want it done, then go. And if not, then send”); compound interest (see the figure); basic maths (arithmetic, probability and statistics, not calculus); black swans (Taleb, “one of the greatest philosopher-scientists of our times”); calculus as an idea about rates of change; and falsifiability, the most important and least understood principle for anyone claiming science is on their side. Macroeconomics, he says, makes no falsifiable predictions and has become corrupted.

Learn to love to read

  • “We live in the age of Alexandria”: every book is a fingertip away, and the desire to learn is what is scarce. Reading was his first love, from his grandfather’s Reader’s Digests on the floor in India to comics and whatever he could find; nobody told him what not to read.
  • “Read what you love until you love to read.” He reads one to two hours a day, which he says puts him in the top 0.00001%, and credits that habit for any material success he has had. The best book, like the best workout, is the one you are excited to do every day.
  • How he reads: skims, jumps around, drops a book if the first chapter doesn’t grab him, keeps ten to twenty going at once, buys any book someone mentions, and rereads more than he reads. Most non-fiction has one point and then example after example; once he has the gist he stops. “The number of books completed is a vanity metric.” Twitter had made him a worse reader and a better writer; he got back to books by treating them like throwaway blog posts.
  • Foundations first: stick to science and the basics, where almost nobody disagrees (maths, hard sciences, microeconomics). Read originals before commentary: Darwin before Dawkins, Adam Smith, von Mises or Hayek before modern macro, Marx if you are into socialism. Study logic and maths until no book in the library scares you; confusion is a mental workout. Some very well-read people aren’t smart because they read the wrong things in the wrong order and built on them.
  • “When solving problems: the older the problem, the older the solution.” To learn to fly a plane, read something modern; for how to stay healthy, calm and good, a book that has survived two thousand years has been filtered by many people. “Explain what you learned to someone else. Teaching forces learning.”

“The genuine love for reading itself, when cultivated, is a superpower.”Building Judgment

Part II

Happiness

About eighty pages in three sections, opened by a tweet that reorders everything before it: “The three big ones in life are wealth, health, and happiness. We pursue them in that order, but their importance is reverse.” The claim underneath is that happiness can be trained like fitness, within a range set by temperament.

Part II · Section 3

Learning Happiness

“Don’t take yourself so seriously. You’re just a monkey with a plan.” Happiness is what is left when the sense that something is missing goes away, and it is a skill built from presence, peace, fewer desires, better habits and acceptance.

Happiness is learned

  • Ten years earlier he would have dismissed the question, and on a scale of one to ten put himself at 2 or 3, maybe 4 on his best days. Now he says 9. Money helps, but it is a very small piece; most of it came from deciding his own happiness mattered most and cultivating it with techniques (see the figure).
  • It is a personal question, like what happens when we die or whom to marry, and definitions differ: flow, satisfaction, contentment. His current one: happiness is a default state, there when you remove the sense of something missing. We are “highly judgmental survival-and-replication machines” trapped in a web of “I need this”; when nothing is missing, the mind stops running into the past or future, and the internal silence is contentment.
  • It is not about positive thoughts. Every positive thought holds a negative one as its contrast (“the Tao Te Ching says this more articulately”): if I’m happy, I was sad once; if he’s attractive, someone else isn’t. So it is the absence of desire, especially for external things; latch onto a happy feeling and try to keep it, and the mind is moving again.
  • Nature has no concept of happiness; it follows unbroken laws from the Big Bang to now. Reality is neutral, and “the world just reflects your own feelings back at you.” That is what he means by calling happiness a choice.
  • It helps to believe in the complete insignificance of the self. If you are the most important thing in the universe, it must bend to you; see your works as writing on water or castles in sand and you have no expectation of how life “should” be. The neutral state is not bland: it is how small children live. “Our lives are a blink of a firefly in the night.”
  • Meditation helps accept reality, but “it’s amazing how little it helps”: a long-time meditator lifting one-pound weights still gets flattened when someone drops a loaded barbell on them. His list of what works, plainly: lowered identity, less mental chatter, no politics, no unhappy people, reading philosophy, meditating, happy company. “And it works.”

Happiness is a choice, and requires presence

  • “Happiness, love, and passion...aren’t things you find—they’re choices you make.” The mind is as malleable as the body, yet we work on everything else while accepting ourselves as programmed in youth; memory and identity are burdens that keep us from living in the present.
  • Walking down the street, only a small part of your brain is on the present; the rest is planning or regretting. He says he doesn’t believe in anything from his past: no memories, regrets, people or trips, because comparing past with present breeds unhappiness. Anticipating our vices pulls us into the future, so cutting vices makes presence easier.
  • “Enlightenment is the space between your thoughts,” which means it can be had moment to moment, a certain percentage every day. “What if this life is the paradise we were promised, and we’re just squandering it?”

Happiness requires peace

  • Happiness is an overloaded word; for him it now means peace more than joy. An internal purpose can make you happy; an external one (“society wants me to do X”, “I am the first son of the first son”) won’t.
  • The enemy is a low-level pervasive anxiety, a “nexting” that keeps asking what comes next. Try sitting and doing nothing, not even reading or music, and you can’t. His method: don’t fight the anxiety, notice it is a stream of thoughts and ask, “Would I rather be having this thought right now, or would I rather have my peace?”
  • “A happy person isn’t someone who’s happy all the time. It’s someone who effortlessly interprets events in such a way that they don’t lose their innate peace.”

Every desire is a chosen unhappiness

  • The most common human mistake is believing some external circumstance will make you happy, which he calls fundamental Buddhist wisdom, not his own. His example: “We bought a new car,” and every night while waiting for it he read the forums about it, knowing he would stop caring the moment it arrived. He was addicted to the desiring.
  • This is not a call to lie on the sand and meditate. You are a living creature that locally reverses entropy; you should self-actualise and do what you are meant to do. The delusion is that the outside change will bring lasting peace.
  • “Desire is a contract you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want.” He tries not to hold more than one big desire at a time, and to recognise it as “the axis of my suffering”, the place he has chosen to be unhappy (see the figure). It is more important to perfect your desires than to do something you don’t 100% desire.
  • Doing more when young means taking on more desires: younger people are healthier but less happy, older people happier but less healthy. Young, you have time and health but no money; middle-aged, money and health but no time; old, money and time but no health. The trick is to get all three at once.

Success does not earn happiness

  • “Happiness is being satisfied with what you have. Success comes from dissatisfaction. Choose.”
  • Asked about Confucius’s “second life”, he says that when he reached his material and social goals he saw that the people around him who had done the same weren’t that happy, and that he adapted to anything quickly. The trite conclusion that happiness is internal set him on the inner path.
  • Whom does he call successful? Creators like Steve Jobs, Marc Andreessen for Netscape, Satoshi Nakamoto for Bitcoin, Elon Musk. But the real winners step out of the game entirely and need nothing from anybody: his trainer Jerzy Gregorek, and historically the Buddha and Krishnamurti. Pascal: all man’s troubles come from being unable to sit quietly in a room alone. “If you could just sit for thirty minutes and be happy, you are successful.”
  • “Peace is happiness at rest, and happiness is peace in motion.” Most of us try to get peace through war (a business, a fight over the dishes), resolving external problems, of which there are unlimited numbers. The only way to peace inside is to give up the idea of problems.

Envy is the enemy of happiness

  • He is trying to drop the word “should”, which is guilt or social programming. Training yourself to be happy is a single-player game with no external validation, and we social creatures only know how to play multiplayer ones (see the figure). Life is single-player: born alone, die alone, “gone in three generations, and nobody cares.” Buffett’s question: would you rather be the world’s best lover and known as the worst, or the reverse? “All the real scorecards are internal.”
  • Jealousy was hard for him to overcome. What ended it: you can’t take one feature of someone’s life (his body, her money). You would have to swap wholesale, 24/7, with their reactions, desires, family, happiness level and self-image, and he didn’t want to be anybody else.

Happiness is built by habits

  • His most surprising discovery of five years: peace and happiness are skills, like nutrition, working out, making money or love, within a genetic range. You find what works by trial and error: Tantra or Vipassana meditation, a ten-day retreat or twenty minutes, yoga, kite-surfing, car racing, cooking. For medicines of the mind the placebo effect is fully effective, so go in believing; he tried Eckhart Tolle’s body-energy exercise from The Power of Now instead of dismissing it, and it felt good.
  • Stable-mood habits: no alcohol, no sugar, no Facebook, Snapchat or Twitter; video games (he was an avid gamer) trade long-term happiness for short-term dopamine, and so does caffeine. “You are a combination of your habits and the people who you spend the most time with.” The “five chimps theory”: you can predict a chimp’s behaviour from the five it spends most time with, so choose your five deliberately. His friend Behzad’s secret: “Stop asking why and start saying wow.”
  • His daily tricks: insight meditation; catching himself judging and finding the positive interpretation (once seconds, now sub-second); sunlight on the skin, looking up and smiling; asking of each desire, “Is it so important to me I’ll be unhappy unless this goes my way?”; dropping caffeine; working out daily (“If you have peace of body, it’s easier to have peace of mind”); telling friends he is a happy person so consistency bias holds him to it.
  • From his tweets: use the phone, calendar and alarm clock apps less; the more secrets you have, the less happy you are; hedonic adaptation is stronger for man-made things (cars, houses, clothes, money) than natural ones (food, sex, exercise); “all screen activities linked to less happiness”; measure how much of your day is obligation rather than interest; the news’ job is to make you anxious and angry, while the underlying trends are positive.

Find happiness in acceptance

  • In any situation you have three choices: change it, accept it, or leave it (see the figure). Changing it is a desire and will cost you suffering until done, so pick one at a time. The phrase he uses to himself most is one word: “accept.” Acceptance is being okay whatever the outcome, balanced, seeing the grander scheme.
  • Two hacks. For big things: write down past episodes of suffering (a break-up, a business failure, a health scare) and trace the growth that came from each years later. For small things: ask at once, “What is the positive of this situation?” Late for a meeting? Watch the birds and spend less time in a boring meeting. Someone floods your inbox with a hundred photos? You get to pick your five favourites. Over a year this became almost instant.
  • The deepest hack is embracing death. Much of what we struggle for is a quest for immortality: an afterlife, children, a legacy. “Here’s a hot tip: There is no legacy.” Our works, civilisations, planet and solar system will all be dust; name a single Sumerian. So your unhappiness helps nobody. “Do something positive. Project some love. Make someone happy. Laugh a little bit. Appreciate the moment. And do your work.”

Part II · Section 4

Saving Yourself

“Doctors won’t make you healthy… Mentors won’t make you rich… Save yourself.” Health first, then habits, then the mind, with an order decided once so the daily negotiation never happens.

Choosing to be yourself

  • “No, you don’t need to do anything.” Stop scribbling lists of what you should do and listen to the voice that wants things done a certain way. His greatest mentor, whom he never met, had one message: “Be yourself, with passionate intensity.”
  • The combinatorics of DNA and experience mean no two people are substitutable, so emulation is a fool’s errand. Your goal is to find the people, business, project or art that need you the most. “To make an original contribution, you have to be irrationally obsessed with something.”

Choosing to care for yourself

  • His number one priority, “above my happiness, above my family, above my work,” is his own health: physical first, then mental, then spiritual, then his family’s health, then their wellbeing (see the figure). Only then the rest of the world.
  • How modern life departs from how we evolved: diets we didn’t evolve for (closer to paleo is probably right: mostly vegetables, a little meat, berries), treadmills instead of play, a visual cortex overloaded at the expense of the other senses, shoes, constant warmth instead of some cold exposure, sterile homes (the hygiene hypothesis), small families instead of tribes, phones checked every five minutes. “We evolved for scarcity but live in abundance,” with bodies that don’t know how to say no.
  • Diet. Nothing outside maths, physics and chemistry is settled science, and diet is like politics: everyone is an expert. Keto seems hard to follow. Fat drives satiety and sugar drives hunger, and the sugar effect dominates, so the deadly combination is the two together, which is what every dessert is; in nature it occurs almost only in tropical fruits. Quality control is easier than quantity control; fasting from a low-carb base is easier than portion control. “When it comes to medicine and nutrition, subtract before you add.” The world’s simplest diet: the more processed the food, the less of it.
  • Exercise. The habit that most changed his life is the daily morning workout. “I don’t have time” means “it’s not a priority”; because health is first, he doesn’t start the day until he has worked out, “however long it takes,” and can count his breaks each year on one hand. What you do matters less than doing something every day. “One month of consistent yoga and I feel 10 years younger.” Walking meetings are shorter, sunnier and have no slides. His trainer, Jerzy Gregorek: “Easy choices, hard life. Hard choices, easy life.”

Meditation and mental strength

  • “An emotion is our evolved biology predicting the future impact of a current event. In modern settings, it’s usually exaggerated or wrong.” The breath is where the autonomic nervous system meets the voluntary one; relaxed breathing tells the body it is safe, freeing resources for the rest of the body.
  • Cold. Inspired by Wim Hof on Tim Ferriss’s podcast, he tried cold showers with Hof’s breathing method, despite being “strongly against the idea of ice baths” as someone from the Indian subcontinent. At first he eased in wincing; after four or five months he walked straight in. The lesson: “most of our suffering comes from avoidance.” Once you’re in, it’s just cold; your body saying so differs from your mind saying so. Hot showers became one less thing he needs.
  • “Meditation is intermittent fasting for the mind.” Too much sugar makes a heavy body; too many distractions make a heavy mind.
  • Everyone says they meditate, like dieting, and almost nobody does. He has tried at least four forms. What works best for him is Choiceless Awareness: walking, ideally in nature, accepting the moment without judging (not “he’s out of shape”, not laughing at a bad hair day because he fears losing his own). He found 90% of his thoughts fear-based, the rest desire. Ten or fifteen minutes of it leaves him peaceful and grateful. Transcendental meditation, or simply watching thoughts until the fears dissolve, also work.
  • His strongest prescription: sit for an hour each morning, eyes closed, making no effort for or against anything, for sixty days. Unresolved experiences have stuck to you like barnacles; they come up like “a giant inbox of unanswered emails, going back to your childhood,” and observing them resolves them, until one day you reach a mental “inbox zero,” which he describes as joy, bliss and peace. “Meditation isn’t hard. All you have to do is sit there and do nothing.”
  • Meditation does not give you control of your mind; it shows how out of control it is, like a monkey flinging faeces round a room, and in the separation from it “is liberation.” He tries to run his brain in “debugging mode,” catching himself fantasy-rehearsing a podcast while brushing his teeth. The mind is a multilayer system: a calm base layer of awareness, like an operating-system kernel, with the anxious “monkey mind” as an application he runs only when needed.
  • An Osho lecture he likes, “The Attraction for Drugs Is Spiritual,” argues that drinking, cannabis and psychedelics, like thrill-seeking and flow states, are attempts to get out of your own head. He wants his sense of self to weaken with age, not strengthen. His list: hiking is walking meditation, journaling writing meditation, praying gratitude meditation, showering accidental meditation, sitting quietly direct meditation.

Choosing to build yourself

  • “The greatest superpower is the ability to change yourself.” His exercise for finding mistakes: at thirty, what advice would you give your twenty-year-old self; at forty, your thirty-year-old self; year by year. His own verdict: he would do the same things with less emotion and less anger. The Epinions lawsuit ended well for him, “but there was a lot of angst and a lot of anger”; today he would simply say what happened, what is fair, and what he is going to do.
  • We are collections of thousands of habits running subconsciously. A light daily workout from his trainer, done every single day, transformed him physically and mentally and taught him the power of habits: “To have peace of mind, you have to have peace of body first.” Getting fit from out of shape is a ten-year journey of swapping one habit every six months or so.
  • Krishnamurti’s “internal state of revolution”: saying you will “try” is wimping out and buying time. If you really wanted it, you would do it. To quit smoking, tell everyone you know you have quit; if you can’t, admit you’re not ready and commit publicly to a smaller goal for three or six months.
  • “Impatience with actions, patience with results.” Do what has to be done at once and with full attention, then wait, because markets, people and products take time. Nivi: inspiration is perishable; act on it immediately.

Choosing to grow yourself

  • He doesn’t believe in specific goals; Scott Adams: “Set up systems, not goals.” Choose environments where you are statistically likely to thrive. He doesn’t want to be the most successful person on the planet, only the most successful version of himself working the least hard possible, so that if his life played out 1,000 times, “Naval is successful 999 times.”
  • He sticks to basics even in physics, and calls himself not religious but spiritual: studying the laws of the universe gives him the awe someone else might feel in Mecca or Medina. Science is the study of truth, and mathematics is its language.
  • Most reading today is for social approval, to fit in with the other monkeys; people read a hundred books about evolution and never Darwin. The smartest and most successful people he knows started as “losers” cast out of normal society, which freed them to go their own way. “For self-improvement without self-discipline, update your self-image.”
  • What he would pass to his children: read everything, for its own sake, even romance novels and comics, until you guide yourself to what you should be reading; and learn mathematics and persuasion. Know statistics and probability “forwards and backwards and inside out.”

Choosing to free yourself

  • “The hardest thing is not doing what you want—it’s knowing what you want.” There are no adults; everyone makes it up as they go.
  • Freedom is still one of his top three values, but the definition changed: from “freedom to” do whatever he likes, to “freedom from” reaction, anger, sadness and being forced to do things (see the figure).
  • From expectations: he doesn’t measure his own effectiveness, which he sees as self-punishment. If others have an agreement with you, it’s your problem; if they only have an expectation, it’s theirs. “Courage is not caring what other people think.” He is known for leaving parties the moment he decides they are a waste of his time: “Value your time. It is all you have.” You are not responsible for making other people happy.
  • From anger: anger signals the capacity for violence and is a loss of control, “a contract you make with yourself to be in physical and mental and emotional turmoil until reality changes.” An angry person pushing your head under water is drowning too.
  • From employment: people who live far below their means have a freedom lifestyle-upgraders can’t imagine. “A taste of freedom can make you unemployable.”
  • From uncontrolled thinking: children live in the moment; puberty is the onset of desire and long-range planning, and with it the ego. A thousand people on a street are all talking to themselves. The mind should be “a servant and a tool, not a master.” He admits he won’t reach enlightenment given how involved he is in the rat race: at best, “a rat who might be able to look up at the clouds once in a while.”

Part II · Section 5

Philosophy

“The real truths are heresies.” Three answers to the meaning of life, four values held as refusals, a Buddhism kept only where he can test it, and the present moment as all there is.

The meanings of life

  • A big question, so three answers (see the figure). First: it’s personal. Any answer from the Buddha or from him will sound like nonsense; the important thing is to sit and dig with the question, perhaps for decades.
  • Second: there is none. Osho: “It’s like writing on water or building houses of sand.” You were dead for the ten billion years before you and will be for the seventy billion or so until the universe’s heat death; even the Mars colonists will disappear. So you make meaning up: a play you watch, a self-actualisation dance, a desire you pursue for the heck of it. Any cosmic answer would only prompt another “why”, turtles all the way down. He doesn’t buy afterlives judged on seventy years (“What kind of silly God…?”); after death is like before birth.
  • Third, from science books friends have written, and less satisfying: the arrow of time comes from entropy, which only rises. Living things reverse it locally because we act, and in doing so accelerate it globally toward heat death, when everything is at one energy level and indistinguishable. Every complex thing we build, “computers, civilization, art, mathematics, or creating a family,” speeds us toward the point where we end up as one thing.

Live by your values

  • He has never fully listed them, but gives four, each phrased as something he won’t compromise on (see the figure). Honesty, so he never has to watch what he says; “Before you can lie to another, you must first lie to yourself.” No short-term thinking or dealing. Only peer relationships, neither above nor below anyone. No anger: “Anger is a hot coal you hold in your hand while waiting to throw it at somebody,” so he cuts angry people out without judging them.
  • Good relationships of every kind are mostly people whose values line up; when values match, the little things don’t matter. His wife wasn’t sure about him at first, and chose him, he says, for his values: “To find a worthy mate, be worthy of a worthy mate” (Munger). A child answers the meaning-of-life question by moving the most important thing in the universe from your body into theirs.

Rational Buddhism

  • “The older the question, the older the answers.” The rational part means reconciling with science and evolution and rejecting what he can’t verify himself. Is meditation good for you? Is clearing the mind good? Is there a base layer of awareness under the monkey mind? Yes, yes, yes, checked personally. Past lives and karma debts, third chakras, levitation, superpowers: not falsifiable, so not fundamental truths.
  • His two poles: evolution, because it explains so much about humans (we are survival-and-replication machines with an ego that gets us up off the ground before the worms eat us), and Buddhism, “the oldest, most time-tested spiritual philosophy” of the internal state. He thinks they reconcile and wants one day to map Buddhism’s non-fanciful tenets onto a virtual-reality simulation. “Try everything, test it for yourself, be skeptical, keep what’s useful, and discard what’s not.”
  • “If wisdom could be imparted through words alone, we’d all be done here.”

The present is all we have

  • Nobody has gone back in time or successfully predicted the future in any way that matters; only this point in space and time exists, and each moment is unique and slips away too fast to grab. “You’re dying and being reborn at every moment.” The past is “some fictional little memory tape,” and death only means there are no more future moments. Here the writing stops being advice and is at its best.

“Inspiration is perishable—act on it immediately.”Philosophy, the last line of the main text

About sixteen pages of what to read next, in a book whose most repeated instruction is to read, then two short lists Naval wrote himself, twelve years apart.

Six years on

The few datable calls

The book is advice, not forecasting, but a handful of its lines are claims about where the world was going. Here is how they look from 2026. The lines are Naval’s; the verdicts are our judgement.

Held

Code and media are “the leverage behind the newly rich” Wealth

The creator economy and one-person software businesses grew through the 2020s, and AI tools since 2023 have made the “army of robots” he describes more literally available to non-programmers.

Held

“Applied Scientists are the most powerful people in the world. This will be more obvious in the coming years.” Wealth

The machine-learning boom put the researchers who build frontier models among the most sought-after and highly paid people in the economy.

Held

Bitcoin “will have repercussions for decades to come” Happiness

He named Satoshi Nakamoto among the people he calls successful. US spot exchange-traded funds for it were approved in January 2024, and it passed $100,000 that December.

Partly

“Forty hour work weeks are a relic of the Industrial Age” Wealth

Remote and flexible work spread after 2020 and never fully reversed, but most salaried work is still measured in hours, and many large employers pushed people back to the office.

Partly

“Someday, I hope, there will be almost 7 billion companies” Wealth

Offered as a hope. US business applications jumped from 2020 and stayed high, and solo work grew; employment by others is still the norm almost everywhere.

Where readers push back

Stress-testing it

Tim Ferriss’s foreword says it first: follow the advice only if it survives testing in your own life. These are the strongest objections; the book half-admits several of them.

A sample of one, who survived

“Wealthy in 999 of 1,000 universes” is a claim about a distribution argued from one life, and a life that included two lost fortunes before the third. The method is never shown working for people who are not Naval. Taleb’s Fooled by Randomness (2001), a book Naval recommends, is the best-known warning against learning from the survivors.

Passion first, or skill first?

The book says specific knowledge is found by following genuine curiosity and passion. Cal Newport’s So Good They Can’t Ignore You (2012) argues the reverse for most people: passion tends to follow mastery, and “follow your passion” is poor advice for anyone without an obvious one. Naval’s test (what did you do effortlessly as a teenager?) is kinder to the lucky few who had a commercial obsession early.

Does money buy happiness after all?

Part I promises money solves only money problems. The research is more generous to money. Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton (2010) found day-to-day emotional wellbeing levelled off around $75,000 a year; Matthew Killingsworth (2021) found it kept rising; and an adversarial collaboration of Killingsworth, Kahneman and Barbara Mellers (2023) concluded that for most people it keeps rising with income, with a plateau only among the least happy. Naval’s point survives in a weaker form: more money helps, but it is not the lever that moves most of the needle.

What meditation can and can’t do

His claims for meditation are personal and he says so. The largest review of the evidence, Madhav Goyal and colleagues in JAMA Internal Medicine (2014), found moderate evidence that meditation programmes ease anxiety, depression and pain, and little or no evidence for improved mood, attention or sleep. That fits his “it’s amazing how little it helps” better than his “inbox zero.”

Out of context by construction

Naval warns that mental models without the experience behind them read like a book of quotes, and the book’s format is a machine for producing exactly that. Jorgenson’s disclaimer concedes it. The reader most likely to quote “desire is a contract” is the one least likely to have paid for it.

The two halves need each other and undercut each other

Part I runs on ambition and one big goal; Part II calls desire chosen unhappiness. His bridge is sequence (“Let’s get you rich first,” as the Buddha was a prince first), but sequencing schedules the contradiction rather than resolving it.

Advice from the ceiling

The aspirational hourly rate is excellent for someone with slack and harmful for someone without. The book says little to people whose hours and pay are still locked together, beyond “learn to sell, learn to build.” The $5,000-an-hour habit, and handing his mother’s to-dos to an assistant, read differently from inside a hardware store.

A life optimised against obligation

Labour leverage is messy, networking a waste, angry people are cut out, other people’s expectations are their problem, and he leaves parties the moment they bore him. Coherent, but what it optimises away is being needed, which is where much of most people’s meaning lives. He half-concedes it himself when he says a child moves the centre of the universe out of your own body.

Words that do a lot of work

Glossary

Wealth
Assets that earn while you sleep.
Money
“Social credits”: how we transfer time and wealth.
Status
Rank in the social hierarchy; an old zero-sum game.
Specific knowledge
Knowledge you cannot be trained for, found by following genuine curiosity.
Accountability
Taking business risk under your own name.
Equity
Owning a piece of a business, and so the upside.
Leverage
A force multiplier for your judgment: labour, capital, or code and media.
Permissionless leverage
Code and media, products with no marginal cost of replication that need nobody’s approval.
Productize yourself
His two-word summary of the wealth half.
Aspirational hourly rate
An absurdly high value on your time, used to decide what to outsource or ignore.
Iterated games
Long-term games with long-term people, where trust and reputation compound.
Risk of ruin
The one outcome to avoid: jail, physical harm, or losing everything on one bet.
Four kinds of luck
Blind luck, hustle, a prepared mind, and character that makes luck find you.
Retirement
“When you stop sacrificing today for an imaginary tomorrow.”
Judgment
Wisdom, knowing the long-term consequences of your actions, applied to external problems.
Clear thinker
Someone who can re-derive ideas from the basics; better than “smart.”
Principal–agent problem
Agents optimise for themselves; owners care. His most fundamental economic model.
Run uphill
When evenly split, take the path more painful in the short term.
Monkey mind
The anxious, chattering layer of the mind, to be run only when needed.
Five chimps theory
You can predict a chimp’s behaviour from the five it spends most time with.
Single-player game
A game with no outside score, like peace, yoga or meditation.
Desire as a contract
An agreement with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want.
Choiceless awareness
His preferred meditation: accepting the moment without judging, often while walking.
Mental inbox zero
The state after enough hour-long sits, when nothing unresolved comes up.
Freedom from
His later definition of freedom: from reaction, anger and expectation.
Rational Buddhism
His name for keeping Buddhism’s testable practices and dropping the rest.

Using it

Reading it

  1. Read it the way it was built

    In short sittings, out of order, with a real problem in hand. Front to back in two evenings feels like reading a dictionary.

  2. If you have an hour

    The tweetstorm and “Find a Position of Leverage” in Part I; “Every Desire Is a Chosen Unhappiness” and the meditation section in Part II.

  3. Hear it unedited

    The “How to Get Rich” podcast and the Knowledge Project interview are fuller than any excerpt, and free.

    Deutsch, the Durants, Taleb and Marcus Aurelius, all summarised on this site.
