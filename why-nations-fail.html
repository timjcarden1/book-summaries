Why Nations Fail

The Origins of Power, Prosperity, and Poverty

“Countries such as Great Britain and the United States became rich because their citizens overthrew the elites who controlled power and created a society where political rights were much more broadly distributed.”

The thesis, stated in the preface and repeated throughout

Daron Acemoglu & James A. Robinson · 2012

The whole book in one breath

Prosperous countries have inclusive institutions: secure property, open markets, a level playing field, and politics broad enough that no one group can rewrite the rules in its favour. Poor countries have extractive ones, designed by a narrow elite to move income from the many to the few. Poor countries stay poor not because their rulers don’t know better, but because the reforms that would make the country rich would also cost those rulers their power, and they can see that as clearly as any economist. Institutions persist through virtuous and vicious circles, and change only at critical junctures, when a shock meets small prior differences. Poverty, in this book, is not a failure. It is a policy, and it works for the people who chose it.

If you keep only seven things

The book’s durable lessons

Ch. 3

Institutions are chosen by whoever holds power

Economic rules are downstream of political ones, so asking what a country should do about its economy without asking who decides is the wrong question.

Ch. 2

Poor countries aren’t poor by mistake

Elites usually know which reforms would bring growth and refuse them, because growth creates rivals. Bad economics is often excellent politics.

Ch. 3

The state must be strong and constrained

Pluralism without a working state gives you Somalia. A working state without pluralism gives you a more efficient extractor.

Ch. 5

Extractive growth is real, fast and finite

Reallocating resources can produce decades of impressive numbers, but not creative destruction, which is what the elite is protecting itself against.

Ch. 4

Divergence starts too small to notice

Societies drift apart institutionally, then a shock hits both, and small prior differences decide which way each breaks.

Ch. 12

Revolutions inherit the machine

The more extractive a state, the more valuable it is to capture, and the more whoever captures it wants to keep it as it is.

Since 2012

Test it on the case it is losing

Chinese frontier innovation under unreformed politics is the live counter-evidence. The authors’ later books show them adjusting.

Chapters 1–5

The theory

The book clears away rival explanations, defines inclusive and extractive institutions, and sets out how they persist and change.

Chapter 1

So Close and Yet So Different

A fence, a peninsula and two colonisations, all pointing to institutions.

  • Nogales is one town with a fence through it. Same families, food, music, climate and disease, and household income on the Arizona side is roughly three times the Sonora side, across every measure from schooling to whether the police are a service or a hazard. Korea after 1945 is the same experiment run harder.
  • In the Americas, Spain found dense, rich empires worth coercing. Pizarro captured Atahualpa at Cajamarca in 1532, and the Spanish kept the Inca machinery of extraction and put themselves on top of it, conscripting labour for the silver mines of Potosí.
  • The English at Jamestown tried extraction too and failed: there was no dense population to coerce. The Virginia Company ended up offering settlers land and a say in government. Inclusive institutions arrived because extraction was impossible, not because of an English cultural inheritance. The same English ran Barbados on the other model.
  • The book draws on Melissa Dell’s study of the Peruvian mining draft: households just inside its old boundary are poorer today than those just outside, centuries later.

Chapter 2

Theories That Don’t Work

Geography, culture and ignorance, each tested where the variable is held constant.

  • The weakest moment is geography. The case against simple climate determinism is strong. The case against geography working through institutions (disease shaping where Europeans settled, which shaped the colonial state) isn’t really made, and the authors’ own famous settler-mortality paper relies on exactly that channel.

Chapter 3

The Making of Prosperity and Poverty

Inclusive versus extractive, and the political rules behind the economic ones.

  • Inclusive economic institutions let most people take part on terms they choose: secure property, impartial law, public services, enforceable contracts and free entry into occupations. Extractive ones move income from the many to the few. The difference is not how much government there is, but whom the rules serve.
  • Economic institutions are chosen by whoever holds political power. So the real object of study is political institutions, which need two things at once: pluralism, so no faction can rewrite the rules, and enough centralisation for the state to keep order and deliver law at all (see the figure below).
  • The engine is Schumpeter’s creative destruction. Growth destroys existing trades, rents and hierarchies. In an extractive society that is existential for the elite, because its income and its power are the same asset. New money means rivals.

Chapter 4

Small Differences and Critical Junctures: The Weight of History

How institutions change: a shock meets small prior differences.

  • Societies that look alike are never quite alike. They drift, accumulating small institutional differences that don’t matter on the day.
  • Then a critical juncture arrives: a plague, a new trade route, a war. The shock hits everyone, the response differs, and the small differences decide it. Afterwards the gap locks in.
  • The Black Death of the 1340s is the cleanest case. In western Europe, scarce labour won better terms and serfdom withered. East of the Elbe, lords tightened control and imposed a second serfdom. The Elbe became an economic border still visible centuries later.
  • The Atlantic trade was the next juncture. Where the crown owned it (Spain), its profits strengthened the monarchy. Where merchants did (England, the Netherlands), they grew rich enough to demand limits on the crown.

Chapter 5

“I’ve Seen the Future, and It Works”: Growth Under Extractive Institutions

Extractive societies can grow, fast, until they hit a ceiling.

  • The title quotes Lincoln Steffens on the Soviet Union, which grew faster than anyone had seen by forcing labour from farms into heavy industry. Western economists, projecting from the fast years, predicted it would overtake the United States.
  • Extractive growth comes from reallocating existing resources, not from creative destruction. It needs a state strong enough to enforce the reallocation and an elite secure enough not to fear it. Both conditions are unstable.
  • So it ends in a stall or in infighting (see the table below). China is the book’s live prediction.

Chapters 6–10

The history

The theory applied across two thousand years: how Venice closed, how England opened, and how empires and elites reversed development elsewhere.

Chapter 6

Drifting Apart

Venice had inclusive institutions and closed them on purpose.

  • Medieval Venice’s commenda, a partnership letting a young man with no capital take goods to sea on an investor’s money and share the profit, turned nobodies into merchants and merchants into political players. Venice became one of the richest cities in Europe.
  • Then the incumbents closed the door. The Serrata, the closure of the Great Council to new families around 1297, was followed by a register of who counted and by the state taking over long-distance trade. The commenda withered, and so did Venice: from economic powerhouse to, in the authors’ phrase, a museum.
  • Venice removes both the excuse of ignorance and the excuse of circumstance. The most commercially sophisticated people in Europe closed their institutions anyway.
  • Rome’s drift from Republic to Empire makes the same point at larger scale.

Chapter 7

The Turning Point

Why 1688 happened in England, and what it produced.

  • England before 1688 was not obviously different from France or Spain. It had three small differences: Parliament already approved taxes, the crown had never monopolised long-distance trade, and the elite was divided.
  • The Atlantic made those differences decisive. Merchants outside the court grew rich and wanted a state that couldn’t confiscate their gains. In 1688 they were strong enough to invite in a foreign prince and impose terms.
  • The Glorious Revolution didn’t produce democracy, since the franchise stayed tiny for another 150 years. It produced a state that could be lobbied by non-aristocrats and could not simply take. Patents became reliable, monopolies were dismantled, and within decades the country that had refused William Lee a patent was financing steam engines.
  • Palma’s account of Portugal on this site runs the same mechanism in reverse: a windfall that lets a ruler stop asking parliament for money.

Elites blocked innovation in plain sight and said why (see the table below).

Chapter 9

Reversing Development

European expansion didn’t just miss the chance to spread prosperity. It destroyed some.

  • In the Spice Islands the Dutch East India Company wiped out local trading states and turned the islands into plantation colonies.
  • The Atlantic slave trade turned African states into slave-raiding machines, reinforcing extractive institutions that outlived the trade itself.
  • In South Africa, deliberate policy created a dual economy, keeping Black Africans out of land ownership and skilled work to supply cheap labour. The underdevelopment was built, not inherited.

Chapter 10

The Diffusion of Prosperity

How inclusive institutions spread, and why unevenly.

  • In Australia, even a convict colony developed inclusive institutions, because ex-convicts and settlers demanded rights the governors couldn’t withhold.
  • The French Revolution and Napoleon’s armies abolished feudal privileges across much of western Europe, and those regions industrialised earlier.
  • Japan’s Meiji Restoration was a critical juncture turned toward reform, while China under the late Qing stayed closed.

Chapters 11–15

The dynamics, and the present

Why good institutions reinforce themselves, why bad ones do too, where countries fail today, and the rare cases where they broke out.

Chapter 11

The Virtuous Circle

Inclusive institutions create the people who defend them, even against their own beneficiaries.

  • The Black Act of 1723 was Whig class legislation creating dozens of capital offences. When enforced, it had to go through courts that held the elite to the letter of its own law. Having made the rule of law their legitimacy, the Whigs couldn’t suspend it selectively.
  • Each extension of the vote in Britain (1832, 1867, 1884 and after) was granted by men who didn’t want to grant it, because the alternative looked worse.
  • In America, public anger at Standard Oil produced the Sherman Act and the 1911 breakup. When Franklin Roosevelt, at the height of his popularity, tried to pack the Supreme Court in 1937, his own party stopped him.

Chapter 12

The Vicious Circle

Extractive institutions guarantee that whoever overthrows the elite will want to keep the machinery.

  • The authors borrow Robert Michels’s iron law of oligarchy. Revolutions fail not because revolutionaries are hypocrites but because the institution they capture was built to be captured.
  • In Sierra Leone, Siaka Stevens inherited the British colonial chiefdoms and marketing boards and found them fit for his purposes. He had a railway ripped up to cut off a region that supported the opposition. No adviser needed to tell him it would make the country poorer.
  • Ethiopia’s Derg overthrew Haile Selassie and moved into his palace. The American South kept its plantation elite in power for a century after emancipation.

Chapter 13

Why Nations Fail Today

Each case isolates a different way the machinery fails, and in each the outcome suits somebody.

  • Zimbabwe. In 2000 the winner of a state bank’s lottery for depositors was President Mugabe. Land reform aimed at loyalty, not productivity, took the food supply with it.
  • Uzbekistan. Independence kept the Soviet cotton system: the state as sole buyer at a fraction of the world price, and schoolchildren sent to bring in the harvest.
  • Colombia. A democracy on paper, with regions where paramilitaries ruled, taxed and traded votes with national politicians. A large share of the Congress elected in 2002 later came under investigation.
  • Argentina. Rich in 1900. The diagnosis is the absence of any constraint that survives a change of government, from Perón’s impeachment of most of the Supreme Court in 1946 onward.
  • North Korea. Not a failed state but a highly successful one, measured against the objectives of the people running it.

Chapter 14

Breaking the Mould

Three escapes, each depending on circumstances no one could have arranged.

  • Botswana. In 1895 three Tswana chiefs travelled to London and persuaded the British not to hand their land to Cecil Rhodes’s company. Colonial neglect left their consultative assemblies, the kgotla, intact. When diamonds were found after independence, Seretse Khama’s government vested the mineral rights in the nation rather than the tribe on whose land they sat, and Botswana grew among the fastest in the world for decades.
  • The American South. The civil rights movement, federal intervention and economic change finally broke the planter elite’s hold in the 1950s and 1960s.
  • China after Mao. The succession fight after 1976 could have gone the other way. Deng Xiaoping’s victory brought a large move toward inclusive economic institutions with no move on the political side. The authors’ verdict: extractive growth, which will hit the ceiling unless politics opens too.

Chapter 15

Understanding Prosperity and Poverty

What follows for China, for aid, and for anyone hoping to help.

  • Growth under authoritarian rule, as in China, can’t be sustained without political change, and the book says so directly.
  • Foreign aid can’t install institutions. Much of it is captured by the elites it is meant to bypass.
  • The thin positive programme: support empowerment, a free media and broad coalitions, and have the patience to wait for a critical juncture. The authors admit there is no recipe.

Context

Where the argument is contested

Jared Diamond: one monocausal story for another

In the New York Review of Books (2012) Diamond accepted that institutions matter, then argued that the border pairs are the rare cases where geography is held constant, and that institutions themselves have geographic origins. The authors replied, and neither side moved. Read the Diamond summary alongside.

Jeffrey Sachs: selected history, and China

In Foreign Affairs (2012) Sachs argued that the book ignores technology, geography and public health as independent drivers, and misreads China’s record of poverty reduction. The China objection has kept its force.

Bill Gates: the terms are too vague

Gates’s 2013 review liked the stories and disliked the analysis: vague definitions, and no account of how growth actually happens through farm productivity, cities and industry.

Is it falsifiable?

Institutions are labelled inclusive partly by their results, and critical junctures are visible mostly afterwards. That gives the framework a lot of room after the fact. David Albouy’s 2012 critique of the settler-mortality data behind the authors’ best-known paper is the sharpest empirical challenge.

The gap the authors filled later

The 2012 book treats the virtuous circle as nearly self-sustaining. The Narrow Corridor (2019) recasts liberty as an unstable balance that must be maintained, and Power and Progress (2023) takes on technology that concentrates power. Read them as volume two.

“Growth will slow” is close to unfalsifiable, since convergence guarantees it. “Extractive institutions can’t innovate at the frontier” is a real prediction with a real counter-example building, and it is where a reader should push hardest.

Using it

Reading it

The book is repetitive by design, with the thesis restated at the start and end of most chapters, which makes it quick to read and easy to put down early.

  1. Chapters 1–3

    The machine: Nogales, the rival theories and the definitions.

  2. Chapters 4 and 7

    Critical junctures and 1688, the argument’s real weight.

  3. Chapters 11–13

    The two circles and the present day.

  4. Chapters 8–10

    The historical middle: rich in detail and safely skimmed.

  5. Chapter 15

    What the authors think should be done: the shortest and least satisfying.

Read alongside

  • Guns, Germs, and Steel, summarised on this site: the book Chapter 2 is written against.
  • The Causes of Portuguese Backwardness, summarised on this site: the same mechanism over four centuries of one country, with a correction on Iberia.
  • Titan, summarised on this site: the monopoly Chapter 11 uses as proof that an inclusive system can break its most powerful man.
  • Acemoglu & Robinson, The Narrow Corridor (2019): the sequel that revises the virtuous circle.
