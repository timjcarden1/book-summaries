Why Nations Fail

The Origins of Power, Prosperity, and Poverty

“Countries such as Great Britain and the United States became rich because their citizens overthrew the elites who controlled power and created a society where political rights were much more broadly distributed.”

The thesis, stated in the preface

Daron Acemoglu & James A. Robinson · 2012

The whole book in one breath

Two halves of one town, Nogales, share their people, food and climate, and one is three times richer than the other, because a border puts them under different institutions. Geography, culture and ignorance fail as explanations; poverty is chosen by those in power, because the reforms that would enrich a country would also create rivals. Inclusive economic institutions (secure property, open entry, a level field) need inclusive political ones (power spread widely, a state strong enough to keep order). Extractive institutions can grow for a while, as Stalin’s Russia and the Maya did, but they never allow creative destruction, and the prize of controlling them breeds war. Institutions drift apart slowly and diverge at critical junctures: the Black Death, the Atlantic trade, the Industrial Revolution. England’s broad coalition won in 1688; Venice closed its doors; Spain, Russia, Austria and the Ottomans blocked industry; Europe’s empires reversed development abroad. Good institutions sustain themselves through a virtuous circle, bad ones through a vicious one in which new rulers inherit the machine. Botswana, the American South and China after Mao broke out, which shows history is not destiny. Aid and expert advice cannot engineer prosperity; only the empowerment of a broad coalition can.

The authors

An economist and a political scientist, fifteen years in the making

Daron Acemoglu (born 1967 in Istanbul) is an economist at MIT. James A. Robinson (born 1960 in Britain) is a political scientist and economist, at Harvard when the book appeared and at the University of Chicago since 2015. The acknowledgments call the book “the culmination of fifteen years of collaborative research”, much of it with their long-term co-author Simon Johnson: papers such as “The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development” (2001), which used the death rates of early European settlers to trace how colonial institutions were set, and “Reversal of Fortune” (2002), which showed that the colonies richest in 1500 became the poorest. The maps are by Melissa Dell, whose 2010 study of the Potosí mita the book cites for the draft’s lasting effects (Chapter 1).

The book came out in March 2012, in the US from Crown and in Britain from Profile, while the Arab Spring was still unfolding. It is written for general readers, argued through stories rather than regressions, and repeats its thesis often. In 2024 Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson shared the Nobel Prize in economics “for studies of how institutions are formed and affect prosperity”.

What the preface says

It opens in Tahrir Square. The Arab Spring began when the street vendor Mohamed Bouazizi set himself alight in Tunisia on 17 December 2010; Ben Ali fell on 14 January 2011 and Mubarak on 11 February. The average Egyptian earns about 12% of the average American’s income, lives ten fewer years, and one in five is in dire poverty.

The protesters blamed corruption, repression and the lack of political rights. “We are living amid a corrupt system which has to change,” said Noha Hamed, a 24-year-old advertising worker. Mohamed ElBaradei tweeted on 13 January 2011: “Tunisia: repression + absence of social justice + denial of channels for peaceful change = a ticking bomb.” The first twelve demands posted by the blogger Wael Khalil were all political; the minimum wage came later.

Most experts blame Egypt’s deserts, its culture or its rulers’ ignorance. The authors side with the square: Egypt is poor because a narrow elite, Mubarak’s reported $70 billion fortune among its spoils, organised society for itself. Britain is richer because of 1688. Egypt’s own revolutions, against the Ottomans, the British and in 1952 the monarchy, just installed a new elite. Whether 2011 would be different depended, they wrote, on whether a broad movement could carry it, like the one a protester, Reda Metwaly, described in the square: “Now you see Muslims and Christians together, now you see old and young together, all wanting the same thing.”

If you remember nothing else

Twelve ideas the book stands on

Ch. 1–2

Institutions, not geography or culture

Nogales, the two Koreas and the reversal of fortune in the Americas hold geography and culture constant and still show vast gaps.

Ch. 2

Poverty is chosen

Rulers usually know which policies would bring growth. They refuse them because growth creates rivals: poor countries are poor “on purpose”.

Ch. 3

Inclusive or extractive

Inclusive economic institutions give most people secure property, open entry and a level field; extractive ones move income from the many to the few.

Ch. 3

Politics chooses the rules

Inclusive politics needs power spread widely and a state centralised enough to keep order. Somalia has the first without the second; North Korea the reverse.

Ch. 3, 7, 8

Creative destruction is the threat

Growth destroys old trades and old power. Elites from Elizabeth I to Francis I and Nicholas I blocked inventions to protect their position.

Ch. 5

Extractive growth is real but finite

Command can move labour into more productive uses, as in Stalin’s Russia, the sugar islands or Shyaam’s Kuba, but without innovation it stalls, and the prize invites war.

Ch. 4

Small differences, critical junctures

Societies drift apart slowly. A shock such as the Black Death or Atlantic trade then turns small differences into large ones.

Ch. 4, 6, 15

History is not destiny

England was a backwater and Venice nearly inclusive. Drift isn’t cumulative, and the Armada or Mao’s succession could have gone the other way.

Ch. 7, 12

The broad coalition

1688 worked because gentry, merchants and manufacturers each needed pluralism to stop the others dominating. Narrow winners just capture the machine.

Ch. 11

The virtuous circle

The rule of law, lower stakes in power and a free press made Britain widen its franchise and America break its trusts rather than repress.

Ch. 12–13

The vicious circle

Extraction pays for its own protection, and the iron law of oligarchy means new rulers, from Siaka Stevens to Mengistu, inherit and worsen the old machine.

Ch. 15

You can’t engineer prosperity

Advice, reform lists and aid fail when institutions are the cause. What works is the empowerment of a broad coalition, as in Brazil after 1978.

Chapters 1–5

The theory

Nogales and the Americas, the three rival theories, the definitions, how institutions change, and how extractive states can grow for a while.

Chapter 1

So Close and Yet So Different

One town split by a fence, and the colonial history that explains the fence: Spain found people to coerce, England found none, and the institutions each built are still there.

The economics of the Rio Grande

  • Nogales is cut in half by a fence. On the Arizona side the average household earns about $30,000 a year, most teenagers are in school, most adults finished high school, people live long, and they take roads, electricity, sewers, public health and law and order for granted. They also take for granted that the government is their agent: they can vote out the mayor, the congressman and the president.
  • A few feet south, in Nogales, Sonora, household income is about a third as high. Most adults lack a high-school diploma, infant mortality is high, roads are poor, crime is high, and opening a business means risking robbery and greasing palms. Democracy there is recent: until the reforms of 2000 the town, like all of Mexico, lived under the Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI).
  • Geography, climate and disease are the same on both sides, since germs cross the border freely. The people are the same too. The area was Mexican until the Gadsden Purchase of 1853 moved the border; the two towns share ancestors, food, music and, the authors “hazard to say”, culture.
  • The obvious difference is the border itself. The people of Nogales, Arizona, live under American economic institutions, which let them choose occupations, get schooling and encourage employers to invest in technology, and under political institutions that let them replace politicians who misbehave. That is why they get the services they want.

From Buenos Aires to Cajamarca

  • The divergence began when the colonies were founded. Juan Díaz de Solís claimed the Río de la Plata for Spain in 1516 and was clubbed to death by Charrúa hunter-gatherers. Pedro de Mendoza founded Buenos Aires in 1534, in a temperate climate that should have suited Europeans, but the Charrúa and Querandí would neither feed the Spaniards nor work for them, and there was no silver.
  • In 1537 Juan de Ayolas went up the Paraná and found the Guaraní, settled maize and cassava farmers. The Spanish defeated them, founded Asunción, married Guaraní princesses and put themselves at the top of the existing system of forced labour and tribute. Within four years Buenos Aires was abandoned; it was not resettled until 1580. This, the authors say, is the logic of the whole colonisation: the colonists wanted others to farm for them, and gold and silver to take.
  • Cortés perfected the method in Mexico: capture the indigenous ruler, claim his treasure, and take over his systems of tribute and forced labour. Moctezuma welcomed him at Tenochtitlan on 8 November 1519 and was seized. The chapter quotes Bernardino de Sahagún’s Florentine Codex on the Spaniards melting down the Aztec treasures.
  • After the conquest (complete by 1521) Cortés divided the most valuable resource, the people, through the encomienda: a grant of indigenous people to a Spaniard, who took their tribute and labour in return for converting them. Bartolomé de las Casas, who arrived on Hispaniola in 1502 and gave up his own encomienda, described it in A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies (1542), including the torture to death of the ruler Bogotá in New Granada for failing to fill a house with gold.
  • Pizarro repeated the method in Peru. At Cajamarca on 16 November 1532 the Spanish sprang a trap, killed perhaps two thousand of Atahualpa’s guards and retainers, and took him prisoner. He filled one room with gold and two with silver for his freedom; they strangled him in July 1533 and took Cusco that November.

The silver mountain and the mita

  • In 1545 the silver of the Cerro Rico was found at Potosí, which by 1650 had 160,000 people, more than Lisbon or Venice. To mine it, Viceroy Francisco de Toledo (arrived 1569) surveyed the whole adult population, moved almost everyone into new towns called reducciones, and revived the Inca labour draft, the mita (“a turn”). Across a catchment of about 200,000 square miles, one-seventh of adult men had to work in the mines. The Potosí mita lasted until 1825.
  • The legacy is visible today. Calca and Acomayo are both high Andean provinces of Quechua-speaking farmers, but Acomayo consumes about a third less, its road is barely passable, and its people farm for subsistence rather than the market. Acomayo lay inside the mita catchment; Calca did not.
  • Toledo added a head tax payable in silver (to push people into the labour market and cut wages), the repartimiento de mercancías (forced sales of goods at prices Spaniards set) and the trajín (people used as pack animals). The whole web was designed to push living standards down to subsistence and take everything above it. It made the Crown and the conquistadors rich and made Latin America the most unequal continent in the world.

…to Jamestown

  • England, weak after the Wars of the Roses, came late. It chose North America because it was all that was left. Arthur Young noted in the eighteenth century that the value of colonial staple exports fell with distance from the sun, from about £8 12s a head in the West Indies to 2s 6d in the northern colonies: “a most important lesson—to avoid colonizing in northern latitudes.”
  • After a failure at Roanoke (1585–87), the Virginia Company founded Jamestown on 14 May 1607 with the Spanish template in mind: find the local king and make his people feed and work for them. The king, Wahunsunacock, ruled the Powhatan Confederacy of some thirty polities from Werowocomoco, twenty miles away, but he lacked the Incas’ centralised control and refused to be coerced.
  • John Smith, a soldier of fortune who had been enslaved by the Ottomans and escaped, saw that the Virginia model could not work: the natives had no gold, and “Victuals you must know is all their wealth.” When the Company tried to crown Wahunsunacock as a vassal of James I, he replied that he too was a king and would not “bite at such a bait”, and imposed a trade embargo. Smith begged the Company to send carpenters, farmers and fishermen instead of goldsmiths, and ruled that “he that will not worke shall not eat.”
  • After Smith left in 1609 came the “starving time”: of about five hundred colonists who entered the winter of 1609–10, only sixty were alive by March, and some resorted to cannibalism.
  • The Company’s next idea was to coerce the colonists themselves. Sir Thomas Gates and Sir Thomas Dale imposed the “Lawes Divine, Morall and Martiall”: company-owned land, barracks, rations, work gangs, and death for running away to the Indians or stealing an ear of corn. It failed because the settlers could always run to the frontier. Population density in most of what became the United States was at most three-quarters of a person per square mile; in central Mexico and Andean Peru it was as high as four hundred.
  • So in 1618 the Company switched to incentives. The headright system gave each settler fifty acres, plus fifty for each family member and servant, freed settlers from their contracts, and in 1619 created a General Assembly that gave adult men a say in the colony’s laws: “the start of democracy in the United States.”
  • English elites kept trying to rebuild hierarchy. Lord Baltimore’s Maryland charter (1632) gave him “free, full, and absolute Power” to create a manorial society of lords and tenants. In Carolina, founded in 1663 by eight proprietors, the Fundamental Constitutions drafted by Anthony Ashley-Cooper and his secretary John Locke set out hereditary “leet-men” at the bottom and landgraves and caziques with 48,000 and 24,000 acres at the top, to “avoid erecting a numerous democracy.” Both failed: settlers demanded land and assemblies. Maryland became a Crown colony in 1691, South Carolina in 1729.
  • By the 1720s all thirteen colonies had a governor and an assembly elected by male property holders. They were not democracies, since women, slaves and the propertyless could not vote, but rights were broad for their time, and these assemblies became the First Continental Congress of 1774.

A tale of two constitutions

  • The Philadelphia convention of 1787 was the end of a process begun in Jamestown in 1619. Mexico’s path was the opposite. When Napoleon invaded Spain in 1808, a junta at besieged Cádiz wrote the Cádiz Constitution of 1812: popular sovereignty, the end of special privileges, equality before the law. Latin American elites, still living off the encomienda and forced labour, hated it.
  • The Hidalgo revolt of 1810, whose army killed indiscriminately when it sacked Guanajuato, united Mexico’s elites against popular participation. When a mutiny forced Ferdinand VII to restore the Cádiz Constitution in 1820, and the new Cortes proposed abolishing labour coercion and military courts, Mexico’s elites declared independence to escape it. Agustín de Iturbide’s Plan de Iguala (24 February 1821) dropped the threatening provisions; he then made himself emperor, “by the grace of God and of bayonets,” as Bolívar put it, and dissolved congress in 1822.
  • The US Constitution was no democracy either. It left the franchise to the states, counted a slave as three-fifths of a person for apportioning seats, and needed fudges like the Missouri Compromise until the Civil War settled the question. But it constrained power, and economic opportunity stayed broad.
  • Mexico had fifty years of near-constant instability. Antonio López de Santa Anna was president eleven times, lost Texas and the Mexican-American War, and between 1824 and 1867 Mexico had fifty-two presidents, few of whom took office by constitutional means. Property rights were insecure and the state was too weak to tax or even to control much of its territory. Alexander von Humboldt had called Mexico “the country of inequality”, and as the United States industrialised, Mexico got poorer.

An idea, a firm and a loan

  • Patents show who could innovate. In the United States between 1820 and 1845, only 19% of patentees came from professional or major landowning families, and 40% had primary schooling or less. Thomas Edison, the last of seven children of a man who split shingles and kept a tavern, is the type. He sold his Quadruplex telegraph to Western Union for $10,000 and held 1,093 US patents.
  • To exploit a patent you need a loan. The United States went from 338 banks with $160 million of assets in 1818 to 27,864 banks with $27.3 billion in 1914, and competition kept interest rates low. In 1910 Mexico had forty-two banks, two of which held 60% of assets, charged high rates and lent to the already rich.
  • The difference was political. Porfirio Díaz seized power in 1876 (the Battle of Tecoac) and ruled for thirty-four years, handing out land and monopolies, banking included, to supporters. Washington, Grant and Eisenhower were also generals, but none used force to take or keep power. Early American politicians tried to create state banking monopolies too, but voters could remove them, and the monopolies crumbled.

Path-dependent change

  • Late-nineteenth-century globalisation made the open frontiers valuable. In the United States a string of laws from the Land Ordinance of 1785 to the Homestead Act of 1862 gave broad access to frontier land (after indigenous peoples had been pushed aside). In Latin America frontier land went to the powerful.
  • Díaz opened Mexico to trade but on the model of Cortés and Toledo, not of the north. Between 1900 and 1910 perhaps thirty thousand Yaqui from Sonora, Nogales’s hinterland, were deported to work, essentially as slaves, on Yucatán’s henequen plantations. The authors call this “path-dependent” change: new, but the next stage of the same institutions.
  • The twentieth century brought revolutions (Mexico 1910, Bolivia 1952, Cuba 1959, Nicaragua 1979), civil wars, expropriations and dictatorships. The chapter gives the death tolls: 2,279 killed for political reasons under Pinochet (1973–90); 42,275 named victims in Guatemala, where some estimates reach 200,000 (1962–96); 9,000 in Argentina (1976–83), with human-rights groups usually saying 30,000. Most of Latin America became democratic only in the 1990s.

Making a billion or two

  • Bill Gates got rich by innovating, and even so the US Justice Department sued Microsoft for abusing its monopoly on 8 May 1998, and clipped its wings in a 2001 settlement.
  • Carlos Slim got rich through entry barriers. His consortium won the 1990 privatisation of Telmex without making the highest bid and paid for the shares partly out of Telmex’s own dividends. When the competition commission found in 1997 that Telmex had substantial monopoly power, Telmex used the amparo, a petition that a law does not apply to you, to escape. When Slim’s CompUSA broke a franchise contract in the United States, a Dallas jury fined it $454 million. “There are no amparos in Dallas.”

Toward a theory of world inequality

  • Even Nogales, Sonora, is richer than most of Mexico (average household income about $5,000), thanks to US-owned maquiladora plants. The average American is about seven times as prosperous as the average Mexican, more than ten times the average Peruvian or Central American, about twenty times the average sub-Saharan African and almost forty times the average person in Mali, Ethiopia or Sierra Leone.
  • The theory in outline: economic institutions shape incentives to learn, save, invest and innovate; politics determines the economic institutions; and political institutions, including the state’s capacity and who can act collectively, determine how politics works. Talent like Gates’s still needs institutions to turn it into a positive force.
  • There is no reason a society will adopt the institutions best for growth, because other institutions may be better for whoever holds power. Slim would not want his entry barriers to fizzle, however many Mexicans that enriched. So the book is about the economics of poverty and prosperity, and also their politics.

“The inhabitants of Nogales, Arizona, and Nogales, Sonora, share ancestors, enjoy the same food and the same music, and, we would hazard to say, have the same ‘culture.’”Chapter 1

Chapter 2

Theories That Don’t Work

The three standard explanations of world inequality, geography, culture and ignorance, each tested against the cases, and each found wanting.

The lay of the land

  • Sustained growth began in England in the second half of the eighteenth century and spread to western Europe, the United States and Britain’s settler colonies (Canada, Australia, New Zealand). The thirty richest countries today are those plus Japan, Singapore and South Korea. The thirty poorest are almost all in sub-Saharan Africa, joined by Afghanistan, Haiti and Nepal. Go back fifty, a hundred or a hundred and fifty years and the groups barely change.
  • The chapter’s map shows the world in 2008: below $2,000 per head in most of Africa, Afghanistan, Haiti, Cambodia, Laos and North Korea; above $20,000 in North America, western Europe, Australasia and Japan.
  • Within the Americas the ranking is stable too: the United States and Canada; then Chile, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico and Uruguay (and Venezuela, depending on oil); then Colombia, the Dominican Republic, Ecuador and Peru; and at the bottom Bolivia, Guatemala and Paraguay. It was the same fifty, a hundred and a hundred and fifty years ago.
  • In the Middle East the oil states (Saudi Arabia, Kuwait) approach the top thirty but would fall with the oil price; Egypt, Jordan and Syria sit near Guatemala and Peru.
  • But the pattern is not eternal. Most of today’s inequality opened up after the Industrial Revolution, and five hundred years ago the Americas were ranked the other way round. Argentina grew for five decades to 1920 and then slid; the Soviet Union grew fast from 1930 to 1970 and then collapsed. A theory has to explain the persistence and the reversals.

The geography hypothesis

  • The claim: poor countries lie between the tropics and rich ones in temperate zones. Montesquieu said hot climates made people lazy and incurious and suited to despots. The modern version, argued forcefully by Jeffrey Sachs, blames tropical disease (malaria above all) and thin tropical soils.
  • Nogales refutes it, as do North and South Korea and East and West Germany. So does history: in 1492 the tropical Aztec and Inca lands had centralised states, roads, famine relief, money and writing (the Incas recorded information on knotted quipus), while the temperate lands that became the United States, Canada, Argentina and Chile were mostly Stone Age societies. The authors call the change a reversal of fortune, and they say it came from the way the lands were colonised.
  • The same reversal happened in Asia (India and China once richer than Japan, Australia and New Zealand) and in Africa (the south was the most thinly settled region before Europeans; South Africa is now among the richest). Angkor, Vijayanagara, Aksum, Mohenjo-daro and Harappa all flourished in the tropics.
  • Disease is mostly a consequence of poverty and of governments that will not invest in public health. Nineteenth-century England was very unhealthy until the state built clean water and sewers; better health was a fruit of its political changes, not their cause. As for soils, low farm productivity in Africa comes mostly from land ownership and incentives, and in any case modern inequality came from the uneven spread of industry, not farming.

Jared Diamond’s version

  • Diamond (Guns, Germs, and Steel) traces intercontinental inequality in 1500 to the plants and animals each region could domesticate. The authors call it “a powerful approach to the puzzle on which he focuses”, but not an explanation of modern inequality.
  • Their test is Spain and Peru. In 1532 the average Spaniard was probably less than twice as rich as an Inca subject. Once the Andes had wheat, barley and horses, Diamond’s logic says the gap should have closed. Instead it widened: the average Spaniard is now more than six times richer than the average Peruvian, because Spain adopted steam, railways, electricity and factories and Peru did not.
  • Continental orientation (the east-west axis that Diamond takes from William McNeill) cannot explain Africa, whose coast the Portuguese reached when income gaps were small, nor why the Industrial Revolution happened in England rather than Moldova. And China and India, with Eurasia’s richest endowments, hold most of the world’s poor.
  • Diamond’s own maps make the point: wild pigs and cattle, and the wild ancestors of rice, wheat and barley, were spread across most of Eurasia, so they cannot explain inequality within it. The Middle East led the Neolithic Revolution and smelted the first iron; what made it poor was the Ottoman Empire and its institutional legacy.

The culture hypothesis

  • It goes back to Max Weber’s Protestant ethic, and survives in the unspoken beliefs that Africans lack a work ethic, that Latin Americans suffer from a “mañana” culture, and (once) that Confucian values blocked growth, a claim now reversed to explain Chinese success.
  • The authors answer “yes and no”. Social norms matter and can be hard to change, and they can support institutions. But religion and national character don’t explain the pattern, and trust and cooperation, which do matter, are mostly outcomes of institutions. Mexicans say in surveys that they trust others less than Americans do; no surprise when the state cannot suppress the cartels or run fair courts.
  • Korea. Before the division at the 38th parallel the peninsula had unusual homogeneity of language, ethnicity and culture. Whatever cultural differences exist now are consequences of the border, not causes.
  • Kongo. The Portuguese reached the Kingdom of Kongo in 1483. Its capital Mbanza had about sixty thousand people, as many as Lisbon and more than London’s fifty thousand. King Nzinga a Nkuwu became João I. The Portuguese sent agricultural missions in 1491 and 1512 to spread the plough and the wheel, and both failed. But the Kongolese adopted the gun at once, to capture and export slaves, and later took up literacy, dress and house styles. They had no incentive to raise farm output that an all-powerful king could seize, and their own lives hung by a thread.
  • In the nineteenth century West Africans built booming exports of palm oil and groundnuts, and southern Africans supplied the Rand. These experiments were destroyed by colonialism and then by post-independence governments, not by African culture.
  • Religion fails as an explanation too. Catholic France soon matched Protestant England and the Netherlands, Italy is as rich as either, and East Asia’s successes owe nothing to Christianity. The Middle East’s poverty tracks Ottoman rule, then British and French colonialism, then authoritarian regimes; the link with Islam is “largely spurious”. Muhammad Ali’s Egypt (1805–48), briefly free of Ottoman and European control, modernised and grew until he died.
  • “English culture” fails because Sierra Leone and Nigeria were English colonies too. “European culture” fails because Argentina and Uruguay have more people of European descent than the United States or Canada, while Japan and Singapore have almost none. China was poor under Mao because of the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution, and grows now because of Deng Xiaoping’s reforms, not because its culture changed. Colombia, Ecuador and Peru have similar incomes but very different indigenous shares.

The ignorance hypothesis

  • This is the view most economists hold. It follows from Lionel Robbins’s 1935 definition of economics as the allocation of scarce means between ends: poor countries have market failures their rulers don’t know how to fix, and good advice would fix them.
  • Ghana under Nkrumah. The adviser Tony Killick described a shoe factory whose hides would have travelled over 500 miles south to a tannery and 200 miles back north to Kumasi, before the shoes went 200 miles south again to the market in Accra, and a mango cannery in a region that grew no mangos, sized to produce more than world demand. Nkrumah was not ignorant: he had Killick and the Nobel laureate Arthur Lewis telling him the policies were bad. He used the projects to buy political support.
  • Ghana under Busia. Kofi Busia, a democratic prime minister from 1969, kept price controls through marketing boards and an overvalued currency because cheap food kept the cities content. When he signed an IMF agreement with a large devaluation on 27 December 1971, riots in Accra followed and the army under Lieutenant Colonel Acheampong overthrew him and reversed it. Busia knew the gamble; the international institutions were “blissfully unaware”.
  • John Smith and Cortés did not differ in knowledge, nor Theodore Roosevelt and Wilson from Porfirio Díaz. They differed in the constraints they faced. And China changed course not because the Party learned economics but because Deng and his allies won a political fight inside it.
  • The chapter’s conclusion is the book’s central claim: poor countries are poor because those who have power make choices that create poverty, “not by mistake or ignorance but on purpose.” To understand that, economics needs politics. Abba Lerner put it in the 1970s: “Economics has gained the title Queen of the Social Sciences by choosing solved political problems as its domain.”

“Poor countries are poor because those who have power make choices that create poverty. They get it wrong not by mistake or ignorance but on purpose.”Chapter 2

Chapter 3

The Making of Prosperity and Poverty

The book’s vocabulary: inclusive and extractive institutions, economic and political, the state that both need, and why rulers so often choose poverty.

The economics of the 38th parallel

  • Within a month of Japan’s surrender on 15 August 1945 Korea was split at the 38th parallel, the South under the United States and the North under the Soviet Union. When the North’s army retreated in 1950 it took a doctor from Seoul, Hwang Pyong-Won’s brother, with it. The brothers met again in Seoul in 2000. The brother, an air-force doctor with a privileged job, had no car and no telephone, refused money because the government would take it, and could not swap his threadbare coat because it was borrowed from the government for the trip. He looked “thin as a rake”.
  • South Korea now lives like Portugal or Spain; North Korea like sub-Saharan Africa, at about a tenth of the South’s level, and North Koreans live about ten years less. Satellite photographs at night show the North almost completely dark.
  • The gap did not exist before 1945. Syngman Rhee (elected 1948) and General Park Chung-Hee were authoritarian, but they ran market economies with private property, and after 1961 Park threw the state behind fast growth. Kim Il-Sung, dictator by 1947, built the Juche command economy with Soviet help, banned private property and markets, and presided over collapsing farm productivity and recurring famine. By the late 1990s the gap was tenfold. Neither culture nor geography nor ignorance can explain it.

Extractive and inclusive economic institutions

  • A North Korean teenager expects propaganda for schooling, ten years in the army, no property, no business and only black markets. A South Korean teenager expects good schooling and the chance to enjoy the fruits of effort. Those different rules are the institutions.
  • Inclusive economic institutions let the great mass of people take part in economic activity and choose what to do. They need secure private property, an unbiased legal system, public services that give a level playing field, free entry for new businesses and free choice of career.
  • Property rights must be broad. The Barbados census of 1680 counted about 60,000 people, almost 39,000 of them African slaves owned mostly by the 175 largest sugar planters. Their property rights were perfectly secure, since twenty-nine of the island’s forty judges and justices of the peace were large planters. That was not inclusive: two-thirds of the population had no rights at all.
  • Inclusive institutions need and use the state, as the enforcer of law, property and contracts and as a provider of roads and basic regulation.
  • Extractive economic institutions have the opposite properties. They are called extractive “because such institutions are designed to extract incomes and wealth from one subset of society to benefit a different subset.” North Korea and the mita, encomienda and repartimiento are the examples.

Engines of prosperity

  • Inclusive institutions create inclusive markets, which are more than free markets: seventeenth-century Barbados had markets, including markets in slaves. Inclusive markets let people move to where they are most productive and let better firms replace worse ones.
  • They also power two further engines: technology and education. It was the United States, not Mexico or Peru, that produced Edison, and South Korea, not the North, that produced Samsung and Hyundai. A society where teachers don’t show up and there are no books, like the Congo or Haiti, wastes its potential Bill Gateses “and perhaps one or two Albert Einsteins” as coerced, uneducated farmers.

Extractive and inclusive political institutions

  • Politics is the process by which a society chooses its rules. Because some groups (North Korea’s party elite, Barbados’s planters) do better under extractive institutions, the outcome depends on who wins the political contest, which depends on the distribution of political power.
  • Political institutions that concentrate power narrowly and without constraint are absolutist; those that distribute it broadly and constrain it are pluralistic.
  • Pluralism is not enough: the state must also be centralised. Somalia’s power is widely distributed, almost pluralistic, among clans that cannot dominate one another, and the result is chaos. The authors use Max Weber’s definition of the state as the “monopoly of legitimate violence”.
  • Their definition: political institutions that are sufficiently centralised and pluralistic are inclusive; when either condition fails they are extractive (see the figure below).
  • The two kinds of institution reinforce each other. Extractive politics lets an elite design extractive economics; the wealth that brings pays for the armies that protect its power. And when a new group overthrows the elite, it faces few constraints either, so it keeps the machine, as Porfirio Díaz did. Inclusive politics, conversely, makes it hard for anyone to capture the state.
  • Mixtures are unstable. The Virginia Company could not have offered land in 1618 without the assembly of 1619, because settlers would not have trusted economic rights without political ones. Inclusive economics under extractive politics will either be turned extractive or will destabilise the regime.

Why not always choose prosperity?

  • Wouldn’t even a predatory dictator want a richer country to plunder? Mobutu ruled Congo from 1965 to 1997, impoverished it, and grew fabulously rich with his circle, “Les Grosses Légumes” (the Big Vegetables). He built a palace at Gbadolite with an airport long enough for the Concorde he rented from Air France, and bought castles and much of Brussels. Would he not have been richer still, with his own Concorde, if Congo had prospered? The answer, “unfortunately for the citizens of many countries”, is no.
  • The reason is Joseph Schumpeter’s creative destruction. Growth replaces old sectors, firms, skills and machines, and so creates political as well as economic losers. Europe’s landed aristocracies, living off rents and royal monopolies, stood to lose both income and power from industry. Artisans lost too: John Kay, inventor of the flying shuttle (1733), had his house burned down in 1753, and James Hargreaves of the spinning jenny was treated the same way.
  • The Luddites lacked political power and lost; industrialisation went ahead in England because aristocratic opposition was muted. In Austria-Hungary and Russia, where monarchs and aristocrats had much more to lose, it was blocked, and those economies fell behind.
  • The same logic applies to political institutions: an absolutist elite will not voluntarily share power, so pluralism has to be forced on it. And centralisation doesn’t come naturally either. In Somalia any clan that built a state would be centralising power in its own hands, so the others block it. Centralisation happens only when one group is strong enough to build a state.

The long agony of the Congo

  • Portuguese and Dutch visitors to Kongo in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries saw “miserable poverty”: no writing, wheel or plough. The elite lived on slave plantations around the capital and arbitrary taxes (one was levied every time the king’s beret fell off). Farmers moved their villages away from roads to escape plunder and slave raiders.
  • The king kept a standing army of five thousand, with a core of five hundred musketeers, by the mid-seventeenth century, which explains his enthusiasm for European guns. Even if he had abolished slavery, who would have believed him while he commanded the musketeers? Only a change in political power would have made reform credible, and that would have made the king and aristocracy losers.
  • Leopold II’s colony made rights even less secure, and independence in 1960 reproduced the pattern. Mobutu expropriated foreign businesses in the Zairianization of 1973 but never controlled much of the country, and needed foreign help to stop Katanga and Kasai seceding. Rival groups fighting to capture the extractive state destroyed what centralisation there was, as across much of sub-Saharan Africa.

Growth under extractive political institutions

  • Extractive institutions can generate growth in two ways. First, the elite can direct resources into high-productivity activities it controls: the sugar islands of Barbados, Cuba, Haiti and Jamaica were among the richest places in the world in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and the Soviet Union grew fast from the first Five-Year Plan in 1928 until the 1970s by moving labour from farms to industry.
  • Second, an elite secure in its power may allow somewhat inclusive economic institutions. South Korea under Park (who took power in a 1961 coup) is the case. By the 1970s its economic institutions were inclusive enough, and incomes equal enough, that the elite had little to fear from democracy; with US pressure and a strong democracy movement, Park’s assassination in 1979 and Chun Doo-hwan’s coup were followed by Roh Tae-woo’s reforms and a pluralistic democracy after 1992.
  • China resembles both. Its early growth came from radical farm reforms; industry reform has been more muted, and the Party still decides which sectors and firms get capital. It is growth under extractive institutions, and a South Korean-style transition is “less likely, though of course not impossible.”
  • Both kinds need political centralisation, which is why much of sub-Saharan Africa can’t manage even limited growth. And both are limited. Without creative destruction the state can allocate resources “by fiat” only so long; the Soviets briefly led in space and nuclear weapons, then stalled in the 1970s. Such regimes also invite infighting to capture the state, as in the later Roman Empire and the Maya cities, and an elite with power will eventually use it to limit competition and grab a bigger share.

Chapter 4

Small Differences and Critical Junctures: The Weight of History

How institutions change: slow drift creates small differences, and a big shock, a critical junction in history, turns them into large ones.

The world the plague created

  • In 1346 the Black Death reached Tana, at the mouth of the Don, from China along the Silk Road. Genoese traders carried it on; it reached Constantinople by early 1347 and France, North Africa and Italy in spring 1348, killing about half the people wherever it struck. Boccaccio described its swellings “the size of a common apple” and death within three days; Bishop Ralph of Shrewsbury ordered prayers in England. England lost about half its population too.
  • Europe’s feudal order was highly extractive: the king granted land to lords for military service, and lords granted plots to serfs, tied to the land, who owed unpaid labour, fines and taxes to a lord who was also their judge and police.
  • The labour shortage shook it. At Eynsham Abbey, where “scarcely two tenants remained” after 1349, the peasants threatened to leave unless the abbot cut their fines and labour, and he agreed. The Crown fought back with the Statute of Laborers (1351), which froze wages at pre-plague levels and made leaving an employer a crime punishable by prison.
  • It failed. In the Peasants’ Revolt of 1381 Wat Tyler’s rebels took most of London. Tyler was executed, but no one tried to enforce the statute again. Feudal labour services dwindled, an inclusive labour market began to emerge, and wages rose.
  • In eastern Europe the same shortage had the opposite effect. Lords there were slightly better organised, held larger and more consolidated estates, and faced weaker towns and less organised peasants. They tightened control. After 1500, western demand for grain made it worse: 80% of Amsterdam’s rye came from the Elbe, Vistula and Oder valleys, and half of the Dutch trade was with the east.
  • This was the Second Serfdom. In Korczyn, Poland, all work for the lord was paid in 1533; by 1600 nearly half was unpaid. In Mecklenburg, a few days’ unpaid labour a year in 1500 became one day a week by 1550 and three by 1600. In Hungary, where lords took full control of the land in 1514, it went from one day a week to three by 1600, and serfs made up 90% of the rural population.
  • In 1346 the two halves of Europe differed little; by 1600 they were “worlds apart”, and a map of serfdom in 1800 shows the east dark and the west light.

Critical junctures

  • The authors define a critical juncture as “a major event or confluence of factors disrupting the existing economic or political balance in society.” It is double-edged: it can break the cycle of extraction, as in England, or intensify it, as in the east.
  • Some junctures hit one country (Mao’s death in 1976 for China); others hit many at once (colonisation, then decolonisation). They matter because ordinary gradual reform is blocked by the vicious circle: those who gain from the status quo are rich and organised.

The making of inclusive institutions

  • England’s institutions came from conflict, not consensus, culminating in the Civil War (1642–51) and above all the Glorious Revolution of 1688. It moved the power to set economic institutions from the king to Parliament, opened the political system to a broad cross-section of society, and built on an already centralising state: “the world’s first set of inclusive political institutions.”
  • Economic institutions followed. Before 1688 England had no serfdom but plenty of monopolies, arbitrary taxation, a manipulated legal system and land in archaic tenures that could not be sold. After it, the state enforced property rights (patents included), applied the law to all, ended arbitrary taxation, abolished monopolies almost completely, used the navy to protect trade, and rationalised property so roads, canals and railways could be built.
  • Watt, Trevithick, Arkwright and Brunel could all count on markets and property. In 1775 James Watt wrote to his father that “after a series of various and violent Oppositions” he had got an Act of Parliament vesting his “Fire engines” in him for twenty-five years, “as there is already considerable demand for them.” The letter shows both the market motive and a Parliament that individual innovators could lobby.
  • Two things made England different: a centralised state (which France and Spain also had), and, more important, a broad coalition able to put durable limits on the monarchy.

Small differences that matter

  • In 1588 England, Spain and France looked alike: relatively absolutist monarchs (Elizabeth I, Philip II and, in France, Henry III; the book says Henry II) facing assemblies that wanted more power. But the English Parliament and the Spanish Cortes controlled taxation and the French Estates-General did not, and after 1492 the Spanish Crown had American silver and didn’t need the Cortes. Elizabeth had to ask Parliament for money, and it demanded limits on her power to grant monopolies.
  • Then Atlantic trade exploded after 1600: the English East India Company (1600), the Dutch one (1602), Jamestown (1607), Quebec (1608), Barbados (1627). The Spanish and French crowns monopolised their share; Elizabeth and her successors couldn’t. So England grew a large class of rich merchants with few ties to the Crown, who resented royal control and played a critical role in the Civil War and 1688. France had its own revolt, the Fronde (1648–52), but its opponents of absolutism were fewer and poorer.
  • Some differences were never small. Poland-Lithuania was ruled by the szlachta, a nobility powerful enough to elect its kings, over a society of serfs; Peter the Great’s Russia was more absolutist than Louis XIV’s France.
  • Where do the small differences come from? From institutional drift. Even hunter-gatherers such as the San of Botswana have institutions, and no two societies make the same ones. Like genetic drift between isolated populations, small random differences accumulate, with no set direction, and they decide how each society reacts when a juncture arrives.

The contingent path of history

  • History weighs on the outcome but does not fix it. In 1588 England had no real navy, only privateers, and the Armada under the Duke of Medina Sidonia, appointed at the last minute when a more experienced commander died, was expected to win. Bad weather and his mistakes lost it, and the Atlantic opened to England on more equal terms. Without that unlikely victory the juncture that produced 1688 would never have got moving.
  • Junctures can also go badly. Robert Michels’s iron law of oligarchy, revolutionaries replacing one tyranny with another, describes most of post-colonial Africa and much of Asia; Botswana is the rare exception. And inclusive institutions can reverse, as Venice’s did (Chapter 6).

Understanding the lay of the land

  • Settler colonies (the United States, Canada, Australia) built institutions close to England’s by a different route, and industrialised quickly. Western Europe was close enough to follow, and the French Revolution, another juncture, pulled it nearer while eastern Europe diverged further.
  • Latin America kept the conquistadors’ institutions. Argentina and Chile did better because, with few indigenous people or minerals, Spain neglected them; the poorest part of Argentina is the northwest, the only part integrated into the colonial economy.
  • Africa formed centralised states late and weakly, and they were usually absolutist and short-lived. The Atlantic slave trade turned Kongo’s absolutism into mass enslavement for guns and luxuries, and across much of Africa it reversed state-building. Colonialism then crushed nascent modernisation, and independence handed the machinery to new rulers. Afghanistan, Haiti and Nepal share the same lack of centralisation.
  • Botswana is the exception, thanks to King Khama’s nineteenth-century reforms, chiefs who outwitted colonial rule, and contingency: Seretse Khama married Ruth Williams, a white Englishwoman, in the 1940s, was banned from the protectorate under pressure from apartheid South Africa, gave up his kingship, and returned to lead the country with no interest in personal wealth.
  • Asia. Ming China turned away from the oceans in the late fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries for fear of creative destruction; India’s caste system was far more rigid than Europe’s occupational names (Baker, Cooper, Smith) and underpinned Mughal absolutism, before British rule made it an extractive colony. China lost the Opium Wars (1839–42 and 1856–60).
  • Japan faced the same shock when Commodore Perry’s four warships entered Edo Bay in July 1853. But the Tokugawa shogunate, in power since 1600, had only a tenuous hold over the other great domains, whose leaders could challenge it; China had no equivalent. The result was the Meiji Restoration, and later South Korea, Taiwan and China followed a similar path from stagnation to fast growth.
  • The Middle East was shaped by the Ottomans, who took Constantinople in 1453 and by Suleiman’s death in 1566 ruled from Tunisia to Iraq. All land formally belonged to the state; taxes were farmed out to private collectors who took half to two-thirds of the harvest; in Palestine peasants fled the fertile land for the hills from the late sixteenth century to escape bandits. Reform from the 1840s stalled, and after 1918 European colonialism and then independent elites (some, like Jordan’s monarchy, colonial creations) inherited the machine. Spared the slave trade and closer to European technology, the region is not as poor as Africa, but most of its people are still poor.

Chapter 5

“I’ve Seen the Future, and It Works”: Growth Under Extractive Institutions

Extractive states can grow, sometimes spectacularly, by ordering resources into productive uses. Stalin, King Shyaam, the first farmers and the Maya show how, and why it never lasts.

I’ve seen the future

  • In 1919 William Bullitt and the muckraking journalist Lincoln Steffens went to Moscow to sound out Lenin. Back in Paris, Steffens told the financier Bernard Baruch, “I have been over into the future, and it works,” later polished into the famous line. Westerners kept seeing the future there until the early 1980s.
  • Stalin, in control by 1927, industrialised by command through Gosplan (founded 1921) and the first Five-Year Plan (1928–33). With no working tax system, he collectivised agriculture to seize its output. Farm production fell, and probably six million people died of famine, with hundreds of thousands more murdered or deported.
  • Yet national income grew about 6% a year from 1928 to 1960, “probably the most rapid spurt of economic growth in history up until then.” The reason: moving labour from primitive farming into industry, and building factories, raised output even when done inefficiently and by force. Industry played the role sugar had played on Barbados.
  • It fooled nearly everyone: the CIA; Khrushchev, who told Western diplomats in 1956 “we will bury you”; a 1977 British textbook that ranked Soviet economies ahead of capitalist ones on everything except political freedom; and Paul Samuelson’s bestselling textbook, which in 1961 said Soviet income might overtake America’s by 1984 and probably by 1997, and in 1980 moved the dates to 2002 and 2012.
  • By the 1970s growth had all but stopped. Once the badly used resources had been moved, there was little left to gain by fiat. Innovation survived only in the military and space programmes (the dog Laika, Yuri Gagarin, and the AK-47).

How the plan really worked

  • The plans were constantly rewritten. All were labelled “draft” or “preliminary”; only one “final” plan, for light industry in 1939, has ever surfaced. Stalin wanted discretion to reward the loyal, and Gosplan’s real job was to supply him with information. Officials avoided decisions, because a wrong one could get you shot.
  • The 1937 census counted about 162 million people, short of the 168 million Stalin had announced in 1934 and the 180 million he expected, because it recorded the famines and purges. He had the organisers shot or sent to Siberia. The 1939 census “got it right”: 171 million.
  • Stalin denounced “equality mongering” in 1931 and brought in bonuses of up to 37% of salary for meeting output targets. They discouraged innovation: new methods risked this month’s target, and beating a target only raised the next one. Targets in tons made steel sheet too heavy; targets in area made it too thin; chandeliers planned by weight could barely hang from ceilings.
  • Profit-based bonuses (prices were set by the state and meant little), innovation bonuses (from 1946, proportional to productivity only from 1956, and capped by the wage bill, which discouraged labour-saving ideas) all failed for the same reason.
  • The sticks were bigger. A law of June 1940 made twenty minutes’ absence or idling a crime punishable by six months’ hard labour and a 25% pay cut. Between 1940 and 1955, 36 million people, about a third of adults, were convicted of labour offences; 15 million were imprisoned and 250,000 shot, besides the 2.5 million Stalin sent to the gulags. “You cannot force people to think and have good ideas by threatening to shoot them.”
  • Real incentives would have meant giving up extractive institutions and with them the Party’s power. When Gorbachev began to do that after 1987, the Party’s power crumbled, and the Soviet Union with it.

On the banks of the Kasai

  • The Lele on the west bank of the Kasai and the Bushong on the east share an origin, related languages and building styles. When Mary Douglas and Jan Vansina studied them in the 1950s, Douglas wrote: “The Lele are poor, while the Bushong are rich . . . Everything that the Lele have or can do, the Bushong have more and can do better.”
  • The Lele farmed for subsistence, hunted without nets and got one maize harvest a year from fortified villages constantly at war, where going into the forest risked kidnap. The Bushong traded in markets and ran a five-crop, two-year rotation (yams, sweet potatoes, cassava, beans) with two or three maize harvests a year. Not geography, not ignorance (the Lele knew the tools), and not culture: the Lele eagerly bought guns.
  • The difference was a political revolution. Around 1620 a man called Shyaam founded the Kuba Kingdom with the Bushong at its core: a bureaucracy to tax, courts and police, councils that leaders had to consult, even trial by jury, apparently unique in pre-colonial sub-Saharan Africa. It was still absolutist and extractive; no one voted for Shyaam.
  • Order brought an economic revolution: American crops (maize, cassava, chilli), the intensive rotation, food per head doubled, and the marriage age cut to twenty to put young men in the fields. Lele men married at about thirty-five and spent their youth fighting and raiding. Shyaam, like Stalin, needed a surplus to extract. And as in the Soviet Union, there was no further innovation: the Belgians found the kingdom much the same in the late nineteenth century.
  • Why the Bushong and not the Lele? Probably contingency: nothing about geography, culture or ignorance stopped the Lele having a Shyaam.

The long summer

  • The Ice Age ended around 15,000 BC; Greenland ice cores suggest a rise of up to fifteen degrees Celsius. After the cold snap of the Younger Dryas, temperatures jumped about seven degrees in under a decade after 9600 BC and have stayed high, the “Long Summer”, in the archaeologist Brian Fagan’s phrase. It was the critical juncture behind the Neolithic Revolution.
  • The first domesticated plants appear around 9500 BC in the Hilly Flanks, from Israel to western Iran: emmer and barley at Jericho (a village of perhaps five hundred), emmer, peas and lentils at Tell Aswad in Syria, both Natufian sites.
  • The Natufians settled before they farmed. Gazelle teeth, whose layers show the season of death, reveal year-round hunting at their sites. Abu Hureyra on the Euphrates, settled around 9500 BC by 100–300 people, stayed a hunter-gatherer village for another five hundred years.
  • Settling needs rules for land, trees and fishing, and someone to enforce them. Natufian graves show inequality: some people buried with obsidian and shells from near Mount Carmel, others with nothing. At Ain Mallaha, near the Sea of Galilee, a large plastered building beside a central space among about fifty huts was almost certainly a chief’s house, and skull cults point to inherited status.
  • So institutions came first. Settled life did not require farming, and more productive tools don’t always mean more work: among the Yir Yoront of Australia the steel axe led to more sleep. The authors argue that Diamond’s account, in which plentiful domesticable species led to farming and then to hierarchy, “puts the cart before the horse”. Once farming began, villages grew two to six times larger, and it spread into Europe from about 6500 BC with migrating farmers.
  • It did not make the Natufians’ homelands rich. Syria and Palestine are relatively poor; Israel’s prosperity was largely imported by post-war Jewish settlement. It was growth under extractive institutions, and it bred conflict over who would control them.

The unstable extraction

  • Maya cities arose around 500 BC, failed in the first century AD, and revived under a new political model in the Classic Era (AD 250–900). About fifty city-states, recognisable by their own glyphs, spoke around thirty-one related languages, left at least fifteen thousand inscriptions, invented cement, and dated events by the Long Count, starting in 3114 BC.
  • Dated monuments trace the arc: ten in AD 514, twenty by 672, forty a year by the mid-700s, ten a year in the 800s and none in the 900s. At Copán, Altar Q lists sixteen kings from K’inich Yax K’uk’ Mo’ (AD 426), including “18 Rabbit”, “Smoke Monkey” and “Smoke Shell” (died 763), down to Yax Pasaj, after whom there was only a probable pretender.
  • The model, from around AD 100 at Tikal, was a divine lord (k’uhul ajaw) over a hierarchy of aristocrats, raising tribute and labour for monuments. It brought stability and specialisation (potters, weavers, toolmakers) and long-distance trade in obsidian, jaguar pelts, shells, cacao, salt and feathers; cacao beans probably served as money. After about AD 300 there was little further innovation.
  • Instead there was war, recorded by the “star wars” glyph and timed to Venus. Tikal, Calakmul, Copán and Palenque fought for dominance and reduced smaller cities to vassals.
  • The collapse coincided with the overthrow of the divine lords. At Copán the palaces emptied after Yax Pasaj died in AD 810; at Quiriguá the last dated monument is also from 810. In the Petexbatún region temples were torn down to build defensive walls. The authors think rebellions, perhaps by elite factions, overthrew the system: extraction creates wealth worth fighting over.

What goes wrong?

  • Extractive institutions are common because they have a powerful logic: with some centralisation they generate limited prosperity and put it in a few hands. That motive drove Shyaam, the Natufians and the Maya kings, as well as the Caribbean planters and the Communist Party.
  • They fail in two ways. They cannot produce creative destruction, so growth stops when catch-up and reallocation run out, as in the Soviet Union by the 1970s. And the wealth they concentrate invites others to fight for it, which can reverse centralisation altogether, as with the Maya.
  • The chapter ends on China: like the Soviet Union in its day, it mesmerises observers, but under the Communist Party it is another case of growth under extractive institutions, unlikely to be sustained without a fundamental political transformation (Chapter 15).

Chapters 6–10

The history

Two thousand years through the theory: Venice and Rome closing, England opening in 1688, absolutists blocking industry, empires reversing development, and the other roads to inclusive institutions.

Chapter 6

Drifting Apart

Venice and Rome both built partly inclusive institutions and then closed them; England, a Roman backwater that collapsed completely, was where the breakthrough later came. History drifts; it doesn’t accumulate.

How Venice became a museum

  • Venice, independent from AD 810, was possibly the richest place in the medieval world. Its population went from 45,000 in 1050 to 70,000 in 1200 and 110,000 in 1330, as big as Paris and probably three times London.
  • Its key innovation was the commenda, a partnership for a single voyage between a “sedentary” partner who stayed home with the capital and a travelling partner who went with the cargo. In the unilateral form the sedentary partner put up all the capital and took 75% of the profit; in the bilateral form he put up two-thirds and took half. It let young men without money into trade, and the records show it: new names made up 69%, 81% and 65% of those in government documents of 960, 971 and 982.
  • New wealth opened politics. From 1032 the doge, elected for life, was checked by a Ducal Council; the first doge so hemmed in, Domenico Flabianico, was a silk merchant from a family new to high office. In 1082 Venice won trading privileges in Constantinople, where its quarter soon held ten thousand Venetians.
  • After a doge was murdered in 1171, a Great Council became the ultimate authority, with a hundred new members nominated each year by four electors chosen by lot; the doge was effectively chosen by the council and bound by an oath. Independent magistrates, a court of appeal, contract and bankruptcy law and the beginnings of modern banking followed. “The dynamic moving Venice toward fully inclusive institutions looked unstoppable.”
  • But every new commenda fortune cut into the old families’ profits and power. On 3 October 1286 a proposal to give the Council of Forty, which the old families controlled, a veto over new members was defeated; on 5 October a version passed giving automatic entry to men whose fathers and grandfathers had sat. In February 1297 came La Serrata, “the Closure”: anyone who had sat in the previous four years was automatically in, and after 11 September 1298 sitting families needed no confirmation at all. The Libro d’Oro, the “Golden Book” of the nobility, sealed it in 1315.
  • Outsiders resisted. The council co-opted opponents by growing from 450 to 1,500 members, and repressed the rest: Venice got its first police force in 1310.
  • The political closure was followed by an economic one. The commenda was banned. From 1314 the state nationalised trade with state galleys, and from 1324 taxed private traders heavily, so long-distance trade became a noble preserve. By 1500 the population had fallen to 100,000, and it shrank again between 1650 and 1800 while Europe’s grew. Venice, “on the brink of becoming the world’s first inclusive society”, fell to a coup, and today lives on tourists who come to see its pre-Serrata wonders.

Two lessons from Venice

  • Moves toward inclusive institutions can be reversed, so there is no simple cumulative improvement. And the small differences that matter at critical junctures are ephemeral: they can appear, vanish and reappear.
  • England shows it. It was a backwater after the Neolithic Revolution, marginal to Rome, and the part of the Western Empire that collapsed most completely in the fifth century. Yet the political revolutions that led to industry happened there, not in Italy or Turkey.

Roman virtues…

  • In 133 BC the tribune Tiberius Gracchus was clubbed to death by senators led by his own cousin, Publius Cornelius Scipio Nasica, and thrown into the Tiber. He came from the best families (Aemilius Paullus, Scipio Africanus); his offence was taking the side of the plebeians on land.
  • The Republic, founded when Tarquin the Proud was overthrown around 510 BC, had many inclusive elements: magistrates elected for a year, several at a time; checks and balances; and a Plebeian Assembly electing tribunes who could veto magistrates and propose laws, rights won through “secessions”, strikes in which soldiers withdrew to a hill outside the city. Slaves, perhaps a third of Italy’s people, had no rights.
  • There was real growth. Dated shipwrecks go from about 20 around 500 BC (probably not Roman) to a peak of 180 around the birth of Christ. Monte Testaccio in Rome is made of about fifty-three million discarded amphorae. The Greenland Ice Core Project (drilled 1990–92 through 3,030 metres of ice) shows lead, silver and copper pollution rising from about 500 BC to a peak in the first century AD not matched again until the thirteenth century.
  • But about two-thirds of the cargo was state property, taxes and tribute or free grain and oil for the city: the fruits of extraction built Monte Testaccio. Real power lay with the Senate and its landowners.
  • Conquest enriched the senatorial families while long campaigns ruined the citizen-soldiers’ farms, which senators absorbed and worked with slaves. Tiberius Gracchus proposed enforcing an old limit of 300 acres of public land and giving the excess to landless citizens. When he claimed the bequest of the king of Pergamum for his land commission and stood for re-election, the senators accused him of wanting to be king. His brother Gaius was murdered too.
  • The conflict ran on through the Social War (91–87 BC), Sulla’s curbs on the tribunes and Caesar’s popularity, until Caesar crossed the Rubicon in 49 BC. After his murder in 44 BC and the civil wars, Octavian won at Actium in 31 BC and ruled alone for forty-five years as Augustus, calling his regime the Principate.

…Roman vices

  • Flavius Aetius, Gibbon’s “last of the Romans”, was the most powerful man in the empire from 433 to 454, when Valentinian III murdered him. By then civil wars and coups came nearly every decade from Marcus Aurelius’s death in 180 to the fall in 476, and barbarians were allies one year and invaders the next: Alaric was a senior Roman general in 405 and sacked Rome in 410 (the book says 408). Rome offered the Vandal king Geiseric, conqueror of North Africa from 429, the emperor’s small daughter as a bride; he sent his wife home without her ears and nose.
  • The barbarians were a symptom. The Republic had beaten far more organised enemies such as Carthage. The cause was the same as for the Maya: extractive institutions breeding infighting.
  • It began with Augustus. The army was restructured so secession was impossible; Tiberius abolished the Plebeian Assembly; citizens got bread (later oil, wine and pork) and circuses instead of a voice. The Praetorian Guard became kingmaker. State lands grew, through confiscation, to as much as half the land in many provinces. In the Year of the Five Emperors (193) Septimius Severus seized power and confiscated his rivals’ property. Good emperors like Trajan, Hadrian and Marcus Aurelius didn’t fix the institutions, and Marcus was followed by Commodus.
  • By the third century every sizeable city had walls, and in Gaul settlements moved back to hilltops. Citizenship was extended to nearly everyone by 212, but equality before the law eroded, and farm workers were pushed into the semi-servile status of coloni: Constantine let landlords chain coloni suspected of fleeing (332), and from 365 they could not sell their own property without the landlord’s leave.
  • By AD 500 the shipwrecks had fallen back to 20, and metal pollution in the ice declined after its first-century peak.
  • Rome innovated early (concrete, pumps, the water wheel) and then stagnated: no stern rudder, slow spread of water power. Two stories show why. Pliny the Elder says Tiberius had the inventor of unbreakable glass killed “lest gold be reduced to the value of mud.” Suetonius says Vespasian turned down a cheap way to haul columns to the Capitol: “How will it be possible for me to feed the populace?” Slavery also killed innovation, since the people who did the work had no stake in improving it, as the American South would later show.

No one writes from Vindolanda

  • In AD 103 Octavius wrote to the centurion Candidus at the fort of Vindolanda, near what became Hadrian’s Wall, asking for at least 500 denarii so he would not lose the 300-denarii deposit on five thousand modii of grain, and complaining that the roads were too bad for his animals. The letter shows money, credit, roads, taxes, literacy and a postal service. Roman England also had mass-produced Oxfordshire pottery, baths, and houses with mortar and tiled roofs.
  • After 411 Rome gave England up. By 450 money had vanished, towns were abandoned, pottery was handmade again, people forgot how to use mortar, and roofs were made of branches. “Nobody wrote from Vindolanda anymore.”
  • England had been behind before: it hunted and gathered for at least 5,500 years after Jericho farmed, while Mesopotamia built Uruk (perhaps 14,000 people by 3500 BC, then 40,000) and Egypt built the pyramids of Giza (around 2500 BC) as England raised Stonehenge. So England’s later lead had shallow roots.

Diverging paths

  • What Europe did share was a set of critical junctures after Rome fell: weak successor kingdoms of Franks, Visigoths, Ostrogoths, Vandals and Burgundians, raided by Vikings, Huns and, after Muhammad’s death in 632, the new Islamic states. Out of these came feudalism.
  • Feudalism was extractive, but it had two by-products: with the whole peasantry reduced to serfdom, slavery disappeared from Europe; and in the power vacuum independent cities of traders and producers flourished. When the Black Death broke serfdom in the west, those made room for a pluralistic society without slaves.
  • Ethiopia shows the same forces elsewhere. Aksum, founded around 400 BC, traded with India, Arabia, Greece and Rome, and its King Ezana converted to Christianity about when Constantine did (312). Arab expansion in the seventh century cut its trade, and it retreated to the highlands. A feudal system, gult, emerged, in which holders owed the emperor military service and took one-half to three-quarters of peasants’ output, more than English lords took at feudalism’s height (about half). But Ethiopia, isolated, never met independent cities or Atlantic trade, and its absolutism went unchallenged.
  • Elsewhere in Africa slavery was never replaced by serfdom, and later East Africa supplied slaves to the Arab world and West and Central Africa to the Atlantic. The slave trade’s profits strengthened England’s opponents of absolutism and Africa’s absolutists.
  • In the Americas, cut off around 15,000 BC, Mexico and the Andes had their own Natufian-style revolutions and extractive growth, and then the reversal of fortune: inclusive institutions arose not in those lands but in the empty north.

Chapter 7

The Turning Point

How a political revolution in 1688 changed England’s institutions, and why that, not coal or culture, set off the Industrial Revolution there.

Trouble with stockings

  • In 1583 William Lee, a Cambridge-educated curate at Calverton, watched his mother and sisters knitting and wondered why a machine couldn’t use several needles at once. By 1589 his stocking frame was ready, and through his MP and Lord Hunsdon he got Elizabeth I to come and see it. She refused a patent: “Thou aimest high, Master Lee. Consider thou what the invention could do to my poor subjects. It would assuredly bring to them ruin by depriving them of employment, thus making them beggars.” Lee failed in France too, and James I refused him on the same grounds.
  • The monarchs’ real fear was not for the knitters but for themselves: unemployed knitters meant instability that threatened royal power. The authors make this the book’s key idea: fear of creative destruction is the main reason living standards did not rise in a sustained way between the Neolithic and Industrial Revolutions. Radical innovations usually come from newcomers, and they must get past the elite.
  • For millennia there was little progress. Life expectancy in Natufian Abu Hureyra was probably not much different from Rome’s, or Rome’s from seventeenth-century England’s. Robert Allen, using Diocletian’s Edict on Maximum Prices of AD 301, found a Roman unskilled worker’s living standard almost exactly that of an unskilled worker in seventeenth-century Italy. England’s wages, though, were already higher and rising.

Ever-present political conflict

  • In 1215 the barons forced King John to sign Magna Carta at Runnymede. It required the king to consult them on taxes, and clause 61 set up a council of twenty-five barons entitled to seize his castles and lands if he broke it. John got the pope to annul it, but the barons’ power remained: England’s “first hesitant step toward pluralism.”
  • The first elected Parliament of 1265 represented not only nobles but a broad set of interests, later including the gentry, commercial farmers on the rise; many of its members consistently opposed the growth of royal power.
  • The Wars of the Roses ended in 1485 with Henry Tudor as Henry VII, who disarmed the aristocracy. Under Henry VIII, Thomas Cromwell’s “revolution in government” in the 1530s built a nascent bureaucracy, and the break with Rome and the Dissolution of the Monasteries removed the Church as a rival power. Centralisation could have led to absolutism, but it also made local elites want a say in how the new power was used, through Parliament. So the Tudors built one pillar of inclusive institutions (centralisation) and indirectly encouraged the other (pluralism).
  • Meanwhile the Peasants’ Revolt of 1381 was followed by a long run of popular risings. Power was shifting not only from king to lords but from elite to people.
  • The Stuarts wanted absolutism. The economy was choked with monopolies: a list read out in Parliament in 1601 prompted one member to ask “Is not bread there?”, and by 1621 there were seven hundred. The historian Christopher Hill described a man living in a house of monopoly bricks, warmed by monopoly coal, washing in monopoly soap and reading monopoly books by monopoly candles.
  • The Statute of Monopolies (1623) barred James I from creating new domestic monopolies but left him overseas trade. Charles I (from 1625) ruled without Parliament after 1629, forced loans and defaulted on them, sold overseas monopolies, leaned on the judges, and levied “ship money” on coastal counties in 1634 and inland ones from 1635.
  • War with Scotland forced him to call the Short Parliament in 1640, which sat three weeks, and then the Long Parliament, which refused to dissolve. Civil war broke out in 1642, and sides followed economics. Shrewsbury and Oswestry merchants with royal monopolies backed the king. Birmingham’s metalworkers, free of monopolies and the seven-year apprenticeship, made swords for Parliament, as did the part of Lancashire making the unregulated “New Draperies”.
  • Cromwell’s Roundheads beat the Cavaliers and Charles was executed in 1649, but the result was Cromwell’s dictatorship, not inclusive institutions. The monarchy returned in 1660; Charles II and then James II (from 1685) pushed again for absolutism. In 1688 a more united Parliament invited William of Orange and James’s Protestant daughter Mary to take the throne. Two months after William landed at Brixham, James’s army disintegrated and he fled to France.

The Glorious Revolution

  • The Declaration of Rights of February 1689 (later the Bill of Rights) settled the succession by Parliament’s choice, not heredity, and said the monarch could not suspend laws, tax without Parliament’s consent or keep a standing army without it. Some clauses were vague (“The election of members of Parliament ought to be free”; Parliaments should be held frequently) because they didn’t need enforcing: after Charles II had ignored the Triennial Act, what mattered in 1688 was that authority itself had moved to Parliament. William stopped interfering with the courts and gave up the customs revenue for life.
  • Parliament’s members had investments in trade and industry, so they enforced property rights. And once they controlled spending, they were happy to raise taxes and spend them, above all on a navy to protect trade.
  • More important was pluralism. England was no democracy: less than 2% of the population, all men, could vote in the eighteenth century; Birmingham, Leeds, Manchester and Sheffield had no MPs; thirteen burgesses elected Buckingham’s members; and “rotten boroughs” like Old Sarum (seven voters) and Dunwich (thirty-two, most of the town having fallen into the sea) each returned two MPs.
  • The real channel was the petition. Anyone could petition Parliament, and Parliament listened. In 1689 the Royal African Company, the slave-trade monopoly chartered by Charles II in 1660 with James as governor, seized an interloper’s cargo; Chief Justice Holt ruled that only Parliament could create a monopoly. The petition war that followed (see the figure) ended in 1698 with the monopoly abolished.

What Parliament did with its power

  • It abolished the hearth tax (1689), which fell hardest on manufacturers, and taxed land instead; it passed laws favouring woollens; and it let landowners petition to sweep away archaic tenures, in hundreds of acts.
  • The Bank of England (1694) started a financial revolution. At C. Hoare’s & Co., whose records survive for 1702–24, two-thirds of the biggest borrowers were merchants and businessmen, not aristocrats; one John Smith borrowed £2,600 in 1715–19.
  • It also centralised. Having refused to strengthen a state it couldn’t control, Parliament now built one: spending reached about 10% of national income (a share Colombia reached only in the 1980s, and Sierra Leone would not reach without aid), and the excise service grew from 1,211 officers in 1690 to 4,800 by 1780. Between 12 June and 5 July 1710 Supervisor George Cowperthwaite rode 290 miles around Richmond in Yorkshire, visiting 263 victuallers, 71 maltsters, 20 chandlers and a brewer and taking 81 measurements, with three sets of books that had to agree. Appointment by merit began to replace patronage.

The Industrial Revolution

  • Secure property made transport possible. In 1662 the Baldwyn family spent £6,000 making the river Salwerpe navigable in return for tolls; in 1693 a bill to hand the tolls to the Earl of Shrewsbury and Lord Coventry failed after Sir Timothy Baldwyn petitioned that “it is of dangerous consequence to take away any person’s right, purchased under an act of Parliament, without their consent.” Under the Stuarts it would have passed. Canals and turnpike roads multiplied.
  • Parliament did tilt the field abroad. After the East India Company, which had bought James II’s protection with bribes, lost its monopoly, the wool interest won bans on wearing Asian silks and printed calicoes (1701) and on all printed calico (the Calico Act of 1721), after earlier securing laws (1666, 1678) that the dead be buried in woollen shrouds. The Navigation Acts, from 1651, reserved English trade for English ships.
  • But pluralism set limits. When the wool lobby turned on fustian, a linen-cotton cloth made in Manchester, Lancaster and Liverpool, the new cotton interests out-petitioned it, and the Manchester Act of 1736 protected them. It was the opposite of Venice’s Serrata.
  • After 1760 patents jumped. Watt’s separate condenser (1760s) and “sun and planets” gear built on Newcomen and on Denis Papin, whose steamboat of 1705 was smashed by the boatmen’s guild at Münden after the Elector of Kassel refused Leibniz’s plea to let it pass; Papin died a pauper, on his way to London. Abraham Darby smelted iron with coal from 1709, John Smeaton powered blowing cylinders by water in 1762, and Henry Cort improved wrought iron in the 1780s.
  • In textiles the spinning wheel (about 1280) had not changed for centuries. Then came Lewis Paul’s rollers (1738), Hargreaves’s jenny (1764), Arkwright’s water frame (1769) and his factory at Cromford (1771, six hundred workers by 1774), Crompton’s mule (1779) and Roberts’s self-acting mule. Spinning a hundred pounds of cotton fell from 50,000 hours by hand to 300 on the water frame and 135 on the self-acting mule. Kay’s flying shuttle (1733) and Cartwright’s power loom (1785) did the same for weaving. English exports doubled between 1780 and 1800.
  • The innovators were new men: the canal builders James Brindley (a millwright), Thomas Telford (a stonemason) and John Smeaton; John McAdam; Richard Trevithick (first steam locomotive, 1804); George Stephenson, son of illiterate parents. Only about a fifth of leading industrialists had any background in manufacturing, and cotton destroyed the old putting-out wool trade: creative destruction in action.
  • The new men wanted political power. On 16 August 1819 sixty thousand people gathered at St Peter’s Fields, Manchester, under banners reading “No Corn Laws”, “Universal Suffrage” and “Vote by Ballot”. The Fifteenth Hussars charged; eleven died and perhaps six hundred were wounded: the “Peterloo Massacre”. But repression was not sustainable in England. After France’s 1830 revolution against Charles X, the First Reform Act (1832) gave seats to Birmingham, Leeds, Manchester and Sheffield, and in 1846 the Corn Laws were repealed.
  • Winners tried to pull up the ladder too. In 1775 Arkwright took out a sweeping patent hoping to monopolise cotton spinning, and the courts would not enforce it.

Why in England?

  • Because of the drift and the juncture. After the Black Death, documents like Magna Carta had bite in the west and meant little in the east, and in England the rule that the king could not tax without Parliament was established before the seventeenth century.
  • Then Atlantic trade, which the English crown could not monopolise, created a new class of merchants. In London in 1686 there were 702 merchants exporting to the Caribbean and 1,283 importing, and 691 exporting to North America and 626 importing, with their clerks, sailors and dockers. They backed Parliament in 1642, formed the Whig Party in the 1670s, and were pivotal in 1688. In France, Spain and Portugal the same class existed but was smaller and weaker.
  • Most critically, the coalition was broad: gentry, manufacturers and Atlantic traders. A narrow winner would have repeated Lancaster against York. A broad one needed pluralism, so that no member could dominate the rest, which is why Parliament listened to petitions from people without votes and why the wool men could not crush cotton.
  • None of it was inevitable. A similar coalition won the Civil War and got Cromwell; James II could have beaten William. Contingency and a broad coalition decided it.

Chapter 8

Not on Our Turf: Barriers to Development

Why the Industrial Revolution passed most of the world by: absolutist rulers blocked it on purpose, and stateless societies could not have used it anyway.

No printing allowed

  • Gutenberg’s press (Mainz, 1445) reached Strasbourg by 1460, Rome and Venice by the late 1460s, London (Caxton) by 1476, Oxford two years later, Budapest in 1473 and Cracow in 1474.
  • In 1485 the Ottoman sultan Bayezid II forbade Muslims to print in Arabic, and Selim I reinforced the ban in 1515. Only in 1727 did Ahmed III let İbrahim Müteferrika open a press, with every book vetted by three kadis. He printed seventeen books between 1729 and 1743; his family managed seven more by 1797. Egypt’s first press came with Napoleon’s army in 1798. Istanbul in the early eighteenth century was said to have eighty thousand scribes.
  • The result: in 1800 perhaps 2–3% of Ottoman subjects could read, against 60% of adult men and 40% of adult women in England, more in the Netherlands and Germany, and about 20% even in Portugal, Europe’s laggard. Books spread ideas and weaken those who control oral knowledge, and the sultans and religious establishment feared that creative destruction.
  • The Industrial Revolution was a critical juncture for every country. The chapter sorts the failures into two kinds: absolutism, rule unconstrained by law, like the tsars backed by a nobility of about 1% of the population, with no parliament until the Duma of 1905; and the absence of political centralisation, as in Somalia or southern Sudan.
  • The two are linked. Centralising a state can breed absolutism, as when Peter the Great (1682–1725) built Saint Petersburg, broke the boyars, abolished the Boyar Duma, created the Table of Ranks and took over the Church, crushing the revolt of the Streltsy guards. Where the Streltsy-type forces won, the state stayed weak.

A small difference that mattered: Spain

  • Spain was formed in 1492 by the union of Castile and Aragon, the same year Granada fell and Columbus landed. Through Habsburg marriages Charles I of Spain (1516) became Emperor Charles V (1519), ruling Iberia, the Americas, the Netherlands, Franche-Comté and the Habsburg lands in Germany.
  • American silver (Guanajuato and Zacatecas by the 1520s, Peru after 1532, Potosí in the 1540s) paid the Crown its “royal fifth”, freeing it from the Cortes.
  • Property was insecure. About two hundred thousand Jews were given four months to leave in 1492, selling their property cheaply and forbidden to take gold or silver; Philip III expelled the Moriscos in 1609–14 on the same terms. Philip II defaulted in 1557 and 1560, ruining the Fuggers and Welsers, and the Habsburgs ruined their Genoese successors with defaults in 1575, 1596, 1607, 1627, 1647, 1652, 1660 and 1662.
  • American trade ran through a single merchants’ guild in Seville, in annual convoys; a merchant in New Spain could not even trade directly with New Granada. So no broad merchant class arose.
  • The Cortes of Castile represented only eighteen cities, two deputies each, could not legislate, and had limited powers over tax. Charles V crushed the Comunero revolt of 1520, and after 1664 the Cortes did not meet again until the Napoleonic invasion almost 150 years later. The Crown sold offices, farmed out taxes and sold immunity from justice. Spain’s urban share fell from one in five to one in ten during the seventeenth century. By the industrial age there was nothing left worth blocking.

Fear of industry: Austria-Hungary

  • The Habsburg empire covered about 250,000 square miles and a seventh of Europe’s people, from the Austrian Netherlands to Romania. Serfdom prevailed in the east, merchants mattered little, and there was no national parliament.
  • Francis I (Holy Roman Emperor from 1792, then emperor of Austria until 1835) opposed “any sort of change”. He told teachers at Laibach in 1821: “I do not need savants, but good, honest citizens.” When Tyrolese petitioned for a constitution, he told them the soldiers obeyed him, and when they replied that it was better to have none, answered “That is also my opinion.” Maria Theresa’s habitual answer to reform proposals had been “Leave everything as it is.”
  • He abolished the State Council, built a police state with censorship, and relied on Metternich, foreign minister for almost forty years from 1809. When Robert Owen urged social reform, Metternich’s aide Friedrich von Gentz replied: “We do not desire at all that the great masses shall become well off and independent . . . How could we otherwise rule over them?”
  • Unlike Kongo’s king, who wanted his people to use ploughs but could not make them believe their gains were safe, Francis actively blocked technology people wanted. In 1802 he banned new factories in Vienna, which might fill with workers hostile to absolutism, and he banned imported machinery until 1811. Of a proposed northern railway he said: “No, no, I will have nothing to do with it, lest the revolution might come into the country.”
  • So the first railway in the empire, Linz to Budweis, ran on horses, with curves and gradients that made later conversion to steam impossible; it used horses until the 1860s. Salomon Rothschild, urged on by his brother Nathan after seeing Stephenson’s Rocket, could not get permission for steam. As late as 1883, when 90% of the world’s iron was smelted with coal, more than half of Habsburg iron still used charcoal, and weaving was never fully mechanised before 1914.

Fear of industry: Russia

  • Russian serfs, at least half the population, owed three days’ unpaid work a week and could be sold at will. Peter Kropotkin remembered people “exchanged for a couple of hunting dogs”, daily floggings “in the stables”, and serf revolts put down by flogging every tenth or fifth man to death.
  • Nicholas I (1825–55), who nearly lost his throne to the Decembrist officers’ coup, swore that revolution would not enter Russia “while there is breath in my body”, and warned manufacturers that factory workers needed “paternal supervision of their morals” or would become a class “as miserable as they are dangerous for their masters.”
  • His finance minister Egor Kankrin (1823–44) killed a plan for a state Commercial Bank to lend to industry and moved its assets to the State Loan Bank, which lent to landowners against serfs as collateral. He banned industrial exhibitions. After the revolutions of 1848, a law of 1849 limited new factories in Moscow and banned new cotton and wool spinning mills and iron foundries there.
  • Kankrin thought railways “encourage unnecessary travel from place to place, which is entirely typical of our time.” Until 1842 Russia’s only line ran seventeen miles from Saint Petersburg to the imperial residences at Tsarskoe Selo and Pavlovsk; Moscow and Saint Petersburg were linked only in 1851, and Count Kleinmichel even censored newspaper discussion of railways after 1849. The book’s map of 1870 shows north-west Europe criss-crossed by railways and Russia almost empty. Only defeat in the Crimean War (1853–56) changed the policy.

No shipping allowed: China

  • Under the Song (960–1279) China invented clocks, the compass, gunpowder, paper money, porcelain and the blast furnace, and in 1500 its living standards were probably at least as high as Europe’s. But it was absolutist, with no parliament, a precarious status for merchants and inventions made at government command.
  • The Ming founder Hongwu (from 1368) allowed foreign trade only as state tribute, executed hundreds for turning tribute missions into commerce, and allowed no ocean-going missions from 1377 to 1397. Yongle (from 1402) sent Zheng He on six great voyages, the first with 27,800 men, 62 treasure ships and 190 smaller ships, then stopped them in 1422. After a last voyage in 1433 all overseas trade was banned, building seagoing ships was made illegal by 1436, and the ban lasted until 1567.
  • The Qing, who overran the Ming in 1644, expropriated on a mass scale; the scholar T’ang Chen wrote in the 1690s that farmers, artisans, merchants and officials were all destitute. In 1661 Kangxi ordered everyone on the southern coast to move seventeen miles inland, and shipping was banned along the whole coast until 1693, with bans reimposed in the eighteenth century. Nobody invests in ships when the emperor can make them worthless overnight. By 1949 China was one of the poorest countries in the world.

The absolutism of Prester John: Ethiopia

  • Europeans identified Ethiopia’s Christian kings with the legendary Prester John. Their visitors (Francisco Álvares, 1520–27; the Jesuit Manoel de Almeida, from 1624; the traveller James Bruce, 1768–73) describe pure absolutism by emperors claiming descent from Solomon and Sheba. Bruce: “all the land is the king’s.” Almeida: lands were reallocated every year or two, so one man ploughed, another sowed and another reaped, and nobody planted trees. Gibbon wrote that the Ethiopians “slept near a thousand years, forgetful of the world by whom they were forgotten.”
  • Tewodros II (crowned 1855) centralised the state, imprisoned the British consul, and killed himself when a British force sacked his capital in 1868. Menelik II (from 1889) beat an Italian army at Adowa in 1896, fifteen thousand Italians against a hundred thousand Ethiopians raised by the nobility, the greatest defeat a pre-colonial African state inflicted on a European power.
  • Haile Selassie (crowned 1930) was driven out by Italy in 1935, restored by the British in 1941 and overthrown by the Marxist Derg in 1974, which abolished the gult system and further ravaged the country. The average Ethiopian today earns about a fortieth of the average English income, lives about fifty-five years, and is literate only one time in three.

The children of Samaale: Somalia

  • Somalia is the other barrier: no state at all. Four pastoral clan families (Dir, Darod, Isaq, Hawiye) trace descent to a mythical Samaale; two farming ones (Digil, Rahanweyn) live in the south. The real units are diya-paying groups of kin who pay and collect blood money. Sultans and elders had no real power; every adult man could speak in council; there was no written law and no police.
  • Each group’s rules, its heer, were written down only under colonial rule. The Hassan Ugaas, about fifteen hundred men of the Dir, recorded theirs on 8 March 1950: a murdered member’s blood wealth was a hundred camels, twenty to the next of kin and eighty shared; insulting a member in council cost 150 shillings.
  • In 1955 a dispute over camels between the Habar Tol Ja’lo and Habar Yuunis clans led to a killing, compensation, and then a second killing at the handover ceremony by a man who mistook his victim. Within forty-eight hours thirteen Yuunis and twenty-six Tol Ja’lo were dead, and the war went on for a year until the British brokered a settlement paid over three years.
  • Power was dispersed “almost pluralistically”, but without a state it produced chaos, not inclusive institutions. No one could have invested in industrial technology.
  • Why no writing? The Kingdom of Taqali in the Nuba Hills of Sudan, founded by Isma’il in the late eighteenth century and independent until 1884, had Arabic writing but used it only for diplomacy. The historian Janet Ewald found that subjects feared writing would let the state claim land and tax them, while the elite preferred oral orders they could deny or reverse. The authors suspect the same politics kept the Somalis from using their own script.

Chapter 9

Reversing Development

The same European expansion that helped make England inclusive imposed or hardened extractive institutions elsewhere: genocide in the Spice Islands, the slave trade in Africa, and a dual economy built on purpose in South Africa.

Spice and genocide

  • In the early seventeenth century the Moluccas were the world’s only source of cloves, mace and nutmeg; nutmeg and mace grew only on the Banda Islands. The Portuguese reached them after Dias rounded the Cape (1488) and da Gama reached India (1498), and took the entrepôt of Melaka in 1511. Tomé Pires wrote in 1515 that “whoever is lord of Melaka has his hands at the throat of Venice.” But they failed to monopolise the spice trade.
  • Southeast Asia was commercialising fast between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries. City-states such as Aceh, Banten, Melaka, Makassar, Pegu and Brunei grew rich on spices and hardwoods, under absolutist kings much like Europe’s.
  • The Dutch went further. In 1600 they got the ruler of Ambon to grant them a clove monopoly; the Dutch East India Company, founded in 1602 with its own army, took a Portuguese fort in 1605, drove out other traders, made Tidore, Ternate and Bacan agree to grow no cloves (Ternate’s treaty let the Dutch destroy any clove trees they found), and tied Ambonese households to the soil, each obliged to tend a quota of clove trees and do forced labour.
  • Banda had no king to coerce, only small towns run by village meetings, so the Dutch had to pay market prices. Jan Pieterszoon Coen, who founded Batavia in 1618, sailed to Banda in 1621 and massacred almost the entire population, probably about fifteen thousand people, keeping alive only enough to teach the craft. He divided the islands into sixty-eight parcels for sixty-eight Dutchmen, worked by slaves bought from the Company, selling at fixed prices to the Company. By the end of the century the Dutch had cut world supply of these spices by about 60% and the price of nutmeg had doubled.
  • The rest of the region retreated from trade. Banten cut down its pepper trees in 1620 hoping the Dutch would leave it alone; in 1686 a Dutch merchant was told in Maguindanao that the old raja had destroyed the nutmeg and cloves for fear of the Company; in 1635 Burma moved its capital from coastal Pegu to Ava, far up the Irrawaddy. It didn’t save them: by the end of the eighteenth century nearly all were colonies.

The all-too-usual institution

  • The American South called slavery its “peculiar institution”, but, as the classicist Moses Finley pointed out, it was present in almost every society. Europeans had stopped enslaving each other by 1400; Africa never made the switch to serfdom, and medieval Mali, Ghana and Songhai used slaves in government, armies and farming.
  • Caribbean sugar changed the scale (see the figure). By 1730 about 180,000 guns a year were imported on the West African coast alone; between 1750 and the early nineteenth century the British sold 283,000–394,000 guns a year, and between 1750 and 1807 some 22,000 tons of gunpowder. The Loango coast took about 50,000 guns a year.
  • The trade did two things to African states. Many became more absolutist, organised around a single purpose, capturing people to sell. And the endless warfare destroyed whatever legitimate order existed. The law itself became a slaving tool: Francis Moore reported from Senegambia in the 1730s that “all punishments are changed into slavery.” At Arochukwa in eastern Nigeria, people judged by the oracle were “swallowed” by it, which meant being led down the Cross River to the European ships.
  • Slaving states rose: Kongo first (until civil war destroyed it), then Oyo, whose imported horses and cavalry let it monopolise the interior of the Slave Coast between 1690 and 1740 (80–90% of slaves sold there came from its conquests), Dahomey, and Asante, whose Akan Wars ended with the conquest of Gyaman in 1747 and supplied most of the 375,000 slaves exported from the Gold Coast in 1700–50.
  • The historian Patrick Manning estimates that West and West-Central Africa had 22–25 million people in the early eighteenth century and, growing at half a percent a year, should have had 46–53 million by 1850. It had about half that, because of about eight million people exported in 1700–1850 and the millions killed in slave wars.
  • Britain banned the trade in 1807, led by William Wilberforce, and policed it with naval squadrons; the United States followed in 1808; slavery itself ended in the British Empire in 1834. But African slavery didn’t wither. Slaves were redeployed at home to produce “legitimate commerce”: palm oil, groundnuts, ivory, rubber, gum arabic. Asante put them on plantations around Kumase growing kola and mining gold; Dahomey’s king had slave palm-oil plantations near Whydah and Porto Novo.
  • Oyo collapsed in the 1820s–30s amid civil wars and the rise of slaving Yoruba city-states like Ilorin and Ibadan; in parts of Nigeria parents kept children indoors for fear of kidnappers. Well over half the people of Asante, Dahomey and the Yoruba cities were probably slaves in the nineteenth century, and 30% of the western Sudan in 1900. Slavery was abolished in Sierra Leone, founded as a haven for freed slaves, only in 1928; in Liberia, founded for freed Americans, a quarter of the workforce was still coerced in the 1960s.

Making a dual economy

  • In 1955 Arthur Lewis described poor economies as dual: a modern, urban, industrial sector and a traditional, rural one with communal land and labour so unproductive it could move to the cities at no cost. He won the Nobel Prize in 1979, and “the problem of development” came to mean moving people from the one to the other.
  • Drive across the Great Kei River in South Africa and you see it: beach houses, sugar cane and fine roads in Natal; brown, deforested land, huts and open fires in the Transkei. The authors’ point is that the Transkei’s backwardness was not a natural remnant. It was built, recently and on purpose, by white South Africa to supply cheap labour and remove black competition.
  • South Africa escaped the slave trade. The Dutch founded Cape Town in 1652 among the Khoikhoi; the farming Xhosa of the Ciskei and Transkei were far enough inland to be left alone. The temperate, malaria-free climate drew settlers: the British took the Cape in the Napoleonic Wars, the Boers’ Great Trek (from 1835) founded the Orange Free State and the Transvaal, and diamonds at Kimberley (1867) and gold at Johannesburg (1886) led to the Boer Wars (1880–81 and 1899–1902) and the Union of South Africa (1910).
  • Africans seized the new markets, as the historian Colin Bundy documented. A missionary in 1832 saw Transkei Africans working to buy “clothes, spades, ploughs, wagons”. In Fingoland in 1876 the civil commissioner John Hemming found brick houses, fruit trees, irrigation, and ploughed land up to the hilltops. In Griqualand East, Africans had bought 38,000 acres by 1879, and three years later about eight thousand African farmers worked ninety thousand acres. In 1869 a missionary collected £46 from African farmers for the Lancashire Cotton Relief Fund: African farmers sending relief to English textile workers.
  • Stephen Sonjica’s father told him “all land is God’s, and he gave it to the chiefs only”; Sonjica secretly saved £80 from a job in King William’s Town, bought oxen and a plough, then a farm. The chiefs resisted surveying and fencing, which they rightly saw as the end of their power, but under colonial rule they became “titled landowners without political power”.
  • Then, between 1890 and 1913, the boom was deliberately destroyed, by white farmers who didn’t want the competition and mine owners who wanted cheap labour. In 1897 George Albu, chairman of the Association of Mines, told a commission that if Africans would not work for lower wages he would compel them: “Tax him, then.”
  • The Natives Land Act of 1913 gave 87% of the land to Europeans, about 20% of the population, and 13% to Africans, in reserves such as the Transkei and Ciskei that became the “Homelands” or Bantustans. It also ended sharecropping partnerships: all new contracts with Africans had to be contracts of service. The 1936 act extended it.
  • Evicted Africans were crowded into Homelands too small to live on, gave up ploughs for hoes, and went to the mines. Chiefs’ power over land was restored, culminating in the Bantu Authorities Act (1951): “Cheap labour must have a cheap breeding place,” G. Findlay wrote in 1940. Miners’ wages fell 30% between 1911 and 1921 and were still 12% below 1911 in 1961, and South Africa became the most unequal country in the world.
  • A colour bar from 1904 barred Africans from a list of skilled mining jobs running from “amalgamator” to “woodworking machinist”; it was extended to the whole economy in 1926 and lasted until the 1980s. Hendrik Verwoerd’s Bantu Education Act rested on his 1954 claim that “there is no place for him in the European community above the level of certain forms of labour.” By the 1930s blacks were disenfranchised everywhere.
  • So the dual economy was not Lewis’s stage of development but its mechanism: the white economy’s profits depended on the Homelands. It was growth without creative destruction, which stalled by the 1970s. It ended not through development but through black political power, organised after the Soweto uprising of 1976, which brought apartheid down in 1994.

Development reversed

  • European expansion did not just fail to spread prosperity; it often fed on the destruction of independent polities and economies. We will never know what Banda, Aceh or Burma might have become.
  • India was the world’s largest textile producer in the eighteenth century. The English East India Company, founded in 1600, lost its Stuart-granted monopoly after 1688, and Parliament banned its best product, calico. Under Robert Clive it turned to conquest: Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764) in Bengal. It looted, took over the Mughal tax system, and presided over the collapse of Indian textiles, de-urbanisation and poverty. Soon Indians were buying British cloth and growing opium for the Company to sell in China.

Chapter 10

The Diffusion of Prosperity

England’s revolution was not the only road to inclusive institutions. Settler colonies got there because extraction was impossible, much of western Europe had them imposed by French armies, and Japan made its own revolution.

Honor among thieves

  • After American independence Britain needed a new dumping ground for convicts. West Africa was too deadly even for them, so it chose Botany Bay, where Captain Cook had landed on 29 April 1770. Captain Arthur Phillip’s fleet of eleven ships set up camp at Sydney Cove on 26 January 1788, now Australia Day, founding New South Wales.
  • Aboard the Alexander were Henry and Susannah Cable, convicts who had met in Norwich Castle jail, with their baby son; the philanthropist Lady Cadogan had campaigned to keep the family together and raised £20 for a parcel of goods for them. On arrival the captain, Duncan Sinclair, said the parcel had vanished, and boasted they could not sue him. Under British law he was right: convicts could not own property, sue, or even give evidence.
  • Their writ described them as “new settlers of this place”; the words were struck out, probably by Judge Advocate David Collins, but he heard the case anyway, before a jury of soldiers. The Cables won £15. It was Australia’s first civil case, and to reach it Collins ignored British law. The first criminal case convicted a convict of stealing another convict’s bread, worth two pence, when convicts were not supposed to own anything.
  • New South Wales was convicts and guards; free settlers were few until the 1820s, and transportation continued until 1840 there and 1868 in Western Australia. Forced labour with no pay didn’t work, and lashing or banishment to Norfolk Island didn’t fix it. There was no Latin American option: perhaps a million Aboriginal people were spread thinly over the continent. So the guards gave convicts tasks and let them work for themselves in their spare time.
  • The guards profited by selling to the convicts, above all rum. When Governor William Bligh, of the Bounty, challenged the rum monopoly in 1806, the monopolists led by the former soldier John Macarthur rose in the “Rum Rebellion” and locked him up. Macarthur was shipped to Britain, released, and came back to become the richest man in Australia on sheep, once the Blue Mountains were crossed in 1813. He and his fellow graziers were the Squatters, since the land they grazed belonged to the Crown: the “Squattocracy”.
  • Convicts could soon hire other convicts, got land when their sentences ended and had their rights restored. Henry Cable, illiterate, owned a hotel called the Ramping Horse and a shop by 1798, went into sealskins, and by 1809 had at least nine farms of about 470 acres plus shops and houses in Sydney.
  • Next came conflict between the elite and the ex-convicts, who wanted an end to transportation, juries of their peers and free land, while the Squatters wanted title to what they grazed: Macarthur played the role of Lord Baltimore. In 1819 London sent John Bigge, who was shocked by convicts’ rights and recommended ending their wages, land and pardons, with the Squatters as a natural aristocracy. It didn’t happen.
  • The ex-convicts’ leader was William Wentworth, explorer of the Blue Mountains and founder of the newspaper The Australian, whose father had accepted transportation to avoid trial for highway robbery. Macarthur listed Wentworth’s supporters with contempt: “repeatedly flogged at the cart’s tail”, “son of two convicts”, “married to a convict who was formerly a tambourine girl”.
  • The tide ran their way. An appointed council limited the governor from 1823; Governor Richard Bourke let ex-convicts sit on juries in 1831; transportation to New South Wales ended in 1840, as free workers wanted; a legislative council two-thirds elected came in 1842. Adult white male suffrage followed in the 1850s, by which time Wentworth himself had turned conservative. In 1856 Victoria and Tasmania became the first places in the world with an effective secret ballot, still called the Australian ballot.
  • Sydney repeated Jamestown 181 years on: no dense population to exploit, no precious metals, no crops for slave plantations (the slave trade was flourishing in the 1780s and nobody found it worth using). Australia and the United States didn’t need England’s revolutions, because there was no entrenched absolutism to remove. Canada and New Zealand followed the same path.

Breaking the barriers: the French Revolution

  • Old-regime France had three estates: nobility, clergy and everyone else. The first two paid no taxes, the Church levied its own on peasants, guilds barred entry to trades, and peasants owed a thicket of feudal dues.
  • Louis XIV (who ruled in person from 1661 to 1715) never called the Assembly of Notables; Colbert built state-controlled industry, a form of extractive growth. By Louis XVI’s accession in 1774, the Seven Years’ War (1756–63), which cost France Canada, had turned money trouble into fiscal crisis. Turgot, Necker and Calonne all failed to fix it. Calonne had Louis summon the Notables, who, like Charles I’s Parliament in 1640, refused and said only the Estates-General could approve reform. It had last met in 1614.
  • The Estates-General met at Versailles in 1789 and deadlocked over the Third Estate’s demand for more votes; the Third Estate’s merchants, professionals and artisans pressed on in a National Assembly, reconstituted as the National Constituent Assembly on 9 July. When the king sacked Necker, Paris rose and stormed the Bastille on 14 July.
  • On 4 August 1789 the Assembly abolished the feudal system and serfdom’s dues without compensation (article 1), ended all tax privileges (article 9) and made every citizen “without distinction of birth” eligible for any office (article 11). Church taxes went, the clergy became state employees, and the guilds and occupational restrictions were abolished. The constitution of 29 September 1791 made France a constitutional monarchy with equal rights for all men.
  • War with the Austria-led first coalition in 1792 radicalised the revolution: the king and queen were executed, and the Terror under Robespierre and Saint-Just killed aristocrats and revolutionaries alike (Brissot, Danton, Desmoulins) until its leaders were executed in July 1794. Then came the Directory (1795–99), the Consulate of Ducos, Sieyès and Bonaparte, and Napoleon’s personal rule, with victories at Austerlitz, Jena-Auerstedt and Wagram. After 1815 the monarchy was restored under Louis XVIII (the book says Louis XVII), but this only slowed the change, which culminated in the Third Republic in 1870.

Exporting the revolution

  • Frankfurt’s Jews lived under medieval rules: at most five hundred families, all in the Judengasse, a street a quarter of a mile long and no more than twelve feet wide, locked in at night, on Sundays and on Christian holidays. At most two new families a year could be admitted and twelve couples a year could marry, both over twenty-five. Jews could not farm or trade in weapons, spices, wine or grain, paid a poll tax, and until 1726 wore yellow rings or striped veils.
  • Mayer Amschel Rothschild was already Frankfurt’s leading dealer in coins by the early 1780s but could not do business outside the ghetto. France emancipated its Jews in 1791; French shells destroyed half the Judengasse in 1796, and about two thousand Jews, the Rothschilds among them, had to move out. They took a contract to supply grain to the Austrian army. Full emancipation came in 1811 under Karl von Dalberg, Napoleon’s Grand Duke of Frankfurt: “you are now a citizen,” Mayer Amschel told his son. His sons’ bank, with branches in Frankfurt, London, Paris, Naples and Vienna, became Europe’s largest.
  • Most of Europe was worse than France. In Nassau-Usingen peasants owed 230 different payments, including a blood tithe on slaughtered animals, a bee tithe and a wax tithe. Guilds in Cologne and Aachen blocked spinning and weaving machines; a few families ran cities from Berne to Florence.
  • Mass conscription, introduced in August 1793, gave France an edge before Napoleon did. It took the Austrian Netherlands, the Dutch United Provinces (the Batavian Republic) and much of Switzerland, the Rhineland by 1795 (annexed in 1802), Savoy in 1792, northern Italy in Napoleon’s campaign of 1796–97 (the Treaty of Campo Formio), the Papal States (the Roman Republic, 1798) and Naples (the Parthenopean Republic, 1799). Everywhere it abolished serfdom and feudal dues, stripped the clergy of privilege, broke the guilds and imposed equality before the law, and Napoleon added his Code.
  • Some reforms were reversed, in Hanover for example, but in many places the Code survived the French. By the mid-nineteenth century industry was spreading fast in almost all the lands France had controlled, while Austria-Hungary and Russia, never conquered, and Poland and Spain, held briefly, stagnated.

Seeking modernity: Japan

  • On 14 October 1867 Ōkubo Toshimichi, a samurai courtier of the Satsuma domain, met the leaders of Chōshū at Yamaguchi and proposed marching on Edo to overthrow the shogun. With Tosa and Aki already aboard, the secret Satchō Alliance was formed.
  • The Tokugawa had ruled since 1600 (as shogun from 1603), sidelining the emperor, with feudal lords, samurai, fixed occupations, trade restrictions and heavy taxes on farmers; the shogun monopolised foreign trade. But Satsuma, in the far south, was autonomous enough to trade abroad through the Ryukyu Islands. Its lord Shimazu Nariakira planned to overthrow the shogun, open trade and build a modern state, but died in 1858; Ōkubo won over his cautious successor Shimazu Hisamitsu.
  • The cause was dressed as restoring the emperor, but Sakamoto Ryōma of Tosa gave the shogun an eight-point plan that also called for two legislative houses deciding “on the basis of general opinion”, the employment of men of ability, and a new legal code. Shogun Yoshinobu resigned, and the Meiji Restoration was declared on 3 January 1868. When he attacked Satsuma and Chōshū forces on 27 January, a civil war followed that ended in the Tokugawa defeat that summer.
  • In 1869 feudalism was abolished and the three hundred domains became prefectures under appointed governors; taxation was centralised; all classes became equal before the law; restrictions on movement and trade ended; the samurai class was abolished, after some rebellions; private property in land and free choice of trade arrived. The same year the government started a Tokyo–Osaka steamship line and the first railway, Tokyo to Yokohama. Satsuma had already built Japan’s first modern cotton mill (1861) with English machinery. In 1890 Japan became the first Asian country with a written constitution, an elected Diet and an independent judiciary.
  • Why Japan and not China? Both were poor and absolutist. But China was a centralised empire where opposition, even the Taiping Rebellion of 1850–64, in which millions died, was never institutionalised, while Tokugawa power over the great domains was never absolute. Both felt the military threat (the First Opium War of 1839–42; Perry in 1853). Japan overthrew its shogun and built its own arms industry; China could not overthrow its emperor until 1911 and tried to import weapons instead.

Roots of world inequality

  • The United States became the first nation to exploit British technology and soon surpassed Britain. Australia followed, less noticed. Most other colonies went the opposite way: their colonisers imposed or took over extractive institutions to get spices, sugar, silver and gold, and sometimes stamped out burgeoning industry.
  • In Europe the industrial juncture played out like the Black Death: western absolutism fell to revolution and export; eastern absolutism dug in and survived until the First World War. In Asia, China reacted like eastern Europe and Japan like western Europe. In Africa the slave trade and Ethiopia’s isolated absolutism locked in extraction.
  • With few exceptions, the rich countries of today are those that industrialised from the nineteenth century, and the poor ones are those that did not.

Chapter 11

The Virtuous Circle

Why inclusive institutions persist and widen: pluralism breeds the rule of law, inclusive economics lowers the stakes of power, and a free press raises the alarm. Britain’s Black Act and slow march to democracy, America’s trust-busting and Roosevelt’s court plan, with Argentina as the counter-case.

The Black Act

  • On 27 June 1722 Baptist Nunn, a keeper of Windsor Forest, wrote that “Blacks came in the night shot at me 3 times” and that he had agreed to pay them five guineas. The “Blacks” were local men with blackened faces who killed deer, burned barns and wrecked fences across southern England.
  • It was not mere lawlessness. The Whigs, founded in the 1670s and dominant in Parliament from 1714 to 1760, were using their power to enclose common rights. General William Cadogan, made a baron in 1716 and an earl in 1718, built a 240-acre deer park on his thousand acres at Caversham by evicting people and ending their rights to graze, cut peat and gather firewood; mounted Blacks raided it on 1 January 1722 and again in July, killing sixteen deer the first time. Robert Walpole made his son ranger of Richmond Park, walled it, set man traps and ended the locals’ grazing and hunting.
  • In May 1723 Parliament passed the Black Act, creating fifty new offences punishable by hanging, including having a blackened face. Nunn built a network of informers.
  • On 10 November 1724 John Huntridge, who lived by Richmond Park, was charged with abetting deer stealers, a hanging offence, on a prosecution started by the Regency Council that Walpole and Cadogan dominated; Walpole took evidence from the informer himself. After an eight- or nine-hour trial a jury of landowners and gentry acquitted him, partly because the evidence had been gathered irregularly. Others were hanged or transported, and the act stayed on the books until 1824, but the elite could not simply get their way.
  • The authors distinguish the rule of law from rule by law: the principle that laws apply equally and no one is above them. It is unthinkable under absolutism and is a product of pluralism. With many groups sharing power, each insisted that the rules bind the others, and the Whigs had argued against the Stuarts in exactly those terms. E. P. Thompson: the rulers were “prisoners of their own rhetoric”; “take away law, and the royal prerogative . . . might flood back upon their properties and lives.”
  • England also stopped censoring the press after 1688. And the circle was not guaranteed: in 1745 the Young Pretender reached Derby, a hundred miles from London.

The slow march of democracy

  • The Luddite riots (1811–16), Spa Fields (1816), Peterloo (1819), the Swing Riots (1830) and the July Revolution in Paris (1830) convinced the elite that reform was the price of avoiding revolution. The 1831 election was fought on it, and the Whig prime minister Earl Grey said so plainly: “The principle of my reform is, to prevent the necessity of revolution . . . reforming to preserve and not to overthrow.” Democracy in Britain was “not given by the elite. It was largely taken by the masses.”
  • The First Reform Act (1832) doubled the franchise from 8% to about 16% of adult men (2% to 4% of everyone), abolished rotten boroughs and gave seats to Manchester, Leeds and Sheffield.
  • The Chartists’ People’s Charter of 1838 demanded a vote for every man over twenty-one, the secret ballot, no property qualification for MPs, paid MPs, equal constituencies and annual parliaments. For the Chartist J. R. Stephens universal suffrage was “a knife and fork question, a bread and cheese question.”
  • Why didn’t the elite simply repress it, as Austria-Hungary and Russia did, or as Britain did in the Caribbean and India? Because of the circle. Repression would have torn up 150 years of constitutional legality (Thompson: “they surrendered to the law”); inclusive economics had left less at stake (no serfs, few monopolies); and an urban, organised population was far harder to crush than peasants. Reformers played on the law’s symbolism: one of the first reform societies was the Hampden Club, named after the MP who defied Charles I over ship money.
  • After the Chartists came the National Reform Union (1864), the Reform League (1865) and the Hyde Park riots of July 1866. The Second Reform Act (1867) doubled the electorate and made working-class voters the majority in towns; the secret ballot followed, and “treating” (buying votes with money, food or drink) was curbed. The Third Reform Act (1884) enfranchised 60% of adult men. The act of 1918, a wartime bargain with the working class (and perhaps a nod to the Russian Revolution), gave the vote to all men over twenty-one and to women over thirty who paid tax or were married to taxpayers; women got the vote on equal terms in 1928.
  • Economic institutions widened alongside. The Corn Laws went in 1846. Gladstone opened the civil service to examination in 1871. The Masters and Servants Acts, which let employers use the law to keep workers from leaving, were repealed. Asquith and Lloyd George (1906–14) brought in health and unemployment insurance, pensions, minimum wages and redistributive taxes; taxes as a share of output doubled in the last three decades of the nineteenth century and doubled again in the first three of the twentieth.
  • Schooling became universal: the Education Act of 1870, free schooling in 1891, a leaving age of eleven in 1893 and twelve in 1899, grammar schools after 1902. The share of ten-year-olds in school rose from 40% in 1870 to 100% in 1900.
  • The authors call this a gradual virtuous circle. Small steps are less threatening than overthrow, so each concession was easier: had the franchise, the rotten boroughs and the Corn Laws all been on the table in 1832, the landowners would have fought much harder. Revolutions that start from scratch, as in France (the Terror, two monarchies, then the Third Republic in 1870) or Russia, venture into uncharted territory. Edmund Burke’s 1790 warning against pulling down an edifice was wrong about France, the authors say, but right that gradual change is more durable.

Busting trusts

  • By the mid-nineteenth century all white men could vote in the United States, and the Homestead Act of 1862 opened frontier land to settlers. After the Civil War the Robber Barons built monopolies. Cornelius Vanderbilt: “What do I care about the Law? Hain’t I got the power?” John D. Rockefeller founded Standard Oil in 1870, had a trust by 1882, controlled 88% of refined oil flows by 1890, and became the world’s first billionaire in 1916. J. P. Morgan and Andrew Carnegie founded US Steel in 1901, the first corporation capitalised at over $1 billion. In the 1890s trusts in many sectors (Du Pont, Eastman Kodak, International Harvester) controlled over 70% of their markets.
  • Pluralism let the victims organise. The Grangers (1867) won control of eleven midwestern legislatures in 1873–74; the People’s Party took 8.5% of the presidential vote in 1892 and then backed William Jennings Bryan. Nearly all the fifty-nine anti-trust petitions to Congress before 1890 came from farm organisations.
  • Then came the Interstate Commerce Act (1887) and the Sherman Antitrust Act (1890). Theodore Roosevelt told Congress in 1901 that trusts should be “not prohibited, but supervised and within reasonable limits controlled”, broke up J. P. Morgan’s Northern Securities in 1902, sued Du Pont, American Tobacco and Standard Oil, and strengthened the ICC with the Hepburn Act (1906). Taft broke up Standard Oil in 1911 and brought in the federal income tax (the Sixteenth Amendment, 1913). Wilson (“If monopoly persists, monopoly will always sit at the helm of government”) passed the Clayton Act (1914), created the Federal Trade Commission, and after the Pujo Committee’s inquiry into the “money trust” created the Federal Reserve Board (1913).
  • Markets alone are not inclusive; left to themselves they can be captured. Inclusive politics is the counterweight, which is the contrast with Carlos Slim’s Mexico.
  • The press mattered. Roosevelt coined “muckraker” in 1906 from Bunyan’s man with the muckrake. Ida Tarbell’s History of the Standard Oil Company (1904) turned opinion against Rockefeller; Louis Brandeis’s Other People’s Money informed the Pujo Committee; Hearst’s Cosmopolitan serialised David Graham Phillips’s “The Treason of the Senate” in 1906, which helped bring direct election of senators (the Seventeenth Amendment, 1913).

Packing the court

  • Franklin Roosevelt took office in early 1933 with a quarter of the workforce unemployed and industrial production down by more than half since 1929, having won 57% of the vote. On 27 May 1935 the Supreme Court unanimously struck down Title I of his National Industrial Recovery Act: “extraordinary conditions do not create or enlarge constitutional power.” The Social Security Act and the National Labor Relations Act were next in line.
  • Re-elected in 1936 with 61%, Roosevelt used a Fireside Chat on 9 March 1937 to say that his gold policy had survived by one vote, that the court was acting as a policymaking body, and that two horses of the three-horse team were pulling and the third was not. He proposed compulsory retirement at seventy and the power to appoint up to six new justices.
  • Only about 40% of the public backed him. Justice Brandeis, sympathetic to the New Deal, spoke against the plan. The Democratic House more or less refused to handle it; the Senate Judiciary Committee called it a “needless, futile and utterly dangerous abandonment of constitutional principle”, and the Senate voted 70 to 20 to send it back to be stripped of court packing. Social Security and the Labor Relations Act were upheld anyway.
  • Democrats in Congress had every short-term reason to back him. They didn’t, because a president who could capture the judiciary could next rule by decree, as Fujimori in Peru and Chávez in Venezuela did in the 1990s, closing congresses and rewriting constitutions.
  • Argentina shows the other outcome. Its 1853 constitution created a Supreme Court, which took on judicial review in 1887. Juan Perón, elected in 1946 after rising through the 1943 coup, had his deputies impeach four of five justices (one resigned first); his new court upheld the conviction of the opposition leader Ricardo Balbín for disrespecting Perón. Ever since, new presidents, military or civilian, have picked their own courts. In 1990 Carlos Menem, having failed to push justices out (he offered Carlos Fayt an embassy; Fayt sent him his book Law and Ethics inscribed “Beware I wrote this”), expanded the court from five to nine within three months of taking office, then rewrote the constitution to run again. Under extractive institutions, capturing the court is worth the risk.

Positive feedback and virtuous circles

  • The circle has three mechanisms. Pluralism and the rule of law make usurpation hard, as Walpole and Roosevelt found, and the idea of equality before the law invites equality in politics. Inclusive economics removes slavery, serfdom and monopoly, so there is less to gain by seizing power and more to lose by repression. A free press exposes threats and mobilises opposition.
  • The outcome of any given conflict is still contingent, but the tendency is for inclusive institutions to persist and expand. Extractive ones have an equally strong tendency of their own.

Chapter 12

The Vicious Circle

Why extractive institutions persist: new rulers inherit the machine and use it, old elites rebuild it in a new form, and the prize of power invites war. Sierra Leone, Guatemala, the American South and Ethiopia.

You can’t take the train to Bo anymore

  • Britain declared a protectorate over the interior of Sierra Leone on 31 August 1896, turning kings like Suluku of Kono into “paramount chiefs”. When it levied a hut tax of five shillings per house in January 1898, the chiefs rose in the Hut Tax Rebellion, strongest in Mendeland in the south. The railway from Freetown, begun in 1896, was rerouted south through Rotifunk to Bo so troops could reach Mendeland quickly.
  • At independence in 1961 Britain handed power to Sir Milton Margai’s Sierra Leone People’s Party, strongest among the Mende; his brother Albert succeeded him in 1964. In 1967 the All People’s Congress of Siaka Stevens, a Limba from the north, narrowly won. By then the railway carried Mendeland’s coffee, cocoa and diamonds. Stevens reasoned that what was good for the Mende was good for his opponents, pulled up the line to Mendeland, and sold the track and rolling stock so it could never come back. You pass the derelict stations at Hastings and Waterloo; “there are no more trains to Bo.”
  • Stevens made himself president in 1971 and the APC the only party in 1978, and ruled by violence until he handed over to his chosen successor Joseph Momoh in 1985.
  • He kept the colonial machine. The Sierra Leone Produce Marketing Board, set up in 1949 in theory to smooth farmers’ prices, soon paid “a lot less” than the world price. After independence it got worse (see the figure), and under Stevens farmers often got as little as 10%, the rest going not to roads or schools but to Stevens, his cronies and political support. The political scientist Robert Bates found in the 1980s that such pricing, not geography, explained why African farming was so unproductive: farmers had no political power.
  • Indirect rule survived too. Paramount chiefs, elected for life by Tribal Authorities from recognised “ruling houses”, still collect a poll tax, descendant of the hut tax, and are “custodians of the land”: land can’t be bought, sold or mortgaged, and an outsider cannot plant tree crops lest he acquire de facto rights. In 2005 Sandor chose a new paramount chief from its only ruling house: Sheku Fasuluku, great-great-grandson of King Suluku.
  • Indirect rule also created a hereditary aristocracy where chiefs had needed popular support. The Margai family won the Lower Banta chieftaincy by siding with the British against the reigning chief Nyama in 1898, and held it until 2010.
  • Diamonds. Kono’s alluvial diamonds, found in January 1930, could be panned by anyone, “democratic diamonds”. Britain gave a protectorate-wide monopoly, the Sierra Leone Selection Trust, to De Beers, whose private Diamond Protection Force (1936) grew larger than the colonial army; by the 1950s it was overwhelmed by illicit diggers. In 1970 Stevens took 51% of the company.
  • Contrast Australia. Its alluvial gold, found in 1851, might have been fenced and auctioned as James Macarthur, son of John, proposed. Instead anyone with an annual licence could dig, and the diggers became a political force behind universal suffrage and the secret ballot.
  • Stevens could not trust an army, so he cut it down and privatised violence: the Internal Security Unit (“I Shoot U”) and the Special Security Division (“Siaka Stevens’s Dogs”). When the central bank governor Sam Bangura criticised his spending in 1980, he was murdered and thrown from the top floor onto Siaka Stevens Street. In the end it was just thirty soldiers under Captain Valentine Strasser who toppled the APC on 29 April 1992.
  • The mechanisms: extractive economics funds the armies, judges and rigged elections that keep extractive politics in place; extractive politics offers no defence against abuse (Lord Acton: absolute power corrupts absolutely); and unchecked power and great inequality raise the stakes, which is why so many African states fell into civil war to capture the state, from Angola to Uganda.

From encomienda to land grab: Guatemala

  • When Ramiro De León Carpio became president on 14 January 1993, he and his finance and development ministers were all direct descendants of conquistadors (Juan de León Cardona; Bernal Díaz del Castillo, chronicler of the conquest of Mexico). The sociologist Marta Casaús Arzú traced a core of twenty-two families, linked by marriage to twenty-six more, who have held economic and political power since 1531: on a broad definition, just over 1% of the population.
  • Guatemala had perhaps two million Maya at the conquest, a level not regained until the 1920s. The elite lived off the encomienda and the repartimiento (here called the mandamiento) and a merchants’ guild, the Consulado de Comercio, that monopolised trade.
  • Hostile to the Cádiz Constitution, the elite declared independence and ruled through the dictator Rafael Carrera (1839–71) with colonial institutions intact. The Consulado, in charge of infrastructure, refused to build a Pacific port or a road from Los Altos to the Suchitepéquez coast, which would have created rival merchants in Quetzaltenango.
  • Coffee changed the form, not the substance. The “Liberals” who overthrew Carrera in 1871 (Miguel García Granados, then Justo Rufino Barrios from 1873) were mostly the same families. They abolished the Consulado but seized nearly a million acres of mostly indigenous communal and frontier land between 1871 and 1883 and auctioned it to the elite. In November 1876 Barrios told governors to supply planters with fifty or a hundred workers from the Indian towns on request.
  • Decree 177 (1877) let employers draft up to sixty workers for fifteen days in their own department or thirty outside it, renewable. Every rural worker carried a libreta recording his employer and debts; an indebted worker could not leave, and the only exemption from the draft was being in debt. Vagrancy laws caught the rest. The repartimiento lasted until the 1920s, the libreta and vagrancy laws until 1945.
  • Jorge Ubico (1931–44), elected unopposed, opposed industry for the same reason as Francis I and Nicholas I, and made it a jailable offence to say obreros (workers), sindicatos (unions) or huelgas (strikes). He resigned on 1 July 1944 after student protests and the “Memorial de los 311”. The democracy of 1945 fell to a coup in 1954, followed by a murderous civil war; democracy returned only in 1986.

From slavery to Jim Crow: the American South

  • In 1860 the South’s manufacturing output was less than that of Pennsylvania, New York or Massachusetts alone; 9% of southerners lived in towns against 35% in the Northeast; railroad density was a third of the North’s. From 1837 to 1859 corn and wheat averaged twelve and ten patents a year, cotton one.
  • Defeat brought abolition and the vote for black men “at bayonet point”, but the planters “lost the war, but would win the peace”. Freed slaves had been promised forty acres and a mule, and some got it under General Sherman, but President Andrew Johnson revoked his orders in 1865. Congressman George Washington Julian asked what abolition would avail “if the old agricultural basis of aristocratic power shall remain?”
  • The sociologist Jonathan Wiener found that of 236 planter-elite families in five Black Belt counties of Alabama in 1850, 101 were still in the elite in 1870, about the same survival rate as between 1850 and 1860; of the 25 largest landholders of 1870, 18 had been elite in 1860. More than 600,000 died in the war, but the law exempted one slaveholder from service for every twenty slaves.
  • Alabama’s Black Code of 1865, like Guatemala’s Decree 177, combined a vagrancy law with a law against “enticing” workers. Reconstruction (1865–77) ended when Rutherford Hayes, needing southern electoral votes in 1877, agreed to withdraw federal troops. Poll taxes and literacy tests then disenfranchised blacks and many poor whites, creating one-party Democratic rule. W. E. B. Du Bois called the South “simply an armed camp for intimidating black folk.”
  • Alabama’s 1901 constitution required separate schools; Section 256 still says so, and an amendment to strike it was narrowly defeated in 2004. Southern politicians blocked federal projects that might loosen control of black labour. In 1900 the South was 13.5% urban, the Northeast 60%. Only after the Second World War and the civil rights movement did the system crumble and the South begin to converge.

The iron law of oligarchy: Ethiopia

  • Haile Selassie held court in Menelik II’s Grand Palace, where a pillow-bearer with fifty-two pillows made sure his feet never dangled, and ran the country as his property. In 1974 the Derg, 108 officers from units across the country, declared loyalty on 4 July, arrested him on 12 September, declared Ethiopia socialist in December and nationalised land and most property in 1975; the emperor died, probably murdered, on 27 August 1975.
  • Facing revolts in Eritrea and Tigray and a Somali invasion of the Ogaden, the Derg split. Major Mengistu Haile Mariam eliminated his rivals by mid-1977 and was saved by Soviet and Cuban arms and troops. At the 1978 anniversary he sat in a gilded armchair, moved into the Grand Palace, used Selassie’s throne and cars, and compared himself to Emperor Tewodros.
  • His minister Dawit Wolde Giorgis recalled that at first neckties were “considered criminal”; by 1978 designer suits, the best whiskey and champagne were required, and ministers switched from the familiar to the formal “you” when addressing him: “now he had become the new Emperor.”
  • Michels called this the iron law of oligarchy; the authors add Marx’s line that history repeats itself, first as tragedy, then as farce. Stevens controlling the same Mende through the same chiefs and boards; Laurent Kabila building a Mobutu-style personality cult with Mobutu’s own information minister, Dominique Sakombi Inongo; Mengistu in the palace. But the farce was worse than the tragedy: where Selassie was indifferent to the Wollo famine of 1973, Mengistu used famine as a political weapon.
  • Why did 1688 and 1789 escape the law? Three things: new merchants and businessmen who wanted creative destruction; broad coalitions rather than a narrow group; and traditions of parliaments and power-sharing that constrained whoever came next. Sierra Leone, Ethiopia and the Congo had none of these, and the colonial economies had left almost no private business, so the prize of power attracted the most unscrupulous men and “brought the worst out of them”.

Negative feedback and vicious circles

  • The vicious circle comes in three forms: the same elite persists and rebuilds (Guatemala, the American South); new rulers inherit and worsen the machine (Sierra Leone, Ethiopia, the Congo); and the fight for the prize destroys the state itself, as the next chapter shows.
  • It is resilient but not a law of physics: the Glorious Revolution and the Meiji Restoration broke it, because broad coalitions made them.

Chapter 13

Why Nations Fail Today

Seven countries, one diagnosis. Zimbabwe, Sierra Leone, Colombia, Argentina, North Korea, Uzbekistan and Egypt differ in climate, colonisers and culture, and share extractive institutions run for an elite.

How to win the lottery in Zimbabwe

  • In January 2000 the master of ceremonies at the lottery of Zimbank, a partly state-owned bank, drew the Z$100,000 ticket (about five times income per head) and “could hardly believe his eyes” to see “His Excellency RG Mugabe” written on it. Mugabe had recently given himself and his cabinet raises of up to 200%.
  • By 2008 Zimbabwe’s income per head was about half its level at independence in 1980. The state stopped providing basic services; a cholera outbreak in 2008–09 had caused 98,741 reported cases and 4,293 deaths by 10 January 2010, Africa’s deadliest in fifteen years; the UN put unemployment at 94% in early 2009.
  • The roots were colonial. Cecil Rhodes’s British South Africa Company invaded Matabeleland and Mashonaland in 1890; white settlers took the rich farmland, won self-government in 1923 and built an apartheid state on the South African model. In 1965 Ian Smith’s white minority, perhaps 5% of the population, declared independence; sanctions and the guerrilla war of Mugabe’s ZANU and Joshua Nkomo’s ZAPU led to Zimbabwe in 1980.
  • Mugabe eliminated or co-opted rivals. As many as twenty thousand people were killed in Matabeleland, ZAPU’s heartland, in the early 1980s; ZAPU was absorbed into ZANU-PF in 1987; he made himself president, abolished the white voter rolls and in 1990 the Senate, and created seats he could fill himself. He kept the white regime’s price controls, state industries and marketing boards, gave state jobs to supporters, and kept regulation tight so no independent black business class could emerge.
  • When a fiscal crisis forced reform in 1991, the Movement for Democratic Change arose. ZANU-PF won 81% and 118 of 120 seats in 1995 (fifty-five unopposed) and Mugabe 93% in 1996 after his opponents withdrew, but in 2000 the party got 49% and 63 seats and in 2002 Mugabe only 56%, both with violence and fraud. From 2000 he answered with land occupations led by “war veterans”, much of the land going to the party elite; farm output collapsed, he printed money, and hyperinflation followed. From January 2009 other currencies became legal and the Zimbabwe dollar vanished. It was the iron law of oligarchy: Ian Smith’s extraction replaced by Mugabe’s.

A children’s crusade? Sierra Leone

  • On 23 March 1991 Foday Sankoh, a former corporal jailed after a failed coup against Stevens in 1971 and trained in Colonel Qaddafi’s camp in Libya, where he met Charles Taylor, crossed from Liberia with Taylor’s men and attacked Kailahun. His Revolutionary United Front said it had come to overthrow the APC.
  • The state had already collapsed. Stevens liked to quote “the cow eats where it is tethered”, and under Momoh (from 1985) roads and schools fell apart; national television stopped in 1987 when the information minister sold the transmitter; in 1989 the radio relay tower outside Freetown fell down; by the end civil servants, teachers and even paramount chiefs went unpaid. A Freetown paper wrote in 1995 that the rebels and “sobels” (soldiers turned rebels) were symptoms, not causes.
  • The RUF’s manifesto, “Footpaths to Democracy”, opened with Frantz Fanon, but within a year Sankoh was executing critics and recruiting children by force, as the army also did. Massacres, mass rape, amputations of hands and ears, and forced labour in the diamond fields followed on all sides. By the war’s end in 2001 probably eighty thousand people had died. The burned-out, bullet-scarred houses are still standing in Koidu.
  • This is state failure: worse even than Shyaam’s extractive order, a return to the Lele side of the river. It happened across Africa, in Liberia (also Taylor’s doing), Angola, Côte d’Ivoire, the Congo, Mozambique, Somalia, Sudan and Uganda, because extraction makes the state a prize worth fighting for and neglects its most basic services.

Who is the state? Colombia

  • Colombia has held regular elections since military rule ended in 1958 (with the two parties rotating power under the National Front until 1974), but it is not inclusive. In the countryside you pay the vacuna, the “vaccination”, a monthly payment to armed men so they don’t kidnap or kill you.
  • In 1981 FARC guerrillas kidnapped Jesús Castaño, a dairy farmer in Amalfi, Antioquia, and demanded $7,500; the family mortgaged the farm to pay and found him dead, chained to a tree. His sons Carlos, Fidel and Vicente founded a paramilitary group; by 1997 they had united the paramilitaries in the AUC, which by 2001 may have had thirty thousand fighters and lived on drugs, extortion and kidnapping as well as fighting the FARC.
  • At Santa Fe de Ralito, AUC leaders and politicians, national senators among them, signed a pact to “refound the country”. In the 2002 congressional elections the paramilitary leader “Cadena” bused peasants in San Onofre to see the faces of the candidates they had to vote for, and threatened to kill council members drawn from a bag if his candidate lost; each of his candidates got forty thousand votes in Sucre. Probably a third of Congress owed its seats to paramilitaries. Salvatore Mancuso: “35 percent of the Congress was elected in areas where there were states of the Self-Defense groups.”
  • The paramilitaries collected taxes, dispensed justice and seized land, perhaps 10% of all rural land; Mancuso reputedly amassed $25 million of property. A pact imposed by Martín Llanos on six mayors in oil-rich Casanare required them to hand over 50% of the municipal budget and 10% of every contract. In early 2010 about 4.5 million Colombians, some 10% of the population, were internally displaced.
  • It persists because it suits national politicians. Álvaro Uribe, whose father was killed by the FARC, won 3 points more in paramilitary areas in 2002 and 11 more in 2006, after paramilitary-backed legislators changed the constitution to let him run again. In return he passed a lenient demobilisation law that, the authors say, institutionalised paramilitarism rather than ending it.

El Corralito: Argentina

  • To end hyperinflation Carlos Menem fixed the peso at one dollar by law in 1991 and encouraged dollar bank accounts. Exports dried up, imports and borrowing soared, and people moved their savings into dollars for safety.
  • On 1 December 2001 the government froze all accounts, allowing weekly withdrawals of 250 and then 300 pesos from peso accounts only: el corralito, “the little corral”. In January the peso was devalued to about four to the dollar, and dollar accounts were forcibly converted into pesos at one to one. Someone with $1,000 saved now had $250: the state had expropriated three-quarters of people’s savings.
  • Simon Kuznets said there were four kinds of countries: developed, underdeveloped, Japan and Argentina. The authors’ answer: Argentina’s fifty years of growth before 1914, on beef, hides and grain under a narrow elite, was growth under extractive institutions. Coups began in 1930; military rule in the 1970s killed at least nine thousand people. Civilian rule meant the Peronist machine, buying votes with patronage, with power concentrated and few constraints.
  • Buenos Aires looks unlike Lima or Guatemala City, but Menem came from Anillaco in La Rioja, once an Inca outpost of encomiendas that bred mules for Potosí and produced the nineteenth-century warlord Facundo Quiroga. Argentina’s institutions rest on a truce: the interior’s warlords left Buenos Aires to make money, and Buenos Aires left the interior unreformed.
  • Latin American democracy often yields strongmen (Perón, the Kirchners, Chávez) because centuries of exclusion leave voters willing to back anyone who seems to be on their side. Chávez, firing opponents and closing papers, was far less constrained than Walpole was in 1724: “Huntridge would have fared much less well in present-day Venezuela or Argentina.”

The new absolutism: North Korea

  • In November 2009 North Korea knocked two zeros off the won, like France in 1960, but gave people one week to exchange, and no one could convert more than 100,000 won (about $40 on the black market), later 500,000. It wiped out much of private wealth, probably more than Argentina took in 2002, and punished black-market traders before they grew rich enough to be dangerous.
  • The elite like what markets produce. Kim Jong-Il has a seven-storey pleasure palace with a bar, karaoke, a cinema and a wave pool he rides on a motorised body board; one scholar used Hennessy’s sales to estimate his cognac budget at up to $800,000 a year. US sanctions in 2006 banned the export to North Korea of more than sixty luxury items, from yachts and jet skis to televisions over twenty-nine inches.
  • Communism, the authors write, was the new absolutism of the twentieth century. Lenin made his party the new elite; Stalin’s collectivisation and purges may have killed as many as forty million; Mao, the Khmer Rouge and North Korea brought famine and collapse. Different in form, the same in effect as Zimbabwe’s or Sierra Leone’s.

King Cotton: Uzbekistan

  • Cotton is about 45% of Uzbek exports. After 1991 the 2,048 Soviet state farms were broken up, but Islam Karimov’s government sets what farmers plant (35% of their land must be cotton) and pays a fraction of the world price. Farmers stopped maintaining combine harvesters, which had picked 40% of the crop at independence, so Karimov found a cheaper option: schoolchildren.
  • Each September schools empty; in 2006 some 2.7 million children were sent to pick for two months, each with a daily quota of 20 to 60 kilos, publicly shamed if they fell short, sleeping in sheds if far from home. With cotton at about $1.40 a kilo in 2006, they were paid about $0.03 for the day’s quota. Probably 75% of the harvest is picked by children, and schools close again in spring for weeding.
  • Karimov, the Soviet first secretary for Uzbekistan from 1989, reinvented himself as a nationalist, won the first presidential election in December 1991 with the security forces behind him, jailed or exiled the opposition, and in 2005 had perhaps 750 or more demonstrators killed at Andijon. He won 91.2% in 2000 (his opponent said he had voted for Karimov) and 88% in 2007, elections worthy of Stalin, whose 1937 vote Pravda described as a thrilling 100%.
  • About a third of Uzbeks are poor, on an average income of around $1,000. His daughter Gulnora runs the family’s interests. When the American tea company Interspan reached 30% of the market by 2005, its staff began to be arrested, beaten and tortured; it left in August 2006, and the Karimov tea interests went from 2% of the market to 67%.

Keeping the playing field at an angle: Egypt

  • After 1954 Egypt was quasi-socialist; in the 1990s it opened markets, but to businessmen allied with Sadat’s National Democratic Party. Under Mubarak (1981–2011), the former head of Unilever’s African and Middle Eastern arm became minister of trade and industry, the owner of one of Egypt’s biggest travel firms minister of tourism, and the founder of the largest cotton exporter minister of agriculture.
  • Regulation fenced off media, steel, cars, alcohol and cement. Ahmed Ezz, whose company made 70% of Egypt’s steel, chaired the parliament’s budget committee and was close to Gamal Mubarak. Privatisation, as in Mexico, turned state monopolies into private ones for the thirty-two “whales” who dominated the economy.
  • In 1996 the beer monopoly Al Ahram Beverages was privatised. A well-connected consortium’s bid of 400 million Egyptian pounds was rejected as too low. Ahmed Zayat’s Luxor Group, better connected, bought 74.9% of the shares floated in London at 68.5 pounds, sold them after a two-for-one split at 52.5 each for a 36% profit, and used that to buy the company for 231 million pounds, when it earned about 41.3 million a year and held 93 million in cash. In 1999 it bought the wine monopoly Gianaclis, protected by a 3,000% tariff with 70% margins. In 2002 Zayat sold to Heineken for 1.3 billion pounds: 563% profit in five years.
  • Mohamed Nosseir bought the Coca-Cola bottling monopoly in 1993 with little competition, thanks to his ties to the minister Atef Ebeid, and sold it two years later for more than three times the price. Around 40% of Egyptians live on less than two dollars a day. Nasser’s partly inclusive economics under extractive politics was an unstable mix that Mubarak’s era resolved toward extraction, and the Arab Spring, in Egypt and in Ben Ali’s Tunisia, was in part the reaction.

Why nations fail

  • Tropical and temperate, once British, Japanese, Spanish or Russian: what these countries share is an elite that designs institutions to enrich itself and keep power. The elites differ (a liberation party in Zimbabwe, a communist clique in North Korea, a family in Uzbekistan, fragmented and murderous in Colombia) and so do the tools. Egypt’s officers could switch from central planning to crony capitalism when the Cold War allegiance changed; even crony capitalism leaves some incentive to invest, which is one reason Egypt is richer than North Korea.
  • Degrees matter: Argentina’s state at least holds a monopoly of violence, and its income per head is double Colombia’s; both are far richer than Zimbabwe and Sierra Leone.
  • Even collapse doesn’t end the circle. In 2007 Stevens’s APC returned to power by election under Ernest Bai Koroma, with many old APC figures in the cabinet and two of Stevens’s sons made ambassadors to the United States and Germany.
  • The solution is to make extractive institutions inclusive. It is hard but not impossible: pre-existing inclusive elements, a broad coalition, or plain luck, as in 1688, can break the circle.

Chapter 14

Breaking the Mold

Three escapes from the vicious circle, Botswana, the American South and China after Mao, each made at a critical juncture, each helped by existing institutions and by luck.

Three African chiefs

  • On 6 September 1895 three chiefs, Khama of the Ngwato, Bathoen of the Ngwaketse and Sebele of the Kwena, landed at Plymouth and took the 8:10 express to Paddington. Their eight Tswana states made up Bechuanaland, a British protectorate since 1885 that Britain governed as little as possible; the high commissioner said it cared about the territory only “as a road to the interior”.
  • Cecil Rhodes’s British South Africa Company, having taken the lands that became Zambia and Zimbabwe from 1889, now wanted Bechuanaland. The chiefs chose the lesser evil, more British control, and with the London Missionary Society’s help met the colonial secretary Joseph Chamberlain on 11 September, then toured the country speaking in Windsor, Reading, Southampton, Leicester, Birmingham, Sheffield, Leeds, Halifax, Bradford, Bristol, Manchester and Liverpool.
  • Rhodes was meanwhile preparing the disastrous Jameson Raid on the Transvaal against Chamberlain’s wishes. On 6 November Chamberlain promised to take only the land needed for a railway, fenced; the Colonial Office confirmed the chiefs would “rule their own people much as at present.” Rhodes cabled: “I do object to being beaten by three canting natives.”
  • What they protected was unusual. The Tswana had real political centralisation and a primitive pluralism. In the kgotla, the assembly of adult men, the anthropologist Isaac Schapera wrote, all tribal policy was settled, anyone could speak, and “it is not unknown for the tribal assembly to overrule the wishes of the chief.” John Comaroff showed that the rules of succession were bent to remove bad chiefs and seat able ones. A proverb put it: kgosi ke kgosi ka morafe, “the king is king by the grace of the people.”
  • Colonial rule brought nothing. In the 1950s Quett Masire, later president, grew improved sorghum and tried to rent two rail trucks at Lobatse to ship it to a mill across the border; the station master refused, then, after a white friend intervened, charged him four times the white rate.
  • The chiefs made their own luck: they could go to London because they had unusual authority, and perhaps legitimacy, from their institutions. Bechuanaland escaped both Rhodes and the kind of indirect rule that ruined Sierra Leone.

Independence, and diamonds

  • At independence in 1966 Botswana was one of the poorest countries in the world (see the figure), surrounded by hostile white regimes in South Africa, Namibia and Rhodesia. Over the next forty-five years it became one of the fastest-growing countries in the world, with the highest income per head in sub-Saharan Africa, level with Estonia, Hungary and Costa Rica. It has held regular competitive elections and never had a civil war or a coup.
  • The parallel with England in 1688: some centralisation, pluralistic institutions that survived colonialism (Britain ran the protectorate from Mafeking in South Africa, and the new capital Gaborone was planned with new kgotlas), and a coalition with a stake in property, the chiefs and elites who owned the main asset, cattle, which unlike land was private property.
  • The Botswana Democratic Party, founded in 1960 by Masire and Seretse Khama (grandson of Khama III; Seretse means “the clay that binds together”), drew in most chiefs. Its Botswana Meat Commission (1967), unlike other African marketing boards, built the cattle trade, fencing against foot-and-mouth disease and promoting exports.
  • The chiefs had blocked mineral prospecting under colonial rule, knowing a find would end their autonomy. When the first big diamond discovery was made under Ngwato land, Seretse Khama’s own homeland, he had the law changed before it was announced so that all subsoil minerals belonged to the nation, not the tribe. Diamond revenue built the state, roads and schools instead of funding wars.
  • The Chieftaincy Act (1965) and its 1970 amendment took land allocation from the chiefs and let the president remove a chief. Only Setswana and English were taught in schools. The 1946 census had shown great diversity (only 20% of the Ngwato reserve identified as pure Ngwato); no census since has asked, “because in Botswana everyone is Tswana.”
  • And contingency: Seretse Khama and Quett Masire were not Siaka Stevens and Robert Mugabe. They contested power in elections rather than subverting them.

The end of the southern extraction

  • At 6:06 p.m. on 1 December 1955 the bus driver James Blake in Montgomery, Alabama, had Rosa Parks, secretary of the local NAACP, arrested for sitting in the white section; she was fined $10 plus $4 costs. By 3 December Martin Luther King Jr. and other leaders had organised the Montgomery Bus Boycott, which lasted until 20 December 1956 and ended in a Supreme Court ruling that the segregation laws were unconstitutional.
  • The economics had shifted. Black migration out of the South averaged a hundred thousand people a year in the 1940s and 1950s, and cotton picking, almost all by hand in 1950, was nearly half mechanised in Alabama, Louisiana and Mississippi by 1960. Black labour was harder to trap and less indispensable, so the elite fought less hard, though it still fought.
  • The juncture was the organisation of southern blacks combined with the fact that the South sat inside the inclusive federal system, which Guatemala’s peasants lacked. The Supreme Court struck down white-only primaries in 1944, school segregation in Brown v. Board of Education (1954) and rural over-representation in Baker v. Carr (1962, “one person, one vote”). Literacy tests had been rigged: in Louisiana in the early 1960s a white applicant passed with the answer “FRDUM FOOF SPETGH”.
  • The Democratic Party’s non-southern wing turned against segregation. Strom Thurmond’s States’ Rights Democrats carried four states and thirty-nine electoral votes in 1948, promising there were “not enough troops in the army” to force desegregation. In 1962, when federal courts ordered the University of Mississippi to admit James Meredith, an air force veteran, and Governor Ross Barnett refused, marshals were attacked by a crowd of about 2,500; 20,000 troops and 11,000 National Guardsmen were sent to Oxford and 300 people arrested. Meredith graduated under guard.
  • Thurmond filibustered the Civil Rights Act of 1957 for twenty-four hours and eighteen minutes, reading from the Declaration of Independence to phone books. It passed, followed by the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, which outlawed literacy tests and poll taxes and put state elections under federal oversight.

Rebirth in China

  • The People’s Republic (1 October 1949) was highly extractive: one party, land nationalised, landlords executed, markets abolished, communal farms paid in “work points”, internal passports from 1956, Soviet-style five-year plans, and a state monopoly on grain used to tax farmers.
  • The Great Leap Forward (from 1958) promised to double steel output in a year from backyard furnaces and catch Britain in fifteen years. Peasants melted down pots, hoes and ploughs; the famine killed between twenty and forty million people, and income per head fell by about a quarter.
  • Deng Xiaoping, a revolutionary general who had led an “anti-rightist” campaign, drew the lesson at Guangzhou in 1961: “No matter whether the cat is black or white, if it catches mice, it’s a good cat.” The Cultural Revolution, launched on 16 May 1966, made him the “number-two capitalist roader”; he was jailed in 1967 and sent to a tractor factory in Jiangxi in 1969. Mao said of the violence: “The more people you kill, the more revolutionary you are.”
  • Rehabilitated in 1974 as first vice-premier, Deng drafted papers in 1975 calling for better universities, material incentives and the removal of leftists, but the Gang of Four, led by Mao’s wife Jiang Qing, blamed him for the Tiananmen demonstrations of 5 April 1976 honouring Zhou Enlai, and he was dismissed again. Hua Guofeng became acting premier.
  • Mao died in September 1976: the critical juncture. Within a month Hua had the Gang of Four arrested; he reinstated Deng in March 1977. Hua’s line was the “Two Whatevers”: uphold whatever Mao decided and follow whatever he instructed. In September 1978 Deng attacked it with “seek truth from facts”, used the Democracy Wall to pressure Hua, and through his ally Hu Qiaomu proposed firm autonomy, market prices and less state control.
  • Deng did not want to abolish communism; he believed growth was possible without endangering the Party’s control, since all opposition had been crushed. The Third Plenum of December 1978 made economic modernisation, not class struggle, the Party’s focus and began experiments with the “household responsibility system”. Hua lost the premiership to Zhao Ziyang in 1980 and his Central Committee seat in 1982. Between 1980 and 1985, twenty-one of twenty-six Politburo members, eight of eleven secretariat members and ten of eighteen vice-premiers were replaced.
  • Then the economics: household responsibility everywhere by 1983; compulsory grain purchases replaced by contracts and farm prices freed in 1985; fourteen “open cities” for foreign investment. Grain output was a third higher in 1984 than in 1978 with fewer people farming, as workers moved to Township Village Enterprises allowed to compete with state firms from 1979. Privatisation waited until the mid-1990s.
  • China broke the mold economically without transforming its politics: growth under extractive political institutions. And it was highly contingent: had the Gang of Four won, there would have been no thirty years of growth.

What the three have in common

  • Like 1688, 1789 and the Meiji Restoration, they show that history is not destiny. But the escape needs a confluence: a critical juncture, a broad coalition or helpful existing institutions, and luck.

Chapter 15

Understanding Prosperity and Poverty

The theory restated, what it predicts, why authoritarian growth, modernisation, engineered reform and foreign aid all disappoint, and why the only real route is the empowerment of a broad coalition.

Historical origins

  • Most of today’s gaps are recent. Five hundred years ago the Aztec state was richer than anything to the north; the United States pulled ahead of Mexico only in the nineteenth century; the two Koreas were indistinguishable before 1945. Was a world where Peru has the Glorious Revolution and colonises Europe possible, or just science fiction?
  • The authors say they chose a simple theory on purpose, not believing it explains everything but because a theory should clarify the main forces. It works on two levels: the distinction between inclusive and extractive institutions, and the historical account of why inclusive ones arose in some places.
  • They restate the core. Extractive institutions can grow, but not for long, for two reasons: sustained growth needs creative destruction, which the elite fears; and the prize of power makes such regimes unstable. Both kinds of institution form circles, but neither circle is absolute. Change comes when critical junctures meet differences created by institutional drift, and drift is not cumulative: as Roman Britain showed, small differences open, close and reopen.
  • So there was no historical necessity that Peru be poorer than Europe. At least three things could have gone differently. The institutional innovations that produced the Inca state could have happened in the Mississippi valley instead, reversing the roles of Peru and the United States. The Incas might have resisted colonisation and modernised, as Japan did after Perry. And Europe itself was not bound to colonise the world: its lead came from contingent turns (feudalism replacing slavery, independent cities, monarchs who didn’t fear trade as the Ming did, the Black Death).

What the theory predicts

  • Contingency limits prediction: few would have picked Britain in the fifteenth century, or China during the Cultural Revolution. But the circles create persistence, so in fifty or a hundred years the United States and western Europe will still be much richer than sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, Central America or Southeast Asia, with some countries breaking the mold in between.
  • Countries with almost no centralisation, such as Somalia and Afghanistan, or a collapsed state like Haiti (even before its 2010 earthquake), are unlikely to grow. Those with some centralisation are likely to grow, if under extractive institutions: Burundi, Ethiopia, Rwanda and Tanzania in Africa; Brazil, Chile and Mexico in Latin America, which also have nascent pluralism. Sustained growth “is very unlikely in Colombia.”
  • China’s growth under extractive politics will run out of steam. Cuba, North Korea and Burma could go either way. And the fall of Mubarak in February 2011 does not guarantee that Egypt will become inclusive: extractive institutions may re-create themselves.

The irresistible charm of authoritarian growth

  • Dai Guofang foresaw China’s building boom and in 2003, with the backing of local party bosses in Changzhou, began building a low-cost steel giant, Jiangsu Tieben. In March 2004 Beijing stopped it and arrested him; after five years of jail and house arrest he was convicted of a minor charge in 2009. His real crime was competing with state firms without approval.
  • Chen Yun, architect of the early reforms, likened the economy to a bird in a cage: the cage could be enlarged, never opened. Jiang Zemin, on becoming general secretary in 1989, called entrepreneurs “self-employed traders and peddlers [who] cheat, embezzle, bribe and evade taxation.” The journalist Richard McGregor reports a red phone on the desk of each big state-company boss, on which the Party calls with orders.
  • China has made great strides toward inclusive economic institutions, but its growth relies on adopting existing technology and heavy investment, not creative destruction. Property is insecure (as Dai found), labour mobility is controlled, and business needs Party patrons, who in turn let it evict people and break laws. After Tiananmen in 1989 politics grew more extractive: Zhao Ziyang, who backed the students, spent more than fifteen years under house arrest and was erased from the record; Liu Xiaobo stayed in prison after his Nobel Peace Prize; and in 2009 the censors even blocked selected New York Times and Financial Times stories about corruption charges involving Hu Jintao’s son.
  • The authors’ verdict: growth by catch-up, imported technology and low-end exports will continue for a while, but will likely end once China reaches middle income, unless it moves to inclusive political institutions first, which is neither likely nor automatic. Premier Wen Jiabao has warned the same. China grew “not thanks to its extractive political institutions, but despite them.”
  • Modernisation theory, from Seymour Martin Lipset, holds that growth and education bring democracy (Thomas Friedman: enough McDonald’s and democracy follows). George H. W. Bush’s China policy (“Trade freely with China and time is on our side”) and hopes for post-Saddam Iraq rested on it. The authors call it wrong: rich democracies are rich because of inclusive institutions, not the reverse; fast-growing authoritarian states haven’t democratised; resource booms in Gabon, Russia, Saudi Arabia and Venezuela won’t do it; and rich, educated Germany and Japan turned to Nazism and militarism, while Argentina, as rich as Britain in the nineteenth century, had coup after coup.

You can’t engineer prosperity

  • The ignorance hypothesis still rules Western policy. The first kind of engineering is the reform list, such as the Washington consensus: stabilisation, smaller government, flexible exchange rates, privatisation, anti-corruption. Sensible alone, it fails because it never asks why bad policies exist, so reforms are not adopted, not implemented, or subverted.
  • Central bank independence is the example. Mugabe made Zimbabwe’s central bank independent in 1995, when inflation was about 20%; it reached 140% in 2002, almost 600% in 2003, 66,000% in 2007 and 230 million percent in 2008. Argentina and Colombia made theirs independent in the 1990s and inflation did fall, but politicians switched to borrowing and spending to reward their followers.
  • The second kind, now in vogue, fixes “micro-market failures” one at a time. In 2006 the NGO Seva Mandir and a group of economists put time clocks in health centres in Udaipur, Rajasthan, where nurses rarely turned up. Attendance jumped, then within little more than a year the local health administration undermined the scheme with official “exempt days” and broken clocks it would not replace. The failure was not ignorance; the administrators were in league with the absent nurses.

The failure of foreign aid

  • After the Taliban fell and the Bonn Agreement of December 2001 put Hamid Karzai in charge, aid poured into Kabul. The first money paid for an airline for UN and aid officials; the few English-speaking bureaucrats and teachers were hired as drivers and interpreters at many times Afghan salaries, stripping the state the aid was meant to build.
  • A multimillion-dollar shelter programme for a remote central valley delivered a few wooden beams, trucked by the cartel of the former warlord Ismail Khan and too big to use; the villagers burned them. The money had passed through the UN (20% for Geneva overheads), an NGO (20% for Brussels) and about three more layers each taking about 20%, and what was left bought wood from Iran at inflated transport prices.
  • Many studies estimate only 10–20% of aid reaches its target. Much of the rest went to overheads, or to dictators like Mobutu. Conditional aid, such as the Bush administration’s Millennium Challenge Accounts, has worked no better: needier countries that fail the conditions still get aid, and no Stevens or Mobutu would dismantle his power for a little more money.
  • Yet the authors don’t want aid ended: it would be callous, and if ten cents in the dollar reaches the poorest, that is ten cents more. Humanitarian aid and some school-building (particularly for Afghan girls) have done real good. Their suggestion: structure aid so that it brings excluded groups into decisions, rather than making it conditional on rulers’ concessions.

Empowerment

  • On 12 May 1978, workers at the Scania truck plant in São Bernardo, São Paulo, downed tools, although strikes had been banned since the 1964 coup, after learning that the military government had been understating inflation. The metalworkers’ union president, a thirty-three-year-old named Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, refused to send them back. “In struggling for wages,” he said later, “the working class won a political victory.”
  • Fernando Henrique Cardoso had argued in 1973 that democracy would come from a “reactivation of civil society”: unions, churches, student groups, professional associations. On 18 August 1979 union leaders, opposition politicians, students, intellectuals and a hundred social movements met in São Paulo, and in October the Workers’ Party was launched at the São Judas Tadeo restaurant in São Bernardo.
  • It won two mayoralties in 1982 and thirty-six municipalities, including São Paulo and Porto Alegre, by 1988; Porto Alegre’s “participatory budgeting” became a world model. Lula took 16% and then 44% in the 1989 presidential election, lost to Cardoso in 1994 and 1998, and won in 2002.
  • Brazil’s poverty rate fell from 45% in 1990 to 30% in 2006, inequality fell sharply, average schooling rose from six years in 1995 to eight in 2006, and Brazil became a BRIC. It was not engineered by economists, bought with aid, or the natural fruit of modernisation. Venezuela, democratic since 1958 without grassroots empowerment, kept patronage and ended up electing Chávez.
  • Revolutions that succeeded (1688, 1789, 1868) and gradual changes that did (North America, nineteenth-century Britain, Botswana) all empowered a broad cross-section of society; those that didn’t (the Bolsheviks, China, Cuba, Vietnam, Nasser, Mugabe) replaced one elite with another. There is no recipe, but some centralised order, some pre-existing pluralism and civil-society organisations help.
  • So does the media. Pamphlets fed 1688 and 1789; blogs, Facebook and Twitter fed Iran’s 2009 protests and the Arab Spring. Fujimori, who staged a coup from office in 1992, and his spymaster Vladimiro Montesinos kept videotaped records of their bribes (see the figure). A general on one tape: “If we do not control the television we do not do anything.” A Chinese commentator listed the Party’s three principles as control of the army, the cadres and the news. But media help only at the margins: change comes when a broad segment of society organises to make institutions inclusive, not to capture them.

Context

What held up, fourteen years on

Chapter 15 makes explicit forecasts, and other chapters make judgements that events have since tested. The verdicts are our assessment as of 2026; the events are well documented.

Held

Egypt’s revolution may re-create extractive institutions 15

The army removed the elected president, Mohamed Morsi, in July 2013. Abdel Fattah el-Sisi has ruled since 2014, and constitutional changes in 2019 let him stay to 2030.

Held

Somalia, Afghanistan and Haiti are unlikely to grow 15

The Taliban retook Afghanistan in August 2021. Haiti’s president was assassinated in July 2021 and armed gangs came to control most of the capital. Somalia still depends on foreign troops.

Held

Aid cannot install institutions 15

Twenty years and vast sums in Afghanistan ended with the state dissolving within days in August 2021.

Held

Growth and trade will not democratise China 15

China grew richer and more closed. Xi Jinping recentralised power from 2012, term limits were abolished in 2018, and Hong Kong’s national security law followed in 2020.

Held

Chávez’s Venezuela is extractive, not a new path 13, 15

Venezuela’s economy shrank by roughly three-quarters between 2013 and 2021, and nearly eight million people left the country.

Partly

China’s extractive growth will run out of steam 5, 15

Growth fell from about 10% a year to about 5% by the mid-2020s, and the property sector unwound after 2021, as predicted. But China now leads in electric vehicles, batteries and solar panels and contests the lead in AI, frontier innovation the book said extractive institutions would not produce. This is where a reader should push hardest.

Partly

Ethiopia, Rwanda, Tanzania and Burundi will grow 15

Ethiopia, Rwanda and Tanzania grew fast through the 2010s. Ethiopia’s boom ended in civil war in Tigray (2020–22) and a debt default (2023), the instability the book warns extractive growth breeds. Burundi stagnated after its 2015 political crisis.

Partly

Brazil, Chile and Mexico will grow; Brazil has broken the mold 15

Brazil fell into its deepest recession on record in 2014–16, and its income per head a decade later was barely above its 2013 level. But its institutions held: they survived the riot in Brasília on 8 January 2023, and the Supreme Court convicted former president Bolsonaro of plotting a coup in 2025. Chile and Mexico grew slowly.

Partly

Sustained growth in Colombia is “very unlikely” 15

Colombia grew at an ordinary pace, not a take-off, but it signed a peace agreement with the FARC in 2016 and transferred power peacefully to its first left-wing president in 2022.

Partly

The virtuous circle protects rich democracies 11

The authors themselves revised this. The Narrow Corridor (2019) recasts liberty as a precarious balance between state and society that has to be defended, not a circle that runs on its own.

Since the book

  • Botswana passed the hardest test of Chapter 14’s account: in October 2024 the Botswana Democratic Party lost power for the first time since independence, and conceded peacefully.
  • Zimbabwe repeated Chapter 12’s iron law. The army removed Mugabe in November 2017, and his former deputy, Emmerson Mnangagwa, replaced him.
  • Uzbekistan: after Islam Karimov died in 2016, the forced labour of children in the cotton harvest described in Chapter 13 was dismantled. International monitors found the harvest free of systemic forced labour in 2021, and the global cotton boycott was lifted in 2022.
  • The research programme: Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in economics.

Context

The critics

Jared Diamond: geography works through institutions (2012)

In the New York Review of Books (June 2012) Diamond agreed that institutions matter but argued that geography shapes them, through tropical disease, the productivity of soils and how long a society had lived under a state, so the book had dismissed geography too quickly. The authors replied, and neither side moved. The Diamond summary on this site is the other half of the argument.

Jeffrey Sachs: selected history, and fast-growing autocracies (2012)

In Foreign Affairs (September/October 2012) Sachs called the book monocausal, argued that geography, technology and public health drive development independently, and pointed out that South Korea, Taiwan and China all grew fast under authoritarian rule. The book’s own answer is Chapter 5: that is growth under extractive institutions, and it does not last.

Francis Fukuyama: the categories are too broad (2012)

Fukuyama, whose The Origins of Political Order (2011) covers much of the same ground, welcomed the book but thought “inclusive” and “extractive” lump too much together, and that it pays too little attention to how states are built before they are made accountable.

Arvind Subramanian: which nations failed? (2012)

In a long review in The American Interest the economist argued that China’s rise, fast and sustained under one-party rule, sits awkwardly with the theory, and that the book explains the past better than it predicts the future.

Bill Gates: vague and simplistic (2013)

Gates enjoyed the stories but found the key terms too loose to test and the verdict on China unconvincing. The authors replied on their blog, defending both.

Is it falsifiable?

Institutions can be labelled inclusive partly by their results, and critical junctures are clearest in hindsight, which gives the framework a lot of room after the fact. Edward Glaeser and co-authors (“Do Institutions Cause Growth?”, 2004) argued that education comes first and that institutions improve as countries grow. David Albouy (2012) challenged the settler-mortality data behind the authors’ 2001 paper, the programme’s empirical foundation; they defended it.

Ideas, not institutions (2016)

Deirdre McCloskey (Bourgeois Equality, 2016) argued that property rights and courts existed long before 1700 in many places, and that what changed in Holland and Britain was an idea: new dignity and liberty for ordinary commerce and innovation.

The authors’ own second thoughts

The 2012 book treats the virtuous circle as nearly self-sustaining. The Narrow Corridor (2019) recasts liberty as a race between state and society that must be run continuously, and Acemoglu and Johnson’s Power and Progress (2023) takes on technology that concentrates power in inclusive economies. Read them as the sequel.

Terms of art

Glossary

Inclusive economic institutions
Secure property for most people, unbiased law, public services and free entry and choice of career: the conditions for broad participation in the economy.
Extractive economic institutions
Rules “designed to extract incomes and wealth from one subset of society to benefit a different subset.”
Inclusive political institutions
Those that are both pluralistic and sufficiently centralised. If either fails, they are extractive.
Pluralism
Political power held by a broad coalition or many groups, and constrained.
Political centralisation
A state able to keep order across its territory; Weber’s monopoly of legitimate violence.
Absolutism
Power that is narrow and unconstrained by law or other groups.
Creative destruction
Schumpeter’s term for growth that replaces old firms, skills and technologies, and with them old power.
Growth under extractive institutions
Growth an elite achieves by directing resources into productive uses it controls; real, but without innovation and unstable.
Critical juncture
“A major event or confluence of factors disrupting the existing economic or political balance in society.”
Institutional drift
The slow, non-cumulative accumulation of small differences between similar societies, like genetic drift.
Contingency
The role of chance and choice at junctures: the Armada’s weather, Mao’s succession, Seretse Khama.
Reversal of fortune
The flip after 1500: the richest colonised lands became the poorest, because Europeans could build extractive institutions there.
Virtuous circle
The positive feedback that keeps inclusive institutions in place and widens them.
Vicious circle
The negative feedback that keeps extractive institutions in place, often under new rulers.
Iron law of oligarchy
Robert Michels’s observation that those who overthrow an elite become one.
Rule of law
Laws applied equally to all, rulers included; distinct from rule by law, and a product of pluralism.
Broad coalition
An alliance of diverse interests, like 1688’s gentry, merchants and manufacturers, that needs pluralism to keep its members from dominating one another.
Ignorance hypothesis
The view that poor countries are poor because their rulers don’t know the right policies.
Encomienda, mita, repartimiento, trajín
Spanish colonial systems of granted labour and tribute, the labour draft, forced sales and forced portering.
Commenda
Venice’s one-voyage partnership between a capital-owning and a travelling merchant; banned after the Serrata.
La Serrata
“The Closure” of Venice’s Great Council to new families, 1297–1315.
Second Serfdom
The tightening of serfdom in eastern Europe after the Black Death and with western demand for grain.
Dual economy
Arthur Lewis’s modern and traditional sectors; in South Africa, a division created by law.
Marketing board
A state monopoly buyer of crops, set up to smooth prices, used to tax farmers.
Indirect rule
Colonial government through chiefs made hereditary and accountable upward, not to their people.
Kgotla
The Tswana assembly of adult men where policy was settled and chiefs could be overruled.
Empowerment
The organisation of a broad segment of society into political power: the authors’ only general route to inclusive institutions.

Using it

Reading it

The book repeats its thesis at the start and end of most chapters, so it reads quickly and survives being dipped into. If you have a few hours:

  1. Preface and Chapters 1–3

    Tahrir Square, Nogales, the three failed theories and the definitions. The core of the book.

  2. Chapters 4 and 7

    The Black Death and 1688: how critical junctures work, and why England.

  3. Chapter 5

    Stalin, King Shyaam, the Natufians and the Maya: growth under extraction, and its limits.

  4. Chapters 11 and 12

    The two circles: the Black Act and Roosevelt’s court plan against Sierra Leone and Ethiopia.

  5. Chapter 15

    China, modernisation theory, aid and Brazil: what the authors think follows.

  6. Chapters 6, 8–10, 13–14

    The historical middle and the case gallery: rich, vivid and safely skimmed after the core.

Read alongside

  • The Causes of Portuguese Backwardness, summarised on this site: the same mechanism, a windfall that lets a crown stop asking its parliament for money, traced through one country with much better data.
