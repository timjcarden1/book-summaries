Man acts.

The action axiom. About 1,370 pages of economic theory are deduced from this one sentence and two small empirical postulates.

Man, Economy, and State with Power and Market

Murray N. Rothbard · 1926–1995

The whole book in one breath

Rothbard starts from one proposition he takes to be undeniable: people act, using scarce means to pursue ends. From that, plus two modest assumptions (people and resources differ; leisure is valued), he deduces economic theory step by step: value, exchange, money, prices, interest, capital, profit and loss. The market that results is a web of voluntary trades, each expected to benefit both sides. Interest comes from the universal preference for sooner over later, capital goods are way stations in a structure of production that saving lengthens, and a “monopoly price” cannot even be identified on a free market. Power and Market then brings in force. Price controls, licences, tariffs, taxes, subsidies, state enterprises and inflation each leave at least one person worse off and usually hurt their own supporters in the end. Even police and courts, he argues, could be sold like insurance. Economics can’t choose your values, he concludes, but it can show that the market principle produces harmony and prosperity and the hegemonic principle produces conflict and poverty.

Origins

A textbook that became a treatise

The project began as a textbook for the William Volker Fund. Murray Rothbard, a young regular at Ludwig von Mises’s seminar at New York University, told the Fund in 1951 that he meant “to do for Mises, what McCulloch did for Ricardo”: make Human Action (1949) readable for students and lay readers. He began work on January 1, 1952. According to Joseph Salerno’s introduction to the 2009 edition, he soon dropped the plan for three reasons. The textbook format kept coming out disorganized. He found “a lot of gaps” in Mises that had to be filled, and he thought Mises’s theory of monopoly price was flawed beyond repair. And the job needed the whole older tradition, not just “a paraphrase of Human Action.” On May 5, 1959 he wrote to Mises: “È finito!” It is finished.

What he delivered was the first systematic treatise on economic “principles” since the First World War. His 1962 preface says the genre died with that war: the last treatises were Philip Wicksteed’s (1910), Frank Taussig’s (1911) and Frank Fetter’s (1915), and the only real exception since was Human Action. Rothbard set out to “fill part of the enormous gap of 40 years.” The core of the book is production and capital: five of the twelve chapters, about 30 per cent of the text. They join the Böhm-Bawerk and Hayek picture of production as a structure in time to the Fetter and Mises theory that interest comes from time preference alone.

The manuscript was too long. An editorial decision cut the treatise to two volumes, and the part on government, written as a third volume, appeared separately eight years later as Power and Market (Joseph Stromberg tells the story in the Scholar’s Edition). What remained in 1962 was a single chapter, chapter 12, which the preface calls a “necessarily brief” survey. Read together, the two books form one argument. Part I describes the market as it would work if no one used force. Part II adds force and traces its effects.

Method

Economics as logic, not lab work

Rothbard follows Mises in treating economics as a branch of praxeology, the general science of human action. Its laws aren’t hypotheses to be tested against data. They are deduced from premises known to be true, so the conclusions are true as long as each step of the reasoning holds. “The fact that men act by virtue of their being human is indisputable and incontrovertible,” he writes; “to assume the contrary would be an absurdity.” The whole structure rests on one axiom and two small empirical postulates.

Axiom

Humans act.

People use means to reach ends, through time, under uncertainty. Everything in chapter 1 is deduced from this alone. The footnote points to Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics, Book I, and to Rothbard’s own “In Defense of ‘Extreme Apriorism’” (1957).

Postulate

Variety.

People and natural resources differ. That is why there is a division of labour and why people trade.

Postulate

Leisure is a good.

People value free time, so work always costs something: the leisure given up. It is “the first proposition … that has not been deduced from the axiom.”

  • Verbal logic over mathematics. Every verbal proposition means something at each step; symbols don’t. Mathematics suits physics, where the conclusions are known and the premises hypothetical. Economics is the reverse. Action proceeds in discrete steps, so calculus and “infinitely small” changes are “antirealistic,” and J. E. Cairnes (1888) was right that mathematics can exhibit economic knowledge but not extend it.
  • History illustrates; it can’t test. Every historical event has many causes and none repeats under controlled conditions. Statistics can apply a theory, never confirm or refute it.
  • Imaginary constructs isolate one factor at a time. Crusoe on his island shows action with no exchange. The evenly rotating economy removes uncertainty, so interest can be studied without profit and loss mixed in. Rothbard avoids the word “model,” which he takes from engineering, where a model is a scale copy of reality.
  • Economics is one branch among others. Why people pick their ends is psychology, what ends they should pick is ethics, how to use means is technology, and what ends people have had is history. Praxeology studies only the formal implications of acting at all.
  • Value-free. Praxeology says what follows from an action, not whether it is good. Rothbard keeps to this in both books. Even the critique of intervention is framed as “this policy produces these effects,” and the last page hands the choice to “the citizen—the ethicist.”
  • General equilibrium in the Austrian sense. Everything hangs together, but as a causal sequence through time, not the Walrasian system of simultaneous equations. Rothbard makes a “complete break” with the fashionable short-run theory of the firm and its cost curves.

If you remember nothing else

Twelve ideas that carry the book

MES ch. 1

Everything starts from action

Economics is deduced from the fact that people act on purpose. Its laws are logical, not statistical, and hold in every time and place.

MES ch. 1, 4

Value is ranked, never measured

Utility is ordinal: people rank alternatives, they don’t add up units of satisfaction. Economics can see only the preferences people show by choosing, and it can never compare one person’s utility with another’s.

MES ch. 2

Free exchange benefits both sides

A trade happens only when each side values what it gets more than what it gives. There is no “equality of value” in exchange, only a double inequality.

MES ch. 3, 4

Money is a market product

The most marketable commodity becomes the medium of exchange. The regression theorem traces money’s value back to its value as an ordinary good, so money “cannot be created out of thin air” by social compact or decree.

MES ch. 5, 6

Interest is the price of time

Time preference alone sets the rate of interest, and interest shows up as the price spread at every stage of production, not only in loans. The productivity of capital explains nothing about it.

MES ch. 6, 8

Production is a structure in time

Capital goods are way stations to consumer goods. Maintaining the structure takes about three times as much saving as consumption. More saving lengthens it and raises real wages; consuming capital shortens it.

MES ch. 8

Capital, not know-how, is the limit

Better methods sit on the shelf until saving pays for them. “The African peasant will gain little from looking at pictures of American tractors.”

MES ch. 9, 10

Firms can’t outgrow calculation

A firm that owned every stage of a capital good would have no outside prices to calculate with. That caps the size of firms and rules out One Big Cartel, and it is the same reason socialism fails.

MES ch. 10

“Monopoly price” is an illusion

On a free market there is no identifiable “competitive price” to measure a monopoly price against. Monopoly in any usable sense is a privilege granted by the state.

MES ch. 11, 12

Credit expansion causes boom and bust

Bank credit pushes interest below the rate time preference would set. The boom is a cluster of malinvestments, the crisis reveals them, and the depression is the recovery.

P&M ch. 2–5

Force comes in three shapes

Autistic, binary and triangular intervention each make at least one person worse off, so no intervention can be shown to raise social utility. No tax can be neutral.

P&M ch. 1, 7

Contract or status, nothing between

Every social relation is voluntary or coerced. Defence can be a market service too, and every mixed economy is unstable, drifting toward one pole or the other.

Part I

Man, Economy, and State

1962, twelve chapters, pages 1–1046. The market economy is built up in order: from one person acting alone, to barter, to money, to a full economy with capital, interest, entrepreneurs, monopoly and banking. Each chapter rests on the ones before it. Chapter 12 then turns to force.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 1

Fundamentals of Human Action

What it means to act, and the laws that follow before a second person even appears.

Action, actors and means

  • Action is purposeful behaviour. A reflex is not action. “We could not conceive of human beings who do not act purposefully”; things that did not would no longer count as human.
  • Only individuals act. Groups, collectives and states have no ends apart from their members. “To say that ‘governments’ act is merely a metaphor”: certain individuals stand in a relationship that they and others recognize as “governmental.”
  • Means need recipes. An actor must have a technological idea of how to reach the end. Once learned, a recipe is an unlimited factor, a general condition of action like air. Air is not a “means” at all, so the old split between “economic goods” and “free goods” is a mistake: a free good is not a good.
  • Time is scarce. Every action happens before, during and after. The day has 24 hours, and Jones must rank watching a baseball game on television against a game of bridge or a drive.
  • Every action is a speculation. The future is uncertain, partly because other people’s choices can’t be predicted and partly because our knowledge of nature is incomplete, so every action can fail.

Goods, orders and factors

Consumers’ goods serve ends directly and are goods of the first order. Producers’ goods are of higher orders, ranked by distance from consumption. Rothbard’s example is Jones eating a ham sandwich in his armchair. The housewife’s labour, the bread, the ham and the knife in the kitchen are first-order producers’ goods; bread still sitting in the shop is second-order. Tracing back, every good resolves into two original factors, labour and nature (“land,” which includes water, oil and minerals), plus produced factors, capital goods. “Economic” does not mean “material”: friendship and music are ends too. The value of each factor is imputed back from the value of its products, as Carl Menger showed, and every stage needs more than one scarce factor. If the sandwich could be moved to Jones’s mouth by his wishing, we would be in Paradise.

Time preference

  • Production takes time: working time plus maturing time, as when grain grows for six months after three weeks of labour, or wine ages.
  • Time preference is universal. “Man prefers his end to be achieved in the shortest possible time.” Ice in winter and ice in summer are not a counterexample. They are different goods.
  • Goods also differ in how long they serve: a sandwich for four hours, a house for ten years. The line between durable and nondurable goods is arbitrary.

Value and marginal utility

  • Utility is ordinal. People rank; they never measure. To say one is “two and a half times happier” invites the question: “Two and a half times what?” Every action swaps a less satisfactory state for one expected to be better, and yields psychic profit or, if the concert disappoints, psychic loss.
  • Units, not classes. Choices are about particular units. If a fire threatens both the stable and the cowshed, a man may save his cows and let the horses go. The old paradox of cheap bread and dear platinum dissolves: nobody chooses between “all bread” and “all platinum.”
  • The law of diminishing marginal utility. A man with six horses serving ten ranked ends uses them for the top six. Lose one and he gives up the sixth end, not the first. If the lost horse was Man o’ War, pulling his wagon, he shifts Seabiscuit from pleasure riding to the wagon. The value of each unit equals the value of the least important end the supply serves.
  • Two goods can be ranked unit by unit on one scale (first Y, first X, second X, third X, second Y …), which settles whether a man with four X and three Y gives up an X or a Y.

The law of returns, and specific factors

Factors differ in specificity. If people stop wanting cigars, machines that can only make cigars become worthless, while tobacco can go into cigarettes and the land into cotton. The rule: a change in the value of a product causes a bigger change in the value of the factors specific to it.

Leisure, saving and capital

  • Leisure is a consumers’ good. Work continues only while its product is valued above the leisure given up, and the marginal disutility of labour rises the longer one works. Play is a pure consumers’ good (Fetter).
  • Capital is a way station. Capital goods are stored-up labour, land and time, on the road to consumer goods. They have no productive power of their own; like Böhm-Bawerk’s stone, they only pass on force put into them.
  • Time preference is the only brake. Crusoe could always invest in longer processes (a bow and arrows, a net, an axe and then a house), but he must eat meanwhile. “Never consuming” is absurd.
  • Capital wears out. A stick that lasts ten days must be replaced by new saving. Assuming, as J. B. Clark and Frank Knight did, that capital reproduces itself hides the need to maintain it.
  • Every man is partly an entrepreneur. Whether a grove of fruit trees nearby discourages investment, or a blight spurs it, depends on forecasts. Probability theory can’t price unique historical events.
  • Past capital is a conservative force. If Crusoe finds a ready-built house, the axe he made for building one gets used for a boat. Idle equipment reveals a past error, not present waste, so “unused capacity” is a bogey.
  • Action is exchange. Every choice trades one state of affairs for another. Cost is the utility of the best alternative forgone.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 2

Direct Exchange

When Crusoe meets another man he can fight him or trade with him. Price theory, property and the whole idea of society come out of the second choice.

Violence or exchange

  • Crusoe finds Jackson on the island. He could murder, rob or intimidate him. Four things restrain him: thinking it immoral, fear of the precedent, the cost of fighting, and the loss of Jackson’s future services. A high time preference can override the last.
  • Slavery makes the master treat the slave like livestock. The slave is always worse off, since he would otherwise have chosen differently. His only choice is to obey or revolt. This is the hegemonic relation of command and obedience.
  • Society is the continuing pattern of voluntary exchanges. It is not a real entity, and “compulsory co-operation” is a contradiction: co-operation can only be voluntary.

Why people trade

  • An exchange needs reverse valuations: A has X and values Y more, B the opposite. Jones has five horses and no cows, Smith five cows and no horses. Trading one for one, Jones would go on to a third trade, but Smith values his remaining cows too highly after two, so both stop there: Jones ends with three horses and two cows, Smith with three cows and two horses.
  • A good’s value to its owner is its use-value or its exchange-value, whichever is higher (Menger). Eyeglasses made to one person’s prescription are worth little to anyone else.
  • “Only gains can come to every participant in exchange.” In a contractual society consumers direct production and every relation is mutual benefit. In a hegemonic one, some gain at others’ expense.
  • Property comes from self-ownership, first appropriation of unused land, production by mixing labour with what one owns, and exchange or gift. Only scarce goods can be property; no one can own the air. Children are not yet self-owners: parents have authority but may not injure them.
  • Rothbard’s chart of human action: isolation (autistic exchange with nature) versus interpersonal action, which is either invasive (war, murder, assault, robbery, slavery) or noninvasive (gifts and voluntary exchange).

The division of labour

If everyone had the same berries there would be nothing to trade; passing fifty berries back and forth would be a game. Specialization rests on differences in natural resources, in capital (itself reducible to the other two) and in skill. Kenneth Boulding’s doctor who is also the town’s best gardener still hires a gardener: “the law of association, the law of comparative costs, or the law of comparative advantage.” Five islanders trading berries, game, fish, eggs and milk form a network that is genuinely co-operative whatever their motives. Jackson may “cordially detest” the others and still gain by trading with them. Friendship is an effect of the division of labour, not its cause. Adam Smith’s “invisible hand” is a metaphor for a conclusion, not an assumption.

Terms of trade and the market price

  • 5,000 berries for one cow is a “double inequality of values,” never an equality. A price is simply the ratio: two cows for 1,000 berries is 500 berries per cow.
  • A seller’s revenue is what he gets; his cost is the next best use. If Jackson’s offers rank 5,000 berries from Crusoe, then 100 barrels of fish from Smith, his revenue is the berries and his cost the fish.
  • With one buyer and one seller the price falls anywhere in a zone: Smith will pay up to 100 barrels of fish for Johnson’s horse and Johnson will take no less than 81, so bargaining skill decides between 81 and 100. Competition narrows the zone.
  • One price. If one seller took 87 while the market is at 89, a buyer could buy from him and resell. Arbitrage enforces a single price across the market.
  • Elasticity of demand describes how total outlay changes with price. “Elasticity of supply” means nothing useful, and “elasticity at a point” assumes the continuous curves that human action never has: choices come in discrete steps, and the calculus is “pernicious” here.
  • Speculation makes demand and supply curves more elastic, and mistaken forecasts correct themselves. “It is, for every good, utility and utility alone that determines the price.” Past costs play no role.
  • Stock and total demand to hold. The “supply” curve is just the physical stock minus the owners’ own reservation demand. The price equates the whole stock with the total demand to hold it (after Wicksteed), so cost is not an independent force on price. The horses end up with the people who value them most.
  • Markets continue as value scales change. Once Rembrandt died, the stock of Rembrandts was fixed and only demand could move their price. Producers of new stock are entrepreneurs, guessing what others will want.

Services, claims and contracts

  • Services are goods too (medical advice, a violinist’s concert). Economics is not about “material” goods. Six kinds of exchange follow from mixing commodities, services and claims.
  • The will is inalienable. A contract to work for life can’t be enforced; the worker can quit and must return any payment for unperformed work. Even the marriage contract should be terminable at the will of either party.
  • Claims include warehouse receipts, shares (Harrison’s iron mine divided into 100 tickets) and credit: Jackson gets 100 pounds of cotton now for a claim on 110 pounds in a year, a price of 1.1.

Property in land, and its defence

  • Columbus could not claim a continent “as far as his eye can see.” Land becomes property through use, and stays owned even if later left idle.
  • The same rule applies to new resources. The first user of the 1,000-kilocycle radio frequency owns it; Ronald Coase (1959) showed that the Federal Communications Commission nationalized the airwaves in the 1920s to forestall common-law property in them. Air lanes and fishing grounds, fixed by latitude and longitude, can be owned. Flowing water should follow first appropriation, not riparian rights.
  • Only violence, theft and fraud are invasions. Contracts are enforced not because promises are sacred but because non-payment is implicit theft. A movie actor who breaks a promise to make three pictures before being paid can’t be forced to perform.
  • Restitution comes first. A thief who stole 100 ounces of gold should work it off with compensation for the delay, and the victim may forgive him.
  • Air pollution and smoke are invasions of property, to be remedied in court, not by an administrative bureau. But no one owns his reputation (it exists in others’ minds), so libel and blackmail are not crimes. Cartel agreements are legal but unenforceable.
  • Auberon Herbert’s formula sums it up: “The sovereignty of the individual must remain intact, except where the individual coerced has aggressed upon the sovereignty of another unaggressive individual.”

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 3

The Pattern of Indirect Exchange

Barter breaks down, money emerges from it, and a monetary economy looks different from the inside.

Why barter fails, and how money emerges

A house builder can’t pay his masons in pieces of the house. The egg seller who wants shoes must find a shoemaker who wants eggs. A plough can’t be divided, and without a common unit no one can compare costs and revenues. So people start trading for goods they don’t want, because they are easier to trade again. A trades ten dozen eggs for ten pounds of butter, then the butter for shoes. Marketability depends on how many people want the good, how divisible, durable and transportable it is. The process snowballs until one or two commodities are money. History offers tobacco in colonial Virginia, sugar in the West Indies, salt in Abyssinia, cattle in ancient Greece, nails in Scotland and copper in ancient Egypt, as well as beads, tea, cowrie shells and fishhooks. In the end gold and silver won.

  • Money is a unit of weight. An ounce is 437.5 grains or 28.35 grams. A coin called a “Houston” of 20 grains is simply worth two “Adamses” of 10. The dollar was the Joachimsthaler, minted by the Count of Schlick in Bohemia in 1518. The book prices everything in ounces and grams of gold, not dollars.
  • It is a mistake to analyse barter and then “insert” money at the end. Money changes the whole pattern of exchange.
  • Income, spending and cash. James Brown earns 26 ounces in September 1961 (20 as a carpenter, 5 as a handyman, 1 for a radio) and spends 24. His income is his “exports,” his spending his “imports,” and his cash balance rises from 6 to 8. Money income equals money expenditure plus net additions to cash.
  • No balance-of-trade problem. In a village of five whose cash balances total 34 ounces, one person’s addition is another’s subtraction, and each change is chosen. There is “never a need for anyone to worry about anyone else’s balance of payments.”
  • Producers’ spending. Fred Jones earns 50 ounces, spends 18 on consumption, invests 30 in a paper factory (machinery, wood pulp, labour) and adds 2 to his cash, expecting 40 ounces in a year. Capitalists buy factors with saved money; goods flow down the stages while money flows up.

What people maximize

  • People maximize psychic income without qualification, and money income only “other things equal.” A man offered jobs at 30, 24 and 21 ounces a month takes the 30 only if the psychic factors are equal; the 24-ounce job may have qualities he likes enough to take it instead. The classical “economic man” was a mistake.
  • Rothbard prefers “exchangeable” and “nonexchangeable” goods to “material” and “ideal.” A money economy makes exchangeable goods more abundant, and so raises the marginal value of the nonexchangeable ones, such as the preservation of a historic monument.
  • Money has three uses and only three: consumption, investment and cash balance. The rule is “buy on the cheapest market and sell on the dearest.” Calling only field or assembly-line workers “productive” is a fallacy that runs from ditch-digger to company president.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 4

Prices and Consumption

How money prices form, why the value of money isn’t a circular argument, and why utility can’t be measured, added or compared.

Money prices

  • Once there is money, every good has a money price, and barter ratios can be computed only through money. The purchasing power of money is the whole array of goods-prices, not a “price level” that one number can capture.
  • For Grade A butter priced in grains of gold, buyers’ and sellers’ schedules cross between two and three grains; with divisible units the price settles at 2.5. As with the horses, the price equates the stock with the total demand to hold it.
  • Speculation is self-correcting, not self-justifying. Supply curves for goods already produced are often near-vertical, and some goods, like Chippendale chairs, trade only now and then.
  • No consumer surplus. The buyer who would have paid 4.5 grains for eggs that cost 3 gains, but his gain can’t be measured or compared with the marginal buyer’s. Asking “how much would you pay rather than go without?” is academic.

The marginal utility of money, and the regression theorem

Money obeys the law of marginal utility like any good. It goes to nonmonetary uses, consumption, investment or cash. Cash is held because the future is uncertain, and all money is always in someone’s cash balance, so there are no mysterious “idle hoards” or “circulation.” That raises a puzzle. People value money by what it buys, but what it buys depends on how people value it. Mises’s regression theorem breaks the circle. Today’s valuation of money rests on yesterday’s purchasing power, and that on the day before, back to the last day of barter, when gold was valued only for its direct uses. So money must emerge from a commodity that already had value. It “cannot be created out of thin air by any sudden ‘social compact’ or edict of government,” although once established it could keep circulating after losing its other uses. Economists who missed this split the “theory of price” from the “theory of money,” with a mythical price level in between. Don Patinkin’s circular system is the target here.

  • Cost follows value. A cake bought and then disliked shows the gap between ex ante and ex post. The nineteenth-century cost theories of value had it backwards. Wicksteed: “Efforts are regulated by anticipated values, but values are not controlled by antecedent efforts.”
  • Everyone plans, from the steel company to the small boy spending two cents on candy. The free market is not “unplanned.”
  • How consumer prices hang together. A consumer buys each good until the utility of another unit no longer exceeds the utility of its price. The more substitutes a good has, the more elastic its demand. All goods compete for the same money: pork with beef, travel with television sets. Complements (golf clubs and balls) move together. Wicksteed’s housewife weighs new potatoes against old, chickens against cod, French lessons against violin lessons.

Durable goods and capitalization

  • Durable goods have two prices: a rent for their services per period, and a capital value for the whole good. If a house expected to earn 10 ounces a year for 20 years sold for 180 while the rents summed to 200 (setting time preference aside), buyers would rush in. Labour is the exception: people can be hired, never bought, except under slavery.
  • Better forecasters gain relative to worse ones. Time preference itself yields no monetary profit; it is built into every capital value.

Welfare comparisons, and fallacies about utility

  • Neither psychic income nor money income measures welfare. Jones may enjoy his hotel room at 18 ounces less than married Smith enjoys a room at 9. No one can say an extra dollar means less to a Rockefeller than to a poor man, who may be an ascetic.
  • No equating of marginal utility and price. Textbook formulas (after W. S. Jevons) that set ratios of marginal utilities equal to ratios of prices are “absurd.” Utilities are ranks, not quantities, and there are no infinitely small steps.
  • Indifference can’t be acted on. To act is to choose, so indifference never shows up in behaviour. If a man doesn’t care whether he uses 5.1 or 5.2 ounces of butter, he simply won’t act in tenths of an ounce; he will use butter in whole ounces. Alternatives are ranked without ties, and asking people to draw their indifference maps in questionnaires misses the point. Schumpeter called Buridan’s ass, starving between two equal bales of hay, “perfectly rational.” Rothbard replies that the ass has three choices, not two, and starving where he stands ranks below both bales. He will pick one, by a coin toss if need be, and the toss reveals his preference. If Jones picks A half the time and B the other half, his preferences shifted; he was never indifferent.
  • Appendix A adds a “law of increasing total utility”: a larger unit is always valued more than a smaller one. A man who must spend 60 ounces on a car or twelve five-ounce units on other things compares the car against the whole bundle.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 5

Production: The Structure

Production is a sequence of stages. Capitalists pay the owners of labour and land now and wait to be paid later, and that service, not the capital goods, is what earns interest.

The evenly rotating economy

  • Hold values, technology and resources constant, and the market settles into a round that repeats forever: the evenly rotating economy (ERE). The real economy never gets there. The ERE is like the mechanical rabbit a greyhound chases: it sets the direction of the chase.
  • The ERE is a mental tool, not an ideal. It has no ethical merit, and actually reaching it would be “most unfortunate.” It is not even fully consistent. With the future certain, no one would hold cash, so money itself would lose its function.
  • Mathematics suits a static state and can’t describe the path to it. George Stigler faulted Böhm-Bawerk for preferring cause and effect to “mutual determination,” and for not being trained in mathematics. Rothbard replies that mathematics is only the servant of logic, and that human action has no quantitative constants, only qualitative laws.

Who owns the capital, and who earns

Imagine two consumer goods, each made by a chain of specific factors, and seven factors in all: two kinds of labour, two of land, three capital goods. The product could be jointly owned by all the factor owners, who share the proceeds when it sells. Or a capitalist could buy the factors’ services outright and own the product himself. Under joint ownership, trace any 100 ounces of consumer spending back through the stages and all of it goes to the owners of land and labour. The capital goods are just intermediate products of land and labour. “Conceptually, no one … receives a return as the owner of a capital good.”

  • Factors earn only when consumers buy. If the product fails, joint owners get nothing, so every factor owner is partly an entrepreneur.
  • “Free to starve.” A man whose only skill no consumer wants can always fall back on working alone. Poverty is a fact of nature, not a lack of freedom. Crusoe was absolutely free and might still starve; a rich slave is not free. Rothbard prefers “individual sovereignty” to “consumers’ sovereignty.”
  • Marketing and advertising costs are productive (Mises), because a good isn’t finished until it is in the consumer’s hands.

Cost is subjective

  • Cost is the value of the alternative given up (after G. F. Thirlby). Specific land and capital cost nothing to use, because they have no other use. Once a good is produced, selling it is costless. So there are no sales “below cost,” and costs affect how much gets produced, not the price directly. “Bygones are forever bygones” (Jevons).
  • In the ERE, price equals average money cost, and economists wrongly concluded that cost determines price in the long run. The causation runs the other way: if consumers would pay only 50 ounces instead of 100, costs would fall to 50. The old cost and labour theories of value could not explain why old paintings are valuable.
  • Marshall’s scissors. Alfred Marshall rescued the classical economists by saying demand rules in the short run and cost of production in the long, when “persistent causes dominate value completely.” Rothbard: the long run “never does and never can exist,” and even subjective costs such as labour and waiting are utilities. The explanation of price must be “monistic and not dualistic.” For him, “it was with the writings of the Austrian School in the 1870’s and 1880’s that economics was truly established as a science.” Emil Kauder’s research suggested the British classical economists’ Calvinist background explains their search for an objective measure of value.

Capitalists and the pure rate of interest

  • Workers and landowners prefer to be paid now. Capitalists buy their future product at a discount. That discount is interest: the price of time, not a return to capital goods as such, and not exploitation. It persists in the ERE, where entrepreneurial profit vanishes.
  • Rates must be compared per unit of time: 20 ounces earned on 500 over two years is 2 per cent a year, while 15 on 500 in one year is 3 per cent. Competition pushes every line toward one rate.
  • The classical trinity of wages, rent and interest paid to labour, land and capital is wrong. Capital goods produce no net income of their own. Interest is not a money cost either, since it is part of the product’s price that goes to the capitalist (Gabor and Pearce).
  • Bargaining. If every factor were specific, as with twelve indispensable parts of one machine, their shares would be indeterminate and bargaining would decide. In fact labour is uniquely nonspecific (every job competes with other jobs), so bargaining theory never applies to wages. Labour even has the stronger reservation price: leisure. Land has none.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 6

Production: The Rate of Interest and Its Determination

The analytical heart of the book: interest comes from time preference and nothing else, and it shows up as the price spread at every stage of production.

Many stages

  • “The interest rate is equal to the rate of price spread.” Suppose stage-four capitalists could buy their factors for 35 instead of 43 while stage-one factors cost 98. Stage four would earn 23 per cent and stage one about 2. Capital would move from stage one to stage four until the percentage spreads were equal again. The loan market is only a subsidiary time market of “derivative importance.”
  • Keynes called Mises’s theory “peculiar” for confusing the marginal efficiency of capital with the rate of interest. Rothbard: “the ‘marginal efficiency of capital’ is indeed the rate of interest!”
  • A two-year process earns 10 per cent, 5 per cent a year. A present claim to 100 ounces is worth about 95 a year from now, about 97.5 half a year from now. As Böhm-Bawerk said, interest arises on any capital, barren, perishable or money.

The time market

Money is “the present good par excellence.” On the time market people exchange present goods for future goods. Some trades are written loans: 100 ounces now for 105 in a year. Far more are purchases of producers’ goods and services. A worker whose labour will yield 20 ounces in five years is paid its discounted value now. “The time market is therefore not restricted to the loan market. It permeates the entire production structure.” Rothbard gives John Smith and James Robinson time-preference scales, with the future money due in ten years. At 3 per cent Smith saves 20 ounces, while Robinson saves nothing and would sell 13 future ounces for 10 present ones. At 1 per cent Smith saves nothing and Robinson would give 22 future ounces for 20 present. No schedule ever lists fewer future than present ounces, because time preference is always positive, so a zero or negative pure rate is impossible. Aggregate the schedules, and the pure rate is “determined solely by the time preferences of the individuals in the society, and by no other factor.” Böhm-Bawerk first saw its importance, but only Frank Fetter and Mises grasped that it is the sole cause.

  • It is a mistake to say the interest rate determines time preference. The causation runs the other way.
  • Gross, not net. In the table above, net income (100) equals consumption (100), so net saving looks like zero. But gross saving is 318, three times consumption, just to maintain the structure. If the first-stage capitalists spent all 100 on themselves, the structure would collapse and the economy would “revert to barbarism” (Wicksell). Gross national product is “not gross at all, but only partly gross.”
  • John Bates Clark and Frank Knight treated capital as a permanent, self-perpetuating fund, what Hayek called “the mythology of capital.” It has to be deliberately maintained by saving. And so “there is, in fact, never any need to worry about the maintenance of consumer spending.” What matters is price spreads, not whether 100 or 100,000 ounces are spent.
  • Capitalists are a function, not a class. Anyone who saves is one. “The only thing that stops a man from being a capitalist is his own high time-preference scale.” That the rich save more is a historical judgment, not a law: think of the “proverbially thrifty French peasant” and the “rich playboy.” The Marxian picture of a capitalist caste goes back to Adam Smith.
  • Consumer borrowing is dissaving. Savers supply present goods; landowners, workers and borrowing consumers demand them.

The myth of the producers’ loan market

  • Textbooks draw a supply of savings (time preference) against a demand curve made of investment projects ranked by their “marginal efficiency” (8 per cent, 7 per cent, 3 per cent). That attributes value-productivity to the money invested.
  • Rothbard’s example: a process makes 10 units selling at 2 ounces. A new method makes 50 units, and the price falls to 1 ounce, so revenue rises from 20 to 50. Competing capitalists bid the factor prices up from 18 to 47, and the spread falls back to the going rate. “The productivity of production processes has no basic relation to the rate of return.” Fetter showed this against Böhm-Bawerk’s “highly unfortunate lapse into a productivity theory.” An economy could run with no producers’ loan market at all.

The joint-stock company

  • Five people pool 100 ounces (A 40, B 20, C 20, D 15, E 5) as 100 one-ounce shares with one vote per share. The corporation just makes entry and exit easier than a partnership. Minority shareholders chose to join and can sell.
  • One-member-one-vote is the co-operative principle, which “absurdly but effectively” claimed the name “co-operative” for itself.
  • A hired manager is a labourer like a ditch-digger. The entrepreneurial decisions stay with the owners. There is no separation of saving and investment, since the shareholders both save and invest.
  • Lending to a firm and buying its stock differ only technically. A firm with 150 ounces of assets (130 owned, 20 borrowed) earning 7.5 pays 1 to its creditors and 6.5 to its owners, 5 per cent each. Creditors are owners with a prior claim. In Mises’s phrase, “the creditor is always a virtual partner of the debtor.”
  • Psychic factors enter too. If investors dislike the armaments industry, they will demand 10 per cent there against 5 elsewhere, and the burden falls on the industry’s specific factors.

What moves time preference, and the rate structure

Time preferences are data to the economist, but some effects are clear. A fall in a person’s real money stock raises his time preference. If everyone knew the world would end on a certain date, the rate would soar toward infinity; a drug for immortality would make it fall sharply. Long and short rates tend to equality through arbitrage. A 100-ounce bond paying 5 falls to 50 when the rate goes to 10 per cent. Friedrich Lutz’s theory that the long rate averages expected short rates is “question-begging.” An appendix answers Schumpeter’s claim that the pure rate would be zero in a stationary economy. Clemence and Doody, defending him, said time preference could be “positive or negative as we prefer.”

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 7

Production: General Pricing of the Factors

Why each factor earns what it does: consumer demand, imputed back through the stages and discounted for time. Then a new line between land and capital.

Imputation

  • The textbook contrast between fixed and variable factor proportions is beside the point. “The key question … is not variability, but specificity.” Nonspecific factors get determinate prices; a single specific factor takes the residual; several specific factors share a residual by bargaining.
  • Discounted marginal value product. A unit of a factor that adds 20 ounces to a product sold a year from now, at 5 per cent, is worth 19 today. A capitalist who hires it for 15 makes a profit of 4, and competition bids its price up to 19. In the ERE every factor earns its DMVP.
  • If 4X + 10Y + 2Z make 100 ounces of product and 3X + 10Y + 2Z make 80, the marginal value of X is 20. With fixed proportions, 3X + 7.5Y + 1.5Z make 75, so the value is 25. “Equal causes produce equal results.”
  • X has uses worth 25, 24, 22, 21, 20, 19, 18 … an ounce. With 30 units available and the last use worth 10, every unit sells for 10, and anyone asking 24 is simply replaced. If X is worth 10 and Y 2, and 3X + 10Y + 2Z make 80, then Z takes the residual: 30 + 20 + 2Z = 80, so Z is worth 15. A change in consumer demand hits specific factors far harder than nonspecific ones.
  • Physical and value product. Value product equals physical product times price (strictly, marginal revenue). There is no general marginal physical product across uses, since eggs, butter and houses can’t be added. In the range where adding a factor raises its average product, the other factors have negative marginal product, so a firm operates only where both average and marginal product are falling and positive.
  • “We have been able to explain the pricing of its unit service without previously assuming the existence of the price itself.” A factor’s price is its opportunity cost to the firm.
  • No separate distribution. Critics say the modern world is too complex for productivity to decide incomes, so bargaining must. Rothbard: nonspecific factors, and the rarity of more than one specific factor, let the market impute value. “There is no separate ‘distribution’; there is only production and its corollary, exchange.” There is no heap of goods waiting to be shared out.
  • Wages are paid out of capital in advance, except for final-stage work and personal services paid by consumers. On this the classical wages-fund side was right, against Henry George and J. B. Clark, who said wages come from the annual product.

Land and capital, redefined

Böhm-Bawerk traced capital back to land and labour through history: “The boy who cuts a stick with his knife is, strictly speaking, only continuing the work of the miner who, centuries ago, thrust the first spade into the ground.” Knight’s followers rejected the whole land/capital distinction as a historical exercise, throwing out the “land baby with the average-period-of-production bathwater.” Mises answered that “acting man does not look at his condition with the eyes of an historian”; the past time spent making a tool doesn’t count. Rothbard keeps the distinction but moves the line. Permanent improvements that are never replaced, such as canals and cleared forests, count as land. Capital goods are things that wear out and must be replaced. The structure of production then closes within a short span. The test is physical permanence, not value. A vineyard stays land even after people lose the taste for wine; it just becomes submarginal. Farm land usually mixes a land element with a capital element, since topsoil 12 to 18 inches deep can be destroyed. A Midtown Manhattan site is the same site it was in the 1600s.

Capitalization and rent

  • Rothbard revives Fetter’s concept: rent is the price of the service of any good per period, including wages. Capital value is the sum of expected future rents, discounted. Two goods like the one in chapter 4 are worth 118.8.
  • The Jones Construction Company, with three machines worth 30, a building worth 40 and four acres worth 40, is worth 110. Estimates of “National Capital” or “World Capital” are “completely meaningless,” because the whole can never be sold.
  • Ground land is capitalized because it can be sold; labour can’t be, except under slavery. That land sells at a finite price at all proves that time preference is universal, since infinite rents would otherwise command an infinite price. A perpetual rent of 50 at 5 per cent is worth 1,000.
  • So “no one receives pure rent except laborers in the form of wages.” A man who buys land earns only interest on his purchase. In the ERE all incomes are wages and interest. In a changing economy, a rise in the capital value of land is a one-time gain to its owner.

Depleting resources

A mine is land, not capital, since it can’t be reproduced. A depleting resource can’t exist in the ERE at all. Speculative reserve demand shifts its use toward the times of greatest demand. The charge that using resources is “robbery of the future” would forbid using them at any time; the market uses them as fast as consumers want. As Stigler put it, “it is an interesting problem to define ‘wasteful’ sensibly without making the word synonymous with ‘unprofitable.’” Chapter 7 ended volume I of the 1962 edition: “Having developed, in Volume I, our basic analysis … we shall now proceed, in Volume II, to develop … ‘dynamic’ problems.” An appendix answers Earl Rolph’s claim that factors are paid their “immediate” product. Rolph’s “production centers,” Rothbard says, can stop worrying, “since they have been implicitly paid simultaneously as they worked.”

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 8

Production: Entrepreneurship and Change

Leave the evenly rotating economy, let the future be uncertain, and profit, loss and the entrepreneur appear.

Profit and loss

  • Jones buys factors for 100 and sells the product for 120 two years later: 10 per cent a year when the interest rate is 5. His pure profit is the extra 10 ounces. He saw that the market had underpriced his factors. A man who pays 1,000 and gets back 1,020 a year later has made a loss of 30.
  • Profits tend to disappear as others copy the move, so there is “no sense whatever in talking of a going rate of profit.” If line A yields 10 per cent, line B 2 per cent and the rest 5, A earns a profit of 5 and B a loss of 3.
  • Profits are a sign that maladjustments are being corrected. “The greater a man’s profit has been, the more praiseworthy his role,” and the greater a man’s losses, the more blameworthy, although Rothbard notes in a footnote that these are value judgments, not praxeology.
  • Losers are driven back to wage-earning. The market “is no respecter of past laurels.” “Capital does not ‘beget’ profit”: one man can lose 10,000 ounces as surely as another profits on 50. Mises: “Nobody has the right to take offense at the errors made by the entrepreneurs.… It is easy indeed to display foresight after the event.”

Saving lengthens the structure

  • Specific factors at the late stages bear the loss; nonspecific labour moves to earlier stages. Money wages may even fall, but real wages rise because physical productivity rises and the discount shrinks. Historically, a growing money supply has hidden this.
  • New investment first shows up as profits in the higher stages, then is imputed to factors. All net income goes to wages, interest and profit. Landowners gain only as entrepreneurs, as interest, or as first finders of land whose value rises.
  • Progress and retrogression. A progressing economy shows aggregate net profits, a retrogressing one aggregate net losses, and a stationary one profits and losses that cancel. Capital gains are income. Capital does not reproduce itself: “the ruins of Rome are mute illustrations.” Fritz Machlup’s study of Austria (1935) shows a country that raised public spending, wages, social benefits, bank credit and consumption at once, and ended up “on the verge of ruin.”

Capital, not technology, is the limit

  • Böhm-Bawerk’s “roundabout” methods are misleading. A net is “the most direct method for catching 100 fish a day.” Mises’s formula is better: more physically productive processes take more time. The shortest processes are adopted first, and new saving goes into longer ones.
  • Horace White objected to Böhm-Bawerk that drilling for oil replaced the long process of Arctic whaling with a much shorter one. Rothbard answers that capital, not knowledge, limits production: new inventions sit on an unused shelf until saving pays for the wells and machinery. White would be right only if the invention made ever shorter processes ever more productive, and then boring for oil with bare hands would beat drilling with machinery. Böhm-Bawerk’s analogy: if an invention raises poor land from 80 bushels to 110, the good land that yielded 100 still does better with it. “While knowledge is a limit, capital is a narrower limit.”
  • So poor countries lack saved capital, not know-how. “The African peasant will gain little from looking at pictures of American tractors.” Truman’s Point Four programme and “technical assistance” are futile.
  • New technique. Sunk capital is scrapped only when the new method’s advantage covers the new outlay; not everyone throws away an old car the day a new model appears (Mises). Railroads in late nineteenth-century America were overbuilt thanks to land grants and subsidies. Innovation, Schumpeter’s whole theory of the entrepreneur, is only one of the entrepreneur’s jobs; his general job is adjusting to change.
  • Workers are the “external beneficiaries” of saving. Their real wages rise even if the investors who made it possible end up with a lower rate of return.
  • In a progressing economy the pure rate falls, and the causation runs from lower time preference to more capital, not the reverse. High rates in poor countries partly reflect risk.

Risk, uncertainty and insurance

A loan certain to repay 105 will fetch 100 now; a risky one only 90. That entrepreneurial component sits on top of the pure rate. Frank Knight’s distinction between insurable risk and uninsurable uncertainty is kept. If 1 per cent of bolts are defective, or ten of a thousand firms burn each year, the loss is a class probability and can be insured, and insurers earn interest. Mises’s distinction between class probability and case probability explains why a unique event can’t be insured. Gambling (dice) and betting (on a horse race or a presidential election) both create new uncertainty; the entrepreneur, like the casino operator, reduces the uncertainty already inherent in the world.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 9

Production: Particular Factor Prices and Productive Incomes

The general theory applied to land, labour, unemployment, the firm, location and the size limit on any enterprise.

Rent, land and population

  • Every good earns gross rent, but only land and labour earn net rent. Marshall’s “quasi-rents” name no real income. A wage is a special case of rent that can’t be capitalized, because people own themselves.
  • Labour is scarcer than land, so some land always lies idle and submarginal. The marginal land in use earns close to zero rent, but never exactly zero. As Fetter said, “no finite unit of product can be shown to be a no-rent unit.”
  • When capital per head rises, the marginal value of capital goods falls and that of land and labour rises. More people with the same capital lower labour’s marginal product and raise land’s (Ricardo), but a wider division of labour offsets that. There is an “optimum” population that maximizes output per head, and the idea “would go far to end the dreary Malthusian controversies.” Land has sometimes been scarcer than labour: ancient Sparta, and medieval Europe with its “surplus labor,” infanticide and beggary.
  • Economists of the Lausanne school (Walras, Pareto) took tastes, techniques and resources as given. Rothbard takes only tastes and resources, with time preference among the tastes. The “rent of ability” and the “industry point of view” are rejected.

Labour

  • All hired work is labour, from ditch-digger to vice president. Sorting it into “labor” and “management” is valueless. A vice president has as much reason to join a union as a mechanic: “if everyone admits that the unionizing of vice presidents is absurd or evil, then perhaps the same adjective would have to apply to the unionization of any workers.”
  • Land differs from labour in three ways: it can be capitalized, it is less scarce and more specific, and it has no reservation price such as leisure. The Georgist “bugbear” of speculators holding land out of use misreads waiting for a better use, which serves consumers.
  • People differ, a “fundamental, empirically derived postulate of this book.” Training costs can’t explain the pay of an opera singer or a batting champion.
  • Productivity. To say “the productivity of labor has advanced” is misleading. Capital, supplied by capitalist-entrepreneurs, raised it. If steel workers earn 25 grains an hour and domestic servants 15, a rise in steel wages to 30 pulls servants’ wages up too, because labour markets are linked from ditch-diggers through foremen to supervisors.
  • Pensions and “welfare” benefits come out of the overt wage; the employer counts the total cost of labour.

The “problem” of unemployment

People say that “at least socialism solves the unemployment problem.” Since the Keynesians of the mid-1930s, economists have said that orthodox economics “assumes full employment.” Rothbard replies that economics assumes nothing: “The economist does not ‘assume’; he knows.” Idle land and capital are no problem, and labour is fully employed to the extent people want to work at the wage the market will pay. Employment without a wage rate is as meaningless as demand without a price. “There can never be, on the free market, an unemployment problem.” If half the labour force insisted on television jobs in New York City, or a man demanded 500 ounces when his product was worth 100, the unemployment would be their choice. Mises: “Unemployment in the unhampered market is always voluntary.” Hayek’s transitional unemployment is real but short, since workers can bid wages down. “Technological unemployment” is absurd: the shovel was a labour-saving device too, and the buggy workers found other jobs.

Costs and the size of the firm

  • There are no cost curves in this book. Stigler says factor prices are constant for the firm “because the firm does not buy enough of any service to affect its price,” which, Rothbard says, gives up on explaining factor prices at all.
  • No cost is truly fixed: at zero output, long enough, the lease ends and the managers are fired. With perfectly divisible factors, returns to scale would be constant.
  • Indivisibility is what gives firms a best size. Stigler’s railroad track can carry 200 trains a day whether 100 or 250 are wanted, and a firm can’t buy half a ten-horsepower motor. Management, selling, research and insurance come in lumps. Above all, the owner’s “ultimate decision-making ability” can’t be enlarged at will.
  • Firm size is settled by entrepreneurs, not economists: a steel plant is big, a barbershop small. Every firm has a finite size, and there is no infinite tendency toward monopoly.

Business income and calculation

  • A business owner earns four kinds of income: interest, wages of management, a rent for “decision-making” or “ownership” ability (choosing managers and deciding how much to invest, a function that survives in the ERE), and profit or loss (which does not). Even the marginal firm earns a positive decision rent. Roy Harrod had seen part of this.
  • An economy made up entirely of producers’ co-operatives could not calculate either.
  • Doctors, lawyers, concert artists and servants sell personal services directly to consumers.

Vertical integration and the limit on firms

The Jones Manufacturing Company makes its own intermediate product. If the upper stage turns 100 ounces of factors into a good it could sell outside for 103, that stage earns 3 per cent against a going rate of 5, and Jones should sell it off or stop making the good. He can tell only because there is an outside market for the intermediate good. With no market, “the good can have no price, whether implicit or explicit. Any figure could be only an arbitrary symbol.” Hence one of the book’s central laws: “complete vertical integration for a capital-good product can never be established on the free market (above the primitive level).” For every capital good there must be a market, and this sets a maximum to the relative size of any firm. “There can never be One Big Cartel over the whole economy or mergers until One Big Firm owns all the productive assets.” Ronald Coase’s “The Nature of the Firm” fits here: firms are islands of voluntary planning, and the market decides how big they get.

  • This is Mises’s argument of 1920 against socialism, generalized. “Paradoxically, the reason why a socialist economy cannot calculate is not specifically because it is socialist!” It is because one agent owns all the resources. A private One Big Firm would fail in the same way, but the market would never let it form. The state is not stopped by losses.
  • The legend that Enrico Barone (1908) proved socialism could calculate is wrong, because Barone’s equations describe only the ERE, where no calculation is needed. Hayek and Lionel Robbins conceded the point and argued only that the equations were too many to solve in practice, which opened the door to the retort that computers could solve them.

Location, and the fallacy of “distribution”

  • On a free market there is no separate problem of “international trade.” Interest, prices and wages tend to be equal everywhere, apart from transport costs and people’s psychic attachment to places. Wheat in Kansas is not the same good as wheat in New York; its price is the New York price minus freight.
  • Cement from Rochester and from Detroit sells in New York City at the same delivered price, so mill prices differ by the freight. Attacks on the “basing-point system” rest on confusing the two. Locational advantages are imputed to the owner of the site.
  • “Distribution” is just production seen from the other side. Property was obtained by production, exchange, first use or theft, and ill-gotten wealth dissipates unless its owners serve consumers.
  • The chapter ends with a summary of the whole market economy, which Rothbard calls a “great architectural edifice,” and welcomes signs of an Austrian revival in capital theory: Ludwig Lachmann’s Capital and Its Structure (1956) and Robert Dorfman’s “groping attempt … to return to the Austrian road” (1959).

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 10

Monopoly and Competition

The most provocative chapter: on a free market, “monopoly price” can’t be told apart from any other price, so the only meaningful monopoly is a privilege granted by the state.

Consumers’ sovereignty or individual sovereignty?

  • W. H. Hutt of the University of Cape Town coined “consumers’ sovereignty” in 1934. Rothbard objects that sovereignty is a political word, meaning ultimate power backed by force, and that producers serve consumers voluntarily. He prefers “individual sovereignty”: each person rules his own person and property.
  • Hutt allowed producers to withhold their services for their own enjoyment but called withholding for more money an “infringement” of consumers’ sovereignty, with monopoly price as the instrument. Rothbard: motives can’t be separated, and a producer can gain from restriction only if consumers’ demand is inelastic, which is the consumers’ own choice. Consumers always want lower prices; is the ideal price zero? On Hutt’s logic Crusoe and Friday would be “bilateral monopolists” ripe for intervention.

Cartels, mergers and the size of firms

  • “Collusion” and “conspiracy” are emotive words. There is “no essential difference between a cartel and an ordinary corporation or partnership”: all pool assets. A merger is a permanent cartel. Mises: critics blame the entrepreneur for the fact “that there is a scarcity of the factors of production and that the earth is not a land of Cockaigne.”
  • If a cartel burned 40 million pounds to double the price from one grain of gold to two, consumers could boycott it or buy the coffee first. “Extortion” is the wrong word.
  • Size. Economics can’t say what size a firm should be; entrepreneurs find out. Technology alone never decides. Two hours of labour, 5 pounds of clay and an oven-hour make a pot; the same labour and oven-hour with a block of wood make a pipe or a model boat. If the clay and oven cost 1 ounce and the pot sells for 2, his labour earns 1. If wood and oven cost 1.5 and the pipe sells for 4, it earns 2.5, and if the boat sells for 5 he makes the boat. Only prices tell him which use of his labour is most “efficient.” “Production for use” versus “production for profit” is a false contrast, whatever Thorstein Veblen and the Technocrats of the early 1930s said.
  • Cartels are unstable. A profitable cartel tends to become a merger; an unprofitable one breaks up as efficient members chafe at their quotas and outsiders enter.
  • Freedom is not abundance. Hutt said consumers are free “only to the extent to which the power of substitution exists.” That confuses freedom with abundance: “it is a reflection on nature, not on the free market, that everyone is ‘free to starve.’” Free competition means free entry. Large capital requirements are no barrier to freedom, since anyone can buy a share of stock for “50 or so dollars.”
  • Benjamin Tucker told the Chicago Conference on Trusts in September 1899 that “the right to cooperate is as unquestionable as the right to compete.… The trust is a denial of competition in no other sense than that in which competition itself is a denial of competition.”
  • No One Big Cartel. It could not calculate (chapter 9). Socialism is One Big Cartel imposed by the state. If central planning were more efficient, why would it not be formed voluntarily? Even if it were, it could not exploit consumers, whose demand is elastic above the free-market price, or workers, whom outside firms would hire.

What is a monopoly?

  • “Control over price” won’t do. Henry Ford and a small wheat farmer both set their asking prices; everyone charges “whatever the traffic will bear.”
  • Definition 1: the only seller of a good. Then everyone is a monopolist. John Jones is the only seller of John Jones’s legal services; the owner of the Empire State Building is the only seller of its space. Whether two goods are “the same” is decided by consumers, not engineers. Lawrence Abbott’s examples: 1948 Chablis, a Steinway versus a Chickering piano.
  • Definition 2: a grant of privilege. Lord Coke: “A monopoly is an institution or allowance by the king, by his grant, commission, or otherwise … for the sole buying, selling, making, working, or using of anything.” Parliament declared in 1624 that “all monopolies are altogether contrary to the laws of this realm and are and shall be void,” and Maryland’s first constitution called monopolies “odious.” In this sense a monopoly cannot arise on a free market.
  • Definition 3: whoever obtains a monopoly price. This is the neoclassical theory, held by Mises: if a seller facing inelastic demand above the competitive price can earn more by selling less, he charges a monopoly price.

Even granting the theory …

  • There is always competition for the consumer’s money: cake soap against detergents, yachts against mansions, books against television. Progress makes demand curves more elastic.
  • There are no lasting monopoly profits, only gains imputed to a factor: to a brand name’s owner, to Mickey Mantle’s talent, to a diamond mine. A world of monopoly prices is impossible, since the factors released must go elsewhere. At most half the industries could have them.
  • Predatory price-cutting is a bonanza for consumers and a bad bet for the predator, who loses more than the small firm he is trying to kill (Wayne Leeman, 1956). One oil man told Harold Fleming that cutting prices to drive out rivals and then raising them was “like trying to sweep back the ocean to get a dry place to sit down”: competitors move back in the moment prices rise. John McGee’s study of the Standard Oil case (1958) found that “in not one instance” did Standard Oil attempt predatory price-cutting; Rockefeller got tired of paying “blackmail” to be bought out and kept his margins small.

The illusion of monopoly price

Now the central argument. A seller who sets output and price has no way of knowing, and neither does anyone else, whether the price is “competitive” or “monopolistic.” “In the market, there is no discernible, identifiable competitive price.” A cut in output might be a move from a subcompetitive price up to the competitive one. Every other proposed test fails. There are no permanent monopoly profits to point to. A factor earning more than a similar one is really a different factor (Mickey Mantle versus another outfielder, the Empire State Building site versus a rural general store). Idle resources can be leisure, submarginal land or the relic of past error. Marginal-cost pricing holds only under pure competition. The pure rate of interest can be deduced independently from time preference, but a monopoly price has no such independent definition. “The concept of monopoly price as distinguished from competitive price is therefore untenable. We can speak only of the free-market price.” Location does not change this: every grocer on Fifth Street has a “location monopoly” against the grocer on Fourth. Neither do “natural monopolies” such as a city water company: “all prices on the free market are competitive.”

Labour unions

  • A union wage is not a monopoly price; Mises was first to see this. No one can own all the labour in a market, and the workers kept out are different people. It is a restrictionist price, and a worse one, because it is not limited by the elasticity of demand.
  • If a union sets a wage above the market, fewer are hired and the rest are pushed into lower-paid work. Generalize it and you get permanent mass unemployment. Industrial unions are more damaging than craft unions. The slogan of ending the “unfair competition of nonunion, low-wage labor” describes exactly this.
  • On a free market a union could keep its wage only if the displaced workers voluntarily refused to undercut it, hence the mystique against the “strikebreaker.” In practice unions’ power has come from violence against strikebreakers and employers’ property, with the police “neutral.” Unions become “private states” (Sylvester Petro).
  • Work rules and seniority lower productivity; seniority is “industrial serfdom” (David McCord Wright). But “to advocate coercive abolition of these work rules would imply literal enslavement.”
  • Three arguments for unions fail. Wages are rarely indeterminate in an advanced economy. Monopsony would need a cartel of all employers. The “Ricardo effect,” that higher wages force the use of more machinery, only shifts capital, which saving limits.

Monopolistic competition

  • Edward Chamberlin and Joan Robinson (both 1933) measured the real world against “pure competition,” where every firm faces perfectly elastic demand. Rothbard: that is impossible, since every firm affects its price, and there are no quantitative lines between “small,” “second order of smalls” and large. Chamberlin himself later wrote that “the welfare ideal itself … is correctly described as one of monopolistic competition.”
  • In oligopoly, firms either act independently, which is competition, or jointly, which is a cartel. “There is no third alternative.” Market rivalry is not “warfare” or a “game.”
  • The paradox of excess capacity. The theory says monopolistic competition gives higher prices, lower output and “excess capacity.” Roy Harrod, one of the theory’s originators, noticed that it made it “impossible to be an entrepreneur and not suffer from schizophrenia!”, since firms would knowingly build plants too big for the output they expected. Rothbard offers two ways out: real cost curves are angular, not smooth; and interest is a return, not a cost, so the demand and cost curves need not be tangent at all.
  • Selling costs are not a separate category. Every cost of production aims to raise demand, and advertising supplies information. It can’t create values. Mises’s example is the candlemakers who could not advertise their way past the electric bulb. Galbraith’s “countervailing power” falls with the rest.
  • Multiform prices. Price discrimination works only to final consumers, for goods that can’t be resold, and only if total revenue rises. Think of tourists haggling in a Middle Eastern bazaar, or a country doctor charging the rich more. It is not immoral, and buyers often prefer not to know. Reuben Kessel (1958) showed how organized medicine used it behind state limits on entry.

Patents and copyrights

  • They are usually lumped together, but they differ. Copyright infringement requires proof that the copier had access to the work; someone who creates the same thing independently infringes nothing. A patent bars John Doe, 2,000 miles away, who invents the same machine on his own.
  • So copyright fits the free market: a book or machine sold on condition that it not be reproduced. An inventor could stamp his machine “copyright” too. Patents invade the property of later independent inventors; they are “grants of exclusive monopoly privilege by the State.”
  • Free-market copyright would be perpetual. There is common-law copyright for unpublished manuscripts, but no common-law patent.
  • The utilitarian case that patents encourage research has no standard for “too much” or “too little” research. It resembles the infant-industry argument for tariffs, and patents distort research toward patentable fields. Those who want more invention are free to fund it. Rothbard cites R. A. Macfie’s nineteenth-century movement to abolish patents in England and Arnold Plant (1934): firms that don’t innovate fall behind, patent or no patent.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 11

Money and Its Purchasing Power

No single “price level,” no social gain from more money, banking as a warehouse business, and a long reckoning with Irving Fisher and Keynes.

The money relation

  • The purchasing power of money (PPM) is set by the stock of money and the demand for it: exchange demand from sellers of goods, plus reservation demand to hold cash. A rise in demand raises PPM; a rise in the stock lowers it. Labelling theories as “quantity,” “cash balance” or “income and expenditure” theories is absurd.
  • The stock of money is always owned by someone. Irving Fisher’s distinction between “circulating” and “idle” money is an error.
  • Any quantity of money is enough. Money’s usefulness lies wholly in what it can buy. “Money per se is therefore unproductive; it is dead stock and produces nothing,” and “there is no such thing as ‘too little’ or ‘too much’ money.” The law: “Every supply of money is always utilized to its maximum extent, and hence no social utility can be conferred by increasing the supply of money.” Gold mining isn’t waste, because gold has other uses.
  • In the ERE no one would hold money, because the future would be certain. Cash is held against uncertainty. Expecting money to lose value lowers its value now, but the process corrects itself; it is not “self-fulfilling.”
  • Clearing economizes on money. If A owes B 10, B owes C 10 and so on around to E owing A 10, fifty ounces of debt are settled with no cash at all.

Keynes, hoarding and liquidity preference

“Hoarding” is a misleading word, since it only means adding to cash balances. Rothbard calls Keynesianism “now happily beginning to wane after sweeping the economic world in the 1930’s and 1940’s,” and he takes its two pillars apart. The first says saving and investment are equalized by changes in national income, with the 45-degree-line diagram; his answer is “So what?” The second says income drives employment, so the economy can settle below full employment. That happens only if money wages are rigid downward, voluntarily (workers accepting union minimums) or by coercion. “The sum and substance of the ‘Keynesian Revolution’ was the thesis that there can be an unemployment equilibrium on the free market. … The only sense in which this is true was known years before Keynes.” Inflation to get around rigid wages relies on “money illusion,” and the unions learned. Liquidity preference fares no better. Franco Modigliani’s two-step account of how people decide to consume, invest and hold cash has them decide all three at once. A demand for cash can come out of consumption (lowering the interest rate), out of investment (raising it) or out of both. Ludwig Lachmann noted that if traders really feared falling prices they would sell forward in futures markets rather than sit on cash.

  • Components of the market interest rate. The pure rate, an entrepreneurial component, a purchasing-power component and a terms-of-trade component. Irving Fisher (1907) treated the purchasing-power component as lenders’ expectations of price changes. Rothbard argues that a fully anticipated change would be priced into factors at once, so the component reflects the change itself, not the expectation of it. If a firm buys factors for 100 and prices double before it sells the product for 240 two years later, the books show “70 percent per annum,” but the real return is 10 per cent. Businessmen who think they gain in every inflation are consuming capital; the belief “is purely illusory.”

Money warehouses and fractional reserves

  • A money warehouse that lends out its deposits or issues receipts for gold it doesn’t hold commits “outright fraud.” It is “difficult to see the economic or moral difference” between issuing pseudo receipts and embezzlement or counterfeiting. Most legal systems allow it; libertarian law would not. A bank run is like catching the embezzler.
  • Whether the claim is a note, a token coin or a demand deposit makes no difference. Time deposits that are payable on demand in practice are money too. “100-percent reserves” is a misleading phrase: the gold belongs to the depositors.
  • Rothbard stresses that he is not accusing bankers of knowingly defrauding anyone; hardly any banker sees it that way.
  • Objections to 100 per cent reserves fail. Banks could charge fees, and credit would still come from real savings lent and relent (as with Swiss bank debentures). The “free banking” school wants freedom for banks, but a free market is not freedom to commit fraud. Jevons: “any present grant or assignment of goods not in existence is without operation.”
  • Coinage could be a private business, like any other. Mints could stamp their brands, and denominations would follow demand. Even Milton Friedman (1960) conceded private coinage was feasible.

Who gains from new money

  • The Hume and Mill picture, in which everyone’s money doubles overnight, misleads. New money enters at particular points. Mr. Jones finds gold and spends it with Smith, who spends it with Robinson. Those who receive it first gain, because prices have not yet risen; those who receive it last lose. “The gains of the approximate first half of the recipients of new money are exactly counterbalanced by the losses of the second half.” Money is never neutral.
  • In a progressing economy the goods side pushes prices down; the money side can’t be predicted. That vindicates the “Austrian-Wicksteedian theory of price.”
  • Interlocal trade and payments. Money flows until its purchasing power is equal everywhere, allowing for transport. A New York restaurant meal and a Peoria meal are different goods. “Exports pay for imports” just means income pays for spending. The Antlers Lodge of Jonesville, with income from outsiders of 75 and spending on outsiders of 78, runs down its cash by 3. That is no more a problem than the balance of trade of the three Jones brothers, and the world’s balance of payments is zero.
  • Two coexisting moneys exchange at purchasing-power parity. If gold buys 50 pounds of butter and silver 25, one gold ounce equals two of silver; at 1:3 anyone could turn 50 pounds of butter into 75 by trading round. “In the late nineteenth century, most Western countries conducted a coup d’etat against silver.”

The equation of exchange, and measuring money

  • Fisher’s MV = PT rests on a non sequitur, “the purchasing power of money is the reciprocal of the level of prices.” If Smith buys 10 pounds of sugar at 7 cents, “70 cents = 10 pounds multiplied by 7 cents a pound” is a truism, not an equality of value. Add in a hat at $10, butter at 60 cents and a television at $500 and T becomes meaningless: pounds of sugar can’t be added to hats. “What is the pound weight of a concert?” V is an “absurd concept.”
  • No index can measure changes in the purchasing power of money. If sugar goes from 10 cents to 15, a hat from $10 to $20, a television from $500 to $300 and a lawyer’s hour from $5 to $8, “we do not even know for certain whether the PPM has risen or declined.” There is no average housewife to fill the market basket.
  • Fisher wrote that he “had come into economics from mathematical physics, in which fixed units of measure contribute the essential starting point.” But money has no fixed unit to stabilize. The voluntary “tabular standard” was never adopted, because debtors and creditors buy different things. “If we do not know what something is, we cannot very well act to keep it constant.”

The business cycle as a puzzle

Why does a boom in the capital-goods industries, the stock market and real estate end in a crash, with bank runs and idle capacity? The key question is: “Why is there a sudden revelation of business error?” Before the late seventeenth century, crises had external causes such as famine, plague, war or a king seizing bullion; after that the cycle took on its familiar pattern. “Overoptimism” explains nothing, as V. Lewis Bassie put it: “It is not the wave of optimism that makes times good.” Joseph Schumpeter’s three overlapping cycles (Kitchin, three years; Juglar, nine; Kondratieff, fifty) are a “mystical adoption of the fallacy of conceptual realism,” and his innovation theory can’t explain why innovations cluster. “Schumpeter almost hit on the right explanation” when he said the only alternative would be a cluster of entrepreneurial errors with no objective cause. “That is perfectly true—for the free, unhampered market!” The answer waits for chapter 12. Every cycle theory quietly assumes bank credit expansion.

  • The consumption function rests on budget studies and a brief run of 1930s data. Keynes said it was “fairly” stable; “What is ‘fairly’ supposed to mean?”
  • The multiplier follows from one line of algebra, and so does its reductio. Call the reader’s income R and everyone else’s V, and suppose V is always .99999 of the national income Y. Then Y = 100,000R, and “the reader’s spending will prime the pump of a 100,000-fold increase in the national income.”
  • The accelerator magnifies a 20 per cent rise in demand into a 200 per cent rise in machine orders only because of the arbitrary one-year period. Measured over the machines’ ten-year life (W. H. Hutt’s point), demand for machines rises from 10 to 12, the same 20 per cent.
  • “The overwhelming bulk of current business cycle theory is not economics at all, but meaningless manipulation of mathematical equations and geometric diagrams.”

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 12

The Economics of Violent Intervention in the Market

The 1962 edition’s bridge to Power and Market: a typology of force, the costs of price control, taxation and state enterprise, a critique of “growth” economics and Galbraith, and the book’s theory of the business cycle.

Types of intervention and their direct effects

  • Intervention is the intrusion of aggressive physical force into society. Most of it comes from states, since the state is “the only organization in society legally equipped to use violence” and “the only agency that legally derives its revenue from a compulsory levy.”
  • Three kinds. Autistic: commands over a subject’s own person or property, such as homicide, a compulsory salute, or banning a religious observance. Binary: a forced exchange or “gift” between intervener and subject, such as taxation, conscription or slavery. Triangular: compelling or forbidding an exchange between two subjects, such as price control or licensing. Economists usually count only the third as “intervention.”
  • The direct effect. On the free market everyone gains ex ante, so the market maximizes “social utility” in the only sense economics can use. Under intervention the coerced lose and the intervener gains. So “no State action can increase social utility.” Franz Oppenheimer’s “economic means” (production and exchange) and “political means” (seizure) mark the line. On the market there can be no exploitation; intervention creates conflict.
  • John C. Calhoun saw that taxation splits society into taxpayers and tax-consumers. Calhoun’s “classes” are really castes created by the state.
  • Majority support doesn’t make intervention voluntary. The police restraining a criminal are counter-intervention. Schumpeter mocked the theory of taxes as club dues: it “shows how far removed this part of the social sciences is from scientific habits of mind.”

Utility after the fact

The market has a quick test of error: profit and loss. Mises pointed out the paradox of “progressives” who think consumers too ignorant to buy wisely but competent to vote. Consumers who dislike a breakfast food stop buying it; voters get no such test. Schumpeter again: “The picture of the prettiest girl that ever lived will in the long run prove powerless to maintain the sales of a bad cigarette.” Government officials are paid by compulsory levy, and the “fittest” in government are those best at coercion or demagogy. A voter holds a tiny fraction of power over rulers he can’t fire; an owner holds absolute power over his property.

Price control and product control

  • A maximum price below the market rate creates a shortage, queues, favouritism and a black market. Selective controls make the shortage worse as resources move to other lines, so the standard excuse, “we must impose controls on this necessary product so long as it continues in short supply,” is “an almost ludicrous error.” A minimum price creates an unsold surplus; a minimum wage creates unemployment. General price ceilings amount to a minimum on the purchasing power of money: part of the money becomes useless, “anesthetized.”
  • Gresham’s law restated: “Money overvalued by the State will drive money undervalued by the State out of circulation.” It is a price-control result, not a law of the free market.
  • Prohibition, such as of liquor, hurts buyers and sellers alike and hands the business to black marketeers, who opposed repeal of Prohibition. Non-transferable ration coupons become a clumsy second money.
  • Monopoly grants. “In chapter 10 we buried the theory of monopoly price; we must now resurrect it.” A licence, tariff, quota or compulsory cartel defines a free-market price that the privileged price can be measured against. The gain goes to the privilege itself, as with New York City taxicab “medallions,” and is capitalized in their price. Closing-hour laws, bans on pushcart peddlers, antitrust laws, conscription and conservation laws all work as indirect grants.

The government budget and taxation

  • All government rests on taxation. Bureaucrats pay no net taxes: a private earner of $10,000 pays $2,000, but a bureaucrat’s “$2,000 tax” is a bookkeeping fiction; his income is simply $8,000. There is no “neutral tax.”
  • The burden of the budget is taxes or spending, whichever is greater. Distribution becomes a separate question only when binary intervention appears; the market has no “assumed distribution” to correct. The Chicago School backs the free market while advocating redistribution through taxes, relying on a “tax illusion.”
  • Income tax penalizes saving by lowering the return on it and by raising the taxpayer’s time preference as his real assets shrink. Irving Fisher’s argument that it taxes saving twice is rejected as irrelevant, and an economist’s bias for saving over consumption is “illicit smuggling of the less rational aspects of the ‘Protestant ethic’ into economic science.”
  • Proportional and progressive taxes, the head tax, the cost principle and the benefit principle all fail as “neutral” taxes. A general sales tax can’t be passed forward to consumers; “the general sales tax is therefore an income tax, albeit a rather haphazard one.” Henry George’s single tax rests on the false idea that landowners do nothing productive. (Power and Market ch. 4 expands all of this.)

Government spending and socialism

  • Measured by what it spends, government seems to produce a lot. In fact “the government’s productive contribution to the economy is precisely zero,” or negative once Bastiat’s unseen costs are counted. There is no government “investment,” only consumption by officials and “waste assets.”
  • Subsidies penalize the efficient and subsidize the predatory. “We can have exactly as many paupers as the country chooses to pay for” (Thomas Mackay, 1896). Mayor La Guardia banned New York’s organ-grinders despite a Park Avenue woman’s plea for her favourite.
  • “Free” services produce chronic shortages: of police, of teachers and schools, of space on government streets. New York City exhorts consumers to use less water. A state enterprise can’t operate on a “business basis,” because taxes give it unlimited funds and no check. When France nationalized the Western Railway in 1908, a critic noted that the state had “added railway accidents to its growing list of monopolies.” Every government firm is an island of calculational chaos.
  • Command posts. Everywhere the state holds defence, money, rivers and coastal seas, streets and highways, public land and eminent domain, the post office, and much of education.
  • “Public” ownership is a myth: try appropriating your aliquot share. Officials own only the current use, so they exploit property fast. Social security spends its receipts and gives itself bonds, so the public pays twice.
  • Socialism is “the violent abolition of the market.” Nazi and Fascist central planning count as socialism. Socialism is underestimated in the United States, where government lending creates many centres of state ownership, and overestimated in Soviet Russia, which borrows world prices and relies on black markets. A “centrally ‘planned’ economy is a centrally prohibited economy.”

Growth, affluence and government

  • “What is so good about growth?” Economists smuggle in the value judgment that faster is better. On the free market each person chooses his own rate of saving; compulsory growth benefits some at others’ expense, so “the Wertfrei economist is therefore not permitted to say that ‘society’ grows.” Soviet growth was growth in capital goods, not living standards.
  • Milton Friedman (1958) called an Egyptian steel mill “the modern equivalent of the Pyramids, except that maintenance expenses are higher.” Following P. T. Bauer, Rothbard defines development as “an increase in the range of effective alternatives open to people” and prefers agricultural tools to prestige dams and steel mills.
  • W. W. Rostow’s The Stages of Economic Growth (1960), with its five stages and “take-off,” repeats the old German historicist error of laws of history, treats technology as the prime mover, and forgets that mass consumption began with the cheap textiles of the Industrial Revolution. Hong Kong’s manufacturing boom shows the free-market way.
  • Galbraith’s The Affluent Society (1958) indicts capitalism for being too affluent and not growing fast enough at once. Its “sustained sneer” at the “conventional wisdom” assumes that marginal utility falls to zero and that advertising creates wants, while exempting the author, and government, from the same logic. Galbraith conceded that for his “social balance” “no test can be applied, for none exists.” Rothbard answers that every “collective good” was once supplied privately, including roads, coinage, mail, fire and police protection, courts and defence, and cites T. S. Ashton on eighteenth-century English private roads and canals. At a 1958 symposium Moses Abramovitz denounced leisure and wanted a “modicum of purposive, disciplined activity.”

Inflation, credit expansion and the business cycle

On a free market money is specie. Inflation is “the process of issuing pseudo warehouse receipts or, more exactly, the process of issuing money beyond any increase in the stock of specie.” It creates money “out of thin air,” helps the first receivers at the expense of the last (fixed-income groups, pensioners, “widows and orphans”), discourages saving, and misleads accountants: costing assets at their historical price shows illusory profits and leads firms to consume capital. When the new money is lent to business, it produces the cycle.

  • The theory explains the two facts other theories can’t: why errors come in a cluster, and why they hit capital-goods industries hardest. Underconsumption theories have it backwards. The crisis comes from “overconsumption” or “undersaving” relative to the investments made.
  • Government action prolongs depressions. Nineteenth-century economists who compared the boom to an alcoholic or narcotic jag were right: more doses only delay and deepen the collapse. “The way to prevent a depression, then, is simple: avoid starting a boom,” by 100 per cent specie reserves. Credit contraction in the crisis speeds the adjustment and creates no new malinvestment.
  • The theory is Mises’s and Hayek’s, with Lionel Robbins and Fritz Machlup. It does not “assume full employment,” as critics charge.
  • Limits on credit expansion. Depositors’ demand for redemption and the fear of runs, which deposit “insurance” removed. The size of each bank’s clientele: a bank that expands faster than its rivals loses gold to them as its notes pass to their customers. In international terms this is the specie drain the British Currency School described. Free banking limits expansion better than central banking, but only 100 per cent reserves stop it; Amasa Walker: if only specie-backed notes were issued, “banking may be as free as brokerage.”
  • Government as promoter. Suspension of specie payments, the central bank as “lender of last resort” and “bankers’ bank,” legal tender laws, deposit insurance, lower reserve requirements, open-market purchases. Off gold, the only limit left is the external drain. The United States in 1962 was on a “restricted international gold bullion standard,” redeemable only by foreign central banks. The inflationist “ideal” would be a world fiat money issued by a world central bank.
  • The runaway boom. Once people expect prices to keep rising they cut their cash holdings, and inflation turns into hyperinflation and a “flight from money.” The market breaks down into barter, as in Germany in 1923, and the public rids itself of the inflation burden “by its ultimate weapon”: refusing to hold the government’s money. A world fiat money that collapsed would leave no older money to return to.
  • Price rises are not the definition of inflation. To stop it, “all that is really necessary is that the government and the banks … cease inflating.” Keynesian “compensatory” fiscal policy relies on the money illusion. The free market walks no tightrope between inflation and depression; government creates the tightrope.

Conclusion and two appendices

“Proudhon, indeed, wrote better than he knew when he called ‘Liberty, the Mother, not the Daughter, of Order.’” Coercion is exploitative and “profoundly disorderly”; the free market is “a system of co-operative exchanges in the exploitation of nature alone.” Appendix A shows that government borrowing from banks is inflation and borrowing from the public diverts savings. The claim that “we owe it to ourselves” is absurd, and repudiation, though it would damage future government credit, is the one way to cut the debt without new taxes or inflation. Appendix B takes on “collective goods.” Paul Samuelson (1954) defined them as goods “which all enjoy in common in the sense that each individual’s consumption of such a good leads to no subtraction from any other individual’s consumption,” with defence and lighthouses as the standard cases. Rothbard answers that defence of New York is not defence of San Francisco and a lighthouse lights only its own area, so truly collective goods are natural conditions like air. As for “free riders”: “am I to be taxed for enjoying the view of my neighbor’s well-kept garden?” “We are all, therefore, free riders on the past.” Forcing beneficiaries to pay for benefits they never chose to buy, as A. C. Pigou proposed for “external economies,” is still coercion.

Part II

Power and Market

1970, seven chapters, pages 1047–1369. Written as the third volume of the treatise and published separately. Now force enters. Rothbard borrows Franz Oppenheimer’s distinction between the economic means of getting wealth (producing and trading) and the political means (taking it), and traces what the political means does to the market Part I described. Many examples come from the 1960s: Yugoslav reform, the disappearing silver coins, the Civil Aeronautics Board.

Power and Market · Chapter 1

Defense Services on the Free Market

Before analysing what the state does, Rothbard asks whether it is needed at all, and answers no.

The laissez-faire contradiction

Free exchange is exchange of property titles, so an economist has to say what property would look like in a free society: self-ownership, plus ownership of unowned resources a person finds and transforms. Man, Economy, and State assumed that no one invades property. Yet economists “have almost invariably and paradoxically assumed that the market must be kept free by the use of invasive and unfree actions.” The laissez-faire liberal wants a government to defend property, but that government must seize its revenue by taxation and claim a compulsory monopoly of defence over a territory. The agency meant to stop invasion of property becomes its biggest invader, despite the state’s “black historical record as the great engine of invasive violence.” Defence services, like every other service, should be “marketable and marketable only.”

Three objections, answered

  • “The state must define property.” No: the principles of a free society already define it (self-ownership and homesteading), and reason and the market can apply them.
  • “Defence is a precondition of the market.” This “proves far too much.” Land room, food, clothing, shelter and, today, paper are preconditions too, and nobody says the state must supply them. Goods are valued in marginal units, not whole classes.
  • “There must be one final court.” The world has never had one. An Argentinian who thinks a man from Ceylon wronged him sues in an Argentinian court, and the Ceylonese courts recognize the result. If people in northern Montana and Saskatchewan can live together without a common government, so can people in northern and southern Montana. Laissez-faire liberals should logically want world government, but almost none do; and if Canada may secede from the United States, why not the South, New York City, Manhattan, each block, each house, each person?

How it might work

  • Much of the law merchant, admiralty law and common law was made by privately chosen judges, and the fairs of Champagne had competing courts (Bruno Leoni, Freedom and the Law, 1961).
  • No one can blueprint a future market, any more than someone 50 years earlier could have predicted the television industry. But defence would probably be sold by subscription, like insurance, and insurance companies would have every reason to reduce crime.
  • Smith, a subscriber of Agency Y, says Jones, a subscriber of Agency X, robbed him. Y’s court tries Jones, with no power of subpoena. If Jones rejects the verdict, the case goes to X’s court or to one of a few competing appeals courts. The legal code would probably make any two courts’ agreement binding, since there are two parties. The code itself would simply spell out the libertarian axiom (no violence against person or property except in defence), and judges would pledge to follow it.
  • What if an agency turns criminal? No change in human nature is assumed; some agencies would go bad, as some people do now. But there would be no “regular, legalized channel for crime,” no state apparatus to capture. Building one from scratch took rulers centuries. Competing agencies are the real checks and balances. The strictly limited government is “truly Utopian”: it “has never worked historically.” And “the worst that could possibly happen would be for the State to be reestablished,” so the experiment has “nothing to lose and everything to gain.”
  • Auberon Herbert (1912): if A and B force C to join a state and pay for it, “what is all this but Socialism on a reduced scale?”

Power and Market · Chapter 2

Fundamentals of Intervention

A taxonomy of force, and the case that no intervention can be shown to raise social welfare.

The state is “uniquely the agency engaged in regularized violence on a large scale,” but the analysis applies to anyone who uses force, legally or not. Oppenheimer contrasted two ways to satisfy wants, work and robbery: “the economic means” and “the political means.” “The state is an organization of the political means.” As far as he knew, he notes, no one before him had classified the types of intervention systematically.

  • Economists recognize only triangular intervention. Many “free market” writers treat conscription and income taxes as no interference at all, although taxes and conscripts are paid in money and so enter the market. In American politics, “left-wingers” oppose autistic intervention and tolerate triangular, “right-wingers” the reverse, and both are ambivalent about binary.
  • Intervention creates conflict. On the market every exchange benefits both parties, so “there can be no such thing as exploitation” and “all is harmony.” Under intervention the intervener gains at the subject’s expense, and everyone scrambles to be on the invading team. Calhoun’s taxpayers and tax-consumers are castes made by the state, not Marxian classes. “Castes” is the better word: the class of men named Jones does not conflict with the class named Smith.
  • Democracy doesn’t make it voluntary. Support for the state is mostly passive and resigned. People vote for the “lesser of the two evils,” words no one uses about a new suit or refrigerator. Before Britain adopted conscription in 1939, 39 per cent of voters favoured it; a week after it became law, 58 per cent did (Lindsay Rogers). Every state rests on majority opinion, as David Hume saw, but majority coercion is still coercion.
  • A defence agency that stops a thief reduces the thief’s utility, but the conflict was introduced by the invader, not the agency. Envy that never shows up in action is “pure moonshine” to praxeology.
  • Utility after the fact. Wesley C. Mitchell wrote of “The Backward Art of Spending Money,” but consumers have direct tests and voters have none. Inflate the money supply and the government can blame “wicked speculators or alien black marketeers.” In the market, people choose absolutely over their own property; the voter has “a 1/50 millionth power” over rulers who then decide for years. And the indirect consequences of intervention usually disappoint its own supporters.

Power and Market · Chapter 3

Triangular Intervention

Controls on prices and products, and the long list of privileges that restrict who may sell.

Price control

  • A control is ineffective if it doesn’t bind: banning car sales below 20 ounces when cars sell at 100 yields “only irrelevant jobs for government bureaucrats.”
  • Maximum prices create a shortage even if supply is fixed, and a worse one the more elastic supply is. The result is queues, favouritism for old customers, “under-the-table” deals and a black market. “A bribe is the sale, by the government official, of permission for the exchanges to proceed.” Selective control shifts resources to substitutes, which is why the usual excuse (“we must impose controls on this product as long as it is in short supply”) is “an almost ludicrous error.”
  • Minimum prices create unsold surpluses and pull resources into the wrong line. A minimum wage above a worker’s productivity means “involuntary mass unemployment.” It makes no difference whether the state or a union imposes it.
  • General price ceilings, usually imposed to “prevent inflation” while the government inflates, set a minimum on the purchasing power of money. Part of the people’s money becomes unspendable. The government first takes resources by inflating, then destroys part of the money’s usefulness.
  • Usury laws hurt the risky borrowers they claim to protect by cutting them off from credit. The myth that medieval usury laws protected needy consumers has it backwards.

Prohibition

  • Banning a product hurts consumers and producers alike; only the bureaucrats gain. The long-run loss falls on workers and landowners in the banned trade.
  • Black markets are scarcer, dearer, can’t advertise and can’t grow large. Prohibition is a grant of privilege to black marketeers, whose skill is evading the law or bribing officials. Most “organized crime” is mala prohibita, serving consumers against a ban, not mala per se.
  • Rationing creates “an inefficient kind of double money” in ration tickets (Henry Hazlitt’s novel The Great Idea, 1951). Priorities and allocations cripple efficient firms. Maximum-hour laws forbid work.

Grants of monopolistic privilege

Monopoly price theory is an illusion on the free market but “applies fully” to grants of privilege, because now there is a free-market price to compare against. A privilege makes consumers’ demand less elastic by removing their alternatives. The monopoly gain goes to “the right to enter the industry,” lasts as long as the privilege, and is capitalized into the firm’s value, so later owners earn only the going interest.

  • Licences to workers always raise their wages, because labour, unlike a firm, can’t expand to take up the slack. The Civil Aeronautics Board shut down Trans-American Airlines despite its perfect safety record and its pioneering low fares.
  • Quality and safety standards. Consumers should be free to choose cheaper, lower-quality treatment for minor ills. Complaints of doctor and teacher “shortages” are heard only in licensed trades. Free medical advice needs no licence, only paid advice, which shows the purpose is to restrict competition. Fraud (straw sold as breakfast food) is theft to be punished in court, with damages paid to the victim. A builder whose building collapses and kills people should go to jail for manslaughter, rather than follow a code written in advance that blocks innovation. Punishing “prevention” in advance would justify any invasion. The SEC and “blue-sky laws” keep new, small firms from raising capital.
  • Tariffs. On a free market there would be no separate theory of international trade. Take it to its logical end: Jones and his hired man Smith put a 1,000 per cent tariff on everything from outside “Jones’ Acres,” and end up working dawn to dusk for bare subsistence. The tariff’s logical goal is “poverty for all, and death for most.” Henry George: “It is not from foreigners that protection preserves and defends us; it is from ourselves.” The infant-industry argument (Taussig) reverses the right conclusion: if a new location is truly better, private investors will build there without help (Mises).
  • Immigration restrictions win restrictionist wages for a high-wage area at the expense of excluded workers, push capital abroad instead, and may leave the home country below its optimum population. India is “overpopulated” only because its people choose to stay or other governments won’t let them in. A consistent restrictionist would have to favour compulsory birth control. Oscar Cooley and Paul Poirot: why not bar a buggy worker from Massachusetts from taking a job in Detroit’s car plants?
  • Child labour laws and compulsory schooling are compulsory unemployment. They raise adult wages and penalize families with children. A 1955 New York news item boasted that thousands of teenage boys would get odd jobs from 26,000 grocers, jobs “which do not interfere with union jobs.”
  • Conscription takes labour at far below the market wage and raises the wages of those left.
  • Antitrust. “The only viable definition of monopoly is a grant of privilege from the government.” The antitrust laws impose “continual, capricious harassment” under criteria that can’t be defined: size, closeness of substitutes, prices too high, too low or the same as a rival’s. Competition is a process, not a quantity. If cartels must be broken up, so must all corporations and partnerships. “One conspicuous form of such restraint is antitrust legislation itself!” Isabel Paterson: “Standard Oil did not restrain trade; it went out to the ends of the earth to make a market.”
  • Outlawing basing-point pricing gave each local firm a monopoly haven protected by rivals’ freight costs, and penalized small firms that couldn’t build branches everywhere.
  • Conservation laws force the present to save for the future. “What did the future ever do to deserve privileged treatment?” The future will probably be richer, and it will in turn be sacrificed to a further “wraithlike” future. Technology keeps changing what counts as a resource: oil was worthless to earlier generations. In 1832 J. D. Brown worried: “Whence shall we procure supplies of timber fifty years hence for the continuance of our navy?” Commons such as the Western open range and ocean fisheries were overused because no one could own them. The Homestead Act of 1862 capped farms at a size useless for Western ranching.
  • Who gained from conservation. Reserving public forests raised the value of private timber and of the land-grant railroads’ holdings (fifteen-mile strips on either side of their tracks). The railroads paid $39,000 of the roughly $50,000 annual budget of the National Irrigation Association, the most vigorous advocate of land-law reform outside the administration (E. Louise Peffer), and timber owners reached a “harmonious understanding” with Gifford Pinchot by 1903.
  • Patents invade the property of later independent inventors; they are not property in ideas. There is no standard for “too much” or “too little” research. (A footnote says Charles I introduced the patent to evade Parliament’s 1624 ban on monopolies; in fact the Statute of Monopolies itself exempted new inventions.)
  • Franchises and “public utilities.” Every good is useful to the public, so the label is arbitrary. Eminent domain is “a license for theft”; made universal, it would produce “a society of mutual plunder.”
  • Bribery of officials buys an informal licence. A defensive bribe, bought to operate at all, moves toward the free market; an invasive bribe, bought to exclude rivals, moves away from it.
  • The way to end monopoly is for the state to abolish its own grants. Businessmen often lobbied for these laws (Gabriel Kolko, The Triumph of Conservatism, 1963), and free-market advocates are “probusiness” only on a free market. Corporations with limited liability announced to creditors are no privilege, though limited liability for torts is.
  • Appendices. Private mints would be policed by fraud law, reputation and assayers, as delicate machine tools are. And an immigration barrier imposed by one state is the cause of “overpopulation” in another, a praxeological root of the Lebensraum arguments behind wars. A state can bar peaceful immigrants only by claiming to own all its territory.

Power and Market · Chapter 4

Binary Intervention: Taxation

Who really pays, why the level matters more than the type, why no tax can be neutral, and why every “canon of justice” in taxation fails.

Taxes, burdens and benefits

  • Praxeologically, taxation and inflation are no different from robbery and counterfeiting. The difference between a government and a criminal band is “a matter of degree rather than kind”: bandits who win passive consent become a state, and a government defeated in civil war becomes a band. Frank Chodorov’s title Taxation Is Robbery (1947) is accurate; he traced taxes to Rhine toll-gates and the Danegeld.
  • Bureaucrats pay no taxes. A $5,000 salary with $1,000 “paid back” is a $4,000 salary; the UN’s tax-exempt staff are “far more candid.” Officials are the full-time rulers, subsidized groups the part-time ones.
  • Tax the codfish industry to buy armaments and the ripples spread, with the long-run burden and gain landing on each industry’s specific factors, mainly landowners.
  • Taxation creates “the problem of distribution.” On a free market there is no distribution apart from production and exchange. The slogan that “capitalism has solved the problem of production” and the state must now solve distribution is wrong on both counts: production won’t be solved “until we are all in the Garden of Eden,” and the free market has no separate distribution to solve. And “by far the most important impact of taxation results not so much from the type of tax as from its amount.”

Taxes on incomes

  • “No tax can be shifted forward.” Prices are already where they maximize net revenue, the stock hasn’t changed, and demand hasn’t changed. If firms could simply add a tax to the price, they would already have raised prices. A general 20 per cent sales tax can’t raise prices at all without more money or less demand for money. If a movie ticket costs $1.00, “85 cents plus 15 cents tax,” then $1.00 is the price. The tax falls back on wages and ground rents. “The general sales tax is an income tax,” though a haphazard one. Harry Gunnison Brown made the first modern attack on the forward-shifting fallacy. Rothbard says this discussion “breaks new ground”: the Austrians never applied their value theory to tax incidence.
  • An excise tax on one product drives marginal firms and nonspecific factors out, so supply falls and price rises. That is not “shifting,” just the tax’s effect.
  • An income tax can’t be shifted. It makes leisure cheaper, penalizes work for money against work in kind (the working woman against the housewife, “do-it-yourself”), and, high enough, would break the market down. It raises the taxpayer’s time preference and lowers the net return on saving.
  • Irving Fisher’s argument that the income tax taxes saving twice is “irrelevant.” Free-market economists who treat saving as especially sacred are implicitly asking for coerced saving: “the free-market advocate should oppose with equal fervor coerced distortion … in either direction.”
  • The employer’s share of social security and unemployment taxes falls wholly on the workers.
  • Corporate income tax. The Star Corporation earns $100,000 and keeps $60,000, paying out $40,000. Under the law, Jones (40 per cent), Smith (35) and Robinson (25) have incomes of $16,000, $14,000 and $10,000, and the corporation $100,000. In truth their incomes are $40,000, $35,000 and $25,000. The double tax encourages retaining earnings and “freezes” investment in old firms.
  • An excess profits tax freezes the economy in the pattern of some past base period, just when change is needed: “It is difficult to find a tax more indefensible from more points of view than this one.”
  • Capital gains are profit, and so income. A uniform income tax would tax accrued gains, corrected for inflation, instead of business income, not both. Taxing only realized gains freezes holdings for generations. An asset bought at 50 that rises 10 a year and is sold at 90 is taxed on 40 in the fourth year, though the income that year was 10. Since no index can measure inflation, uniform taxation of income is conceptually impossible. (Rothbard stresses that he is not advocating uniform taxation.)

Taxes on accumulated capital

  • Taxing capital is genuine double taxation, and it kills “the goose that lays the golden egg.” Here the type matters as well as the level: a 20 per cent tax on capital does far more harm than 20 per cent on income.
  • Inheritance and gift taxes are the purest taxes on capital. They also penalize charity and family ties, often at the urging of people who say private charity is never “enough.”
  • Property taxes depend on arbitrary assessments, which invite favouritism and bribery. They tax debt twice: if C holds a $6,000 bond on B’s $10,000 property, B’s property is assessed at $16,000.
  • Tax capitalization. A $10,000 house earns $500 a year at 5 per cent. A 1 per cent annual tax cuts its value to $8,333 (tax $83, net $417, still 5 per cent). The owner at the time bears the whole loss; later buyers pay the tax but not the burden. A general property tax lowers the interest rate instead.
  • A tax on individual wealth, never tried, would be worse. Smith and Robinson each own $100,000; Smith earns $50,000 a year, Robinson $1,000. A 10 per cent wealth tax forces Robinson to sell his assets. “No quicker route could be found to promote capital consumption and general impoverishment.”

The progressive tax, and the level of taxes

  • Progression penalizes service to consumers. The critics’ charges about work and saving are true, but a proportional tax does much the same.
  • The argument that progression cuts saving is weak for free-market economists. Consistently applied, it would have them “advocate taxation of the poor to subsidize the rich.”
  • The poor, being more numerous, may bear the greatest total burden (Gabriel Kolko, Wealth and Power in America, 1962). High rates in practice need progression, because the many would revolt against steep rates imposed on everyone.
  • The Chicago School supports the market yet endorses redistribution through taxes, relying on a “tax illusion” that people act on gross rather than net income.

The single tax on ground rent

  • Henry George rightly called attention to the land question, but “every one of its doctrines is fallacious.” The gains George promised come from abolishing other taxes, not from taxing land.
  • No one can separate ground rent from the return on improvements. Idle land earns no rent, so a tax on rent can’t force it into use. Its capital value reflects expected future rents. Idle land is actually a good sign: if all land were used, labour would be abundant relative to land, the stage of true overpopulation.
  • A Georgist tract said India and China have plenty of land and labour, so they can’t be short of capital. Rothbard: “India and China are short of capital because they are short of time.”
  • Landowners do produce. They bring sites into use and allocate them to the best bidders. Spencer Heath: “Land itself does not service civilized men any more than food itself does. Both are served to them.” A 100 per cent tax would drive land values and rents to zero, so “it would yield no revenue at all!” It would act as a maximum price: first-comers would grab the best sites, downtowns would be overcrowded, outlying sites underused. The result would be “locational chaos,” amounting to nationalization of land. George admitted the single tax would “accomplish the same thing [as land nationalization] in a simpler, easier, and quieter way.”
  • The real land problem is violation of the “first-user, first-owner” rule. When the state sells unused land to speculators, the price a later user pays is really a tax, a form of tax farming. Feudal land grants are permanent regional taxation. Franz Oppenheimer: “In the beginning was the ground rent.” Mises: “Nowhere and at no time has the large-scale ownership of land come into being through the working of economic forces in the market.” Stolen property passes legitimately to the thief’s heirs only if the victims and their heirs can’t be traced.

Canons of “justice” in taxation

  • Economists gave up searching for the “just price” and accepted the market price. Yet “in no other area of his subject does the economist become more grandiosely ethical” than on taxes. The “just tax” can’t be the market tax, because on the market there are no taxes. And the justice of taxation itself has never been proved.
  • Adam Smith’s maxims (low cost of collection, convenience, certainty) are not self-evident. The bureaucrat who collects taxes prefers high costs. An unjust tax is better collected badly. Some conservatives once favoured the income tax because it was inconvenient enough to provoke revolt. Uncertainty permits bribery.
  • Uniformity is no virtue if the tax is unjust: should everyone be enslaved equally? An exemption is not a subsidy. “To blame him for escaping is equivalent to blaming the slave for fleeing his master.” Mises: “What is a loophole? … Thanks to these loopholes this country is still a free country.” And uniformity is impossible anyway: bureaucrats can’t pay taxes, and “income” can’t be defined (food grown for oneself, the owner’s own house, a housewife’s work, averaging, inflation).
  • Ability to pay is ambiguous and has never been justified. E. R. A. Seligman’s claim that the rich enjoy “a law of increasing returns” is wrong, since money does not “make money” by itself. Harold Groves likened taxes to gifts to the Red Cross: “seldom have more fallacies been packed into two sentences.” Pricing by ability to pay would wreck the market. The principle resembles “the highwayman’s principle of taking where the taking is good.”
  • Sacrifice theories need interpersonal utility comparisons, which Lionel Robbins, then “decidedly ‘Misesian,’” discredited in the 1930s. Equal sacrifice might even call for regressive taxes. Minimum sacrifice would take Jones’s $50,000 income down to Smith’s $30,000 before taking a cent from Smith. A philosophical anarchist’s sacrifice is infinite, and he would be exempt. “If taxation is pure hurt, why endure it at all?”
  • The benefit principle would make welfare recipients pay for their own doles and the weak pay more for police than the strong. Policing a bank vault worth $100 million costs less than guarding 100 acres worth $10 an acre. Bureaucrats would have to “serve without pay.” Coercion implies the payers do not think they benefit.
  • An equal head tax (“one vote, one tax”) comes closer to a market price than a proportional tax, and the cost principle comes closest. But specific costs can’t be known without a market, and a monopoly’s costs are inflated. So: “either abandon the goal of neutrality, or abandon taxation itself.”

Voluntary contributions

  • William F. Warren, president of Boston University, described living in a community (like old Hamburg) where each citizen, unsworn and unchecked, dropped what he thought fair into a “strong public chest,” and “every annual call of the authorities on that community was honored to the full” (Boston University Year Book, 1876). But gifts separate payment from service, which invites overuse.
  • A poll tax that only voters pay is really a price for joining the state, and few would vote. Politicians of every party urge “We don’t care how you vote, but vote!” because they want a “mandate.”
  • “Voluntary taxation” (Auberon Herbert, J. Greevz Fisher, sometimes Gustave de Molinari) is unstable. Either the state bans competing defence agencies, which is compulsory monopoly, or it allows them and soon is just one agency among many. As a one-vote-per-person “co-operative,” it would probably “wither away” in competition with joint-stock agencies.

Power and Market · Chapter 5

Binary Intervention: Government Expenditures

Government spending doesn’t add to output. It redirects resources, it can’t calculate, and it breeds conflict.

Subsidies

  • Writers on public finance describe government spending but rarely analyse it. C. Lowell Harriss even said a theory of it was impossible. That spending must grow to “cope with the growing complexities of the economy” is a slogan, never proved.
  • Transfer payments open the political means to wealth and create a separate distribution process, “not ‘redistribution.’” “All cases of subsidy coercively penalize the efficient for the benefit of the inefficient.” Energy shifts from production to politics, and those who rise are best at coercion and winning favours.
  • State poor relief subsidizes poverty, and per-child payments encourage larger families. Thomas Mackay (1896): “the cause of pauperism is relief … we can have exactly as many paupers as the country chooses to pay for.” Governments drive out “charity rackets” and beggars to steer giving through official channels. Unemployment relief lets unions hold out for wages above the market.

Government versus private ownership

  • All government spending on resources is consumption by officials, not “investment,” whatever the Keynesians say.
  • “Free” services such as police, schools, streets and water produce chronic shortages. There are police shortages in crime districts, teacher and school shortages, traffic jams on government roads. New York and other cities exhort consumers to use less water, and when it runs short the consumers get blamed. No private firm behaves like that.
  • Government can’t run on a “business basis.” Taxation gives it virtually unlimited funds and no checkrein, so it has no rational way to choose between road A and road B, or to decide how much to spend at all. The answer to every shortage is “more money.” “It is impossible to play enterprise,” since managers risk none of their own money. And “if business operation is so desirable, why take such a tortuous route?” Just sell the enterprise.
  • The US Post Office was made a compulsory monopoly. When France nationalized the Western Railway in 1908, freight damage, delays and accidents grew, and a critic said the government had “added railway accidents to its growing list of monopolies.” Raising New York subway fares to end the deficit drove riders into cars and made the deficit bigger.
  • “Each governmental firm introduces its own island of chaos into the economy; there is no need to wait for socialism for chaos to begin its work.”
  • Conflict. Government service must be uniform, so it pits citizens against each other. Parents fight over segregated or integrated schools, and over socialist or antisocialist teaching. The market would supply all of them.
  • Command posts, always held by the state: police and military, courts, the mint and the definition of money, rivers and coastal seas, streets and highways and unused land, and the post office (useful for inspecting heretics’ mail). Education is nearly one too. Through its money monopoly the state has achieved “the ability to expand its revenue by inflating the currency at will.”
  • Social security confiscates wage earners’ income, then spends it and gives itself bonds, so “the public must pay twice.”

Socialism and “public” ownership

  • Socialism is “the violent abolition of the market.” Economics is not concerned with “the color of the uniform”: “The Nazi and Fascist regimes were as socialist as the Communist system.” Every step toward state control is a step toward socialism.
  • Its extent is underestimated in the United States, where government loans and loan guarantees make the state a part owner, and overestimated in Soviet Russia, which prices by the world market and survives on black markets and blat. By 1970 Eastern Europe was moving toward markets, Yugoslavia furthest, with private and co-operative ownership and no central planning of investment.
  • “Public” ownership is a myth. Try claiming your share of the post office in court. Officials own the use but not the capital value, so they “milk the property as quickly as he can.” F. A. Harper: “If I cannot sell a thing, it is evident that I do not really own it.” The private owner, not the official, takes the long view.

Democracy

  • If the majority votes in a dictator, democracy ends; if it may not, the majority does not rule. Democracy fits neither the purely free society nor socialism, so it is unstable and transitional.
  • National democracy is self-contradictory. If the majority of a country may rule it, why not the majority of a region, a town, a block? Deny secession and you must accept world majority rule. Technology now permits direct democracy by voting device on each television set; defenders of representative government who say voters can’t judge issues can’t explain how they judge candidates.
  • “Ballots for bullets.” If elections substitute for civil war, they should give the result a fight would have given. Women, the old, the sick and the 4-F would then lose the vote, trained soldiers and police would get several, and apathetic voters would be screened out by poll taxes, distant polling booths, write-in ballots and open voting. Democracy as practised fails its own best argument.

Power and Market · Chapter 6

Antimarket Ethics: A Praxeological Critique

The ethical case against the market, tested for consistency and conceptual possibility rather than answered with a rival ethic.

What praxeology may say about ethics

Economics furnishes no ultimate values, but it can veto ethical goals that are self-contradictory or conceptually impossible, and “if the end is absurd, so is the approach toward that end.” (A footnote: “the means must be justified by the end. What else but an end can justify a means?”) Some criticisms of the market are purely factual and have been answered already. Cycles and unemployment come from credit expansion and wage floors. “Monopoly” in any meaningful sense is a state grant, or Crusoe and Friday would be wicked monopolists. And there is nothing people can’t do for themselves that only government can do. The rest mix in ethics.

  • “People don’t know their own interest.” The free-market case never assumed a “Perfectly Wise Individual,” only a right to pursue one’s interest as one sees it. People can hire experts and test them; government experts are paid by compulsory levy whether or not they help. It is a contradiction to treat people as incompetent to run their lives but competent to vote for those who will.
  • Immoral choices. Whiskey instead of milk, cosmetics instead of books. Mises showed that controlling one consumer choice leads logically to controlling all: a ruler who loves Bach and thinks Mozart immoral would have the same right to ban Mozart. But the real question is: “Can force advance morality?” Mr. Jones, forbidden at gunpoint to do the immoral thing, is not moral; he has been deprived of the chance to be. “There is no sense to any concept of morality … if a man is not free to do the immoral as well as the moral thing.” Morality spreads only by persuasion.
  • Human nature. Libertarians don’t assume men are angels. If all men were evil, as F. A. Harper asked, the rule “government to the extent of evil” would produce a totally evil dictator with total power. Since people mix good and evil, the best system is one that gives evil no legitimate channel. The state is “a ready, swift channel for the exercise of evil.”
  • Equality is impossible. Human diversity is a postulate of economics, and equality would reduce society to “the mindless uniformity of the ant heap.” Worse, it can’t be reached even in principle. How can a New Yorker’s enjoyment of the Manhattan skyline be equalized with an Indian’s, or the Indian’s swim in the Ganges with the New Yorker’s? Equality of opportunity fails for the same reason, and pursuing it would mean abolishing the family and raising babies in state nurseries. “Egalitarianism is, therefore, a literally senseless social philosophy.” Spencer’s Law of Equal Freedom is better, but Clara Dixon Davidson showed (1892) that its second clause is redundant; call it “the Law of Total Freedom.”
  • Security. The market relieves uncertainty through savings, entrepreneurs (who pay workers in advance and carry the risk, including through futures markets), insurance and voluntary charity. The state produces nothing and can make some secure only by making others insecure. The one security it could give everyone, security against aggression, only a non-state defence agency can supply.
  • The society of status. Intellectuals, “conspicuously not craftsmen or peasants,” idealize medieval belonging. But the Middle Ages lived with famine; the Happy Craftsman was usually the king’s craftsman; the serf’s “belonging” was enforced by violence; and a status society can’t absorb a growing population except by famine, infanticide or beggary. A free society lets anyone form a commune, and nineteenth-century Utopias were quickly abandoned.
  • Charity and poverty. Being “free to starve” is a fact of nature, not a lack of freedom. Only capitalist production has reduced poverty. Coerced “charity” is the opposite of charity, deadens giving, and exalts “the poor” into a privileged caste. In the long run, “selfish” capital investment is the greatest charity.
  • “Selfish materialism.” There are no “economic ends.” A rich man may buy a yacht or build an orphanage. A consistent altruist should applaud money-making, since income measures service to others, and condemn the coal miner who becomes a grocery clerk. W. H. Hutt came close to saying so. As exchangeable goods grow abundant, nonexchangeable ones grow more valued.
  • Back to the jungle? The “survival of the fittest” asks: fittest for what? In the jungle the fit are the strong; on the market, those best at serving others. “It is precisely statism that is bringing back the rule of the jungle.” A promised refutation of libertarianism titled “Back to the Jungle” never appeared.
  • “Economic power” is the right to refuse an exchange. If B pulls a gun to make A trade, either B is an aggressor or refusing to trade is coercion. “There is no middle ground!” The man denied a job at Ford is claiming Ford’s property, not being robbed. Power over nature (the axe) must be kept distinct from power over man (the gun). Albert Jay Nock’s “social power” and “State power” are the same distinction, and history is a race between them.
  • Luck can’t be isolated, except in pure gambling, and no one proposes to compensate roulette losers. The traffic-manager analogy fails, because every owner regulates his own road. “Too complex” for rich countries and “too backward” for poor ones: “the appointed moment for laissez faire somehow never arrives.”
  • Human rights are property rights. Free speech: “Where?” On one’s own property or someone else’s by agreement. Street demonstrations are an insoluble conflict only because government owns the streets. Justice Holmes’s man who falsely cries “fire” in a crowded theatre violates property rights: if he is the owner, he defrauds his patrons; if a patron, he breaks the terms of his admission. No right needs to be “limited.”
  • Appendix: Henry M. Oliver. Kenneth Arrow praised Oliver’s A Critique of Socioeconomic Goals (1954) as a step toward axiomatizing social ethics. Rothbard calls it “a tissue of logical fallacies.” Infringement should be defined as “direct physical interference with another man’s person or property, or the threat of such physical interference,” which covers fraud as implicit theft. A contract between A and B to shoot C is not itself a crime; the shooting is. When Oliver says complete legal liberty is possible “only under a system of anarchy,” Rothbard replies: “If this be anarchism, then many libertarians will embrace the term!”

Power and Market · Chapter 7

Conclusion: Economics and Public Policy

What a value-free science can still say about politics.

  • Economic laws take the form “if A, then B, then C.” The historian and the forecaster must judge when they apply, and they are qualitative: quantitative prediction goes beyond economics. To the model-builder the acid test is: “If you can forecast so well, why are you not doing so on the stock market?” Operations research and linear programming are management technology, not economics.
  • On a purely free market the economist is only an educator. Intervention makes him useful, because the question is then what a government act will do, and that takes long chains of reasoning.
  • Welfare economics moralizes in secret, through interpersonal utility comparisons, the “compensation principle,” the “social welfare function,” and the claim that income taxes are better than excises. Borrowing the “community’s” values doesn’t make a judgment neutral. An economist who advises a client is committed to the client’s ends.
  • Rothbard rejects the utilitarian shortcut of assuming everyone shares the same ultimate ends. An OPA director told that price controls cause shortages may resign, may value power or plunder more, or may like shortages. Praxeology can’t “say him nay.”
  • Cumulative intervention. In 1929 the United States began supporting farm prices. Surpluses followed. Then came production quotas on a past base period, and then the “soil bank,” paying farmers to idle land. They idled their worst land, farmed the rest harder, and received “government checks for not producing anything.” The consumer paid four times: in taxes, in higher prices, in wasted surpluses and in forgone output. Each intervention creates problems that call for repeal or for another intervention, so every mixed economy is unstable.
  • The free economy is praxeologically stable, but psychologically its survival is in doubt. Proudhon called liberty “the Mother, not the Daughter, of Order.” Mankind must choose between “the society of contract” and “the society of status,” and at that point “the praxeologist as such retires from the scene; the citizen—the ethicist—must now choose.”

Context

Where Rothbard parts from Mises

The book set out to explain Human Action and ended up amending it. The agreements run deeper than the disagreements, but the disagreements are what made Rothbard’s name. In a letter of February 1954 he already expected the book to “command the attention of the profession” partly for “its differences from Mises in such areas as monopoly, banking ethics, and government.”

Checked against events

What held up

A book of deductions makes few forecasts, but the two books did say datable things about their own time. Here is how those claims look from 2026.

Partly

Keynesianism is “happily beginning to wane” MES ch. 11, 1962

Wrong on timing. Keynesian demand management peaked after the book appeared, and in January 1971 Nixon declared himself “a Keynesian in economics.” Right on the direction: the stagflation of the 1970s broke the Phillips-curve trade-off, and monetarism and rational expectations dominated academic macroeconomics by 1980. Keynesian policy came back in new forms, in New Keynesian models and in the stimulus packages of 2008–09 and 2020.

Held

Unemployment equilibrium needs rigid wages, and inflation works only through “money illusion” MES ch. 11, 1962

Rothbard said unions would learn to see through inflation. Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps made the same point in 1967–68 with the expectations-augmented Phillips curve. In 1974–75 American inflation passed 12 per cent while unemployment reached 9 per cent, which the simple Keynesian trade-off said couldn’t happen together.

Held

Socialism can’t calculate; Soviet planning is overrated MES ch. 9, 12; P&M ch. 5

The Soviet bloc collapsed in 1989–91, and China’s growth began only after market reforms from 1978. Later work showed official Soviet growth statistics were heavily overstated, as Rothbard and the sources he cites suspected. The claim that Soviet planning depended on world prices and black markets is now standard.

Partly

Eastern Europe is moving toward the market, Yugoslavia furthest P&M ch. 5, 1970

Yugoslavia’s “market socialism” ended in debt crisis, hyperinflation in 1989 and the break-up of the country in 1991. But after 1989 the whole region did turn to markets and private ownership, more thoroughly than anyone in 1970 expected.

Partly

The gold link will give way to fiat money MES ch. 12, 1962

In 1962 the dollar was on a “restricted international gold bullion standard,” convertible only for foreign central banks, and Rothbard called world fiat money the inflationists’ ideal. On August 15, 1971 Nixon closed the gold window, and major currencies have floated since 1973. There is no world central bank, although the IMF created its own reserve asset, the SDR, in 1969.

Open

Fiat money ends in runaway inflation MES ch. 12

No major currency has had a hyperinflation. American inflation peaked at 14.8 per cent in 1980 and 9.1 per cent in 2022. But the hyperinflations in Zimbabwe (2007–09) and Venezuela (from 2016) followed his script closely: the public stopped holding the currency and switched to dollars and barter.

Held

Deposit insurance removes the check that runs place on banks MES ch. 12, 1962

The moral-hazard argument is now textbook banking economics. The American savings-and-loan crisis saw more than 1,000 thrifts fail between 1986 and 1995, at a cost to taxpayers of about $124 billion. The 100 per cent reserve cure was never adopted.

Held

Price ceilings produce shortages and queues MES ch. 12; P&M ch. 3

Nixon’s wage and price controls of August 1971, and the oil-price ceilings that followed, were followed by the gasoline lines of 1973–74 and 1979. The lines ended after oil prices were decontrolled in 1981. Rent control’s effect on housing supply is the other standard case.

Held

Social security is financed twice, from taxes and again from future taxes or borrowing MES ch. 12; P&M ch. 5

The trust funds hold special Treasury securities; the payroll taxes were spent. Redeeming the securities requires general taxes or new borrowing, exactly as described. The trustees’ 2025 report projects the combined funds will be exhausted in 2034.

Partly

Poor countries need saved capital and free markets, not prestige projects and aid MES ch. 8, 12

Hong Kong, which the book cites, and later Singapore, Taiwan and South Korea grew through trade and investment, and many aid-financed steel mills and dams disappointed, as P. T. Bauer argued. But Korea and Taiwan also used heavy industrial policy, which the book would condemn.

Open

Defence and courts could be sold on the market P&M ch. 1

No society has tried it. But private arbitration has grown into a large industry, and in the United States private security guards outnumber public police officers.

Missed so far

Every mixed economy is unstable and must drift to one pole P&M ch. 5, 7

The mixed economies of the West have lasted more than half a century since 1970, with governments spending roughly a third to a half of national income and neither collapsing into socialism nor returning to laissez faire.

Missed so far

Mathematical economics is dying Salerno’s introduction, 2009

Salerno wrote that the failure to foresee the 2008 crisis showed Rothbard “has been proven correct” and that the “death knell is now tolling” for mathematical modelling. Mathematical macroeconomics was widely criticized but not abandoned, and the Austrian school remains a small minority in the profession.

Context

The arguments it started

Can economics be known a priori?

Rothbard’s method answered Milton Friedman’s “The Methodology of Positive Economics” (1953), which judged theories by their predictions. He replied to Fritz Machlup’s defence of testing (1955) with “In Defense of ‘Extreme Apriorism’” (1957). Most economists still side with testing.

Bryan Caplan’s “Why I Am Not an Austrian Economist” (1997) became the best-known modern critique. The Austrian reply remains that history never offers controlled repeats.

Monopoly price

Rothbard’s break with Mises divided Austrians. Israel Kirzner’s Competition and Entrepreneurship (1973) accepted that a single owner of a unique resource can earn a true monopoly price even on a free market. Rothbard’s followers hold that no such price can be identified.

Indifference and demonstrated preference

Caplan’s “The Austrian Search for Realistic Foundations” (1999) argued that indifference can be revealed by random choice between options, and that ruling it out cripples welfare economics. Walter Block and Guido Hülsmann (both 1999) replied in defence of Rothbard’s position.

Fractional-reserve banking

Lawrence White’s Free Banking in Britain (1984) and George Selgin’s The Theory of Free Banking (1988) argue that competitive fractional-reserve banks are stable and serve a changing demand for money. Jesús Huerta de Soto’s Money, Bank Credit, and Economic Cycles (1998) and Joseph Salerno defend Rothbard’s 100 per cent reserves.

The business cycle

Gordon Tullock’s “Why the Austrians Are Wrong About Depressions” (1988) asked why businessmen fall for cheap credit again and again, and why the theory can’t explain how long depressions last. Milton Friedman’s “plucking model” (1964, revisited 1993) found that the size of a boom does not predict the size of the next bust, against the Austrian story. Roger Garrison’s Time and Money (2001) is the leading restatement in its defence.

Anarcho-capitalism

Robert Nozick’s Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974) argued that competing protection agencies would converge on a dominant agency, in effect a minimal state. Rothbard replied in “Robert Nozick and the Immaculate Conception of the State” (1977).

Tyler Cowen’s “Law as a Public Good” (1992) argued that agencies able to cooperate on a common code could also form a cartel that works as a state. David Friedman’s The Machinery of Freedom (1973) defends the same system on economic rather than rights grounds.

Collective goods

Samuelson’s 1954 definition made defence and lighthouses the textbook public goods. Ronald Coase’s “The Lighthouse in Economics” (1974) found that many British lighthouses had been built and run privately, paid for by fees collected at ports, which supports Rothbard’s scepticism. Most economists still treat national defence as the standard public good.

Tax incidence

Rothbard’s law that no tax can be shifted forward is rejected by mainstream public finance, which makes incidence depend on elasticities (Arnold Harberger’s 1962 model of the corporation tax is the classic). Empirical studies such as Timothy Besley and Harvey Rosen’s (1999) find that American sales taxes are passed on to consumer prices, sometimes more than fully.

Minimum wages

Chapter 10’s deduction that a binding minimum wage causes unemployment was challenged by David Card and Alan Krueger’s New Jersey study (1994), answered by David Neumark and William Wascher (2000), and argued over ever since. Austrians say the effect shows up in hours, training and jobs never created, which such studies can miss.

Patents

Fritz Machlup’s report for the US Senate (1958) concluded that no economist could confidently recommend either introducing a patent system or abolishing an existing one. Michele Boldrin and David Levine’s Against Intellectual Monopoly (2008) revived the abolitionist case on empirical grounds, reaching Rothbard’s conclusion by a different route.

Terms of art

Glossary

Praxeology
The science of human action, deduced from the fact that people act. Economics, or catallactics (the study of exchange), is its most developed branch.
Evenly rotating economy (ERE)
An imaginary economy where every period repeats and nothing is uncertain. It isolates interest from profit and loss.
Time preference
Preferring a satisfaction sooner rather than later, other things equal. It is always positive and it alone sets the pure rate of interest.
Pure rate of interest
Mises’s “originary interest”: the discount of future against present goods that remains in the ERE. Market rates add entrepreneurial, purchasing-power and terms-of-trade components.
Time market
All exchanges of present goods for future goods, above all capitalists buying factor services. The loan market is a small, derivative part of it.
Structure of production
The stages through which goods pass from raw resources to the consumer. Saving lengthens it; consuming capital shortens it.
Original factors
Labour and land. Capital goods are produced factors, “way stations” that resolve into land, labour and time.
Land
In Rothbard’s revision, nature-given resources plus permanent improvements that are never replaced (canals, cleared forests). Capital goods are things that wear out and must be reproduced.
Specific / nonspecific factor
A factor useful in one line only, or in many. Changes in product values hit specific factors hardest.
DMVP
Discounted marginal value product: what a factor adds to the value of the product, discounted for the time until the product sells. Factors tend to earn it.
Rent
In Fetter’s sense, the price of any good’s services per period. Wages are the rent of labour.
Capitalization
Valuing a durable good at the discounted sum of its expected future rents.
Capitalist-entrepreneur
The owner who advances money to factors and bears the uncertainty of the product’s future price.
Decision-making rent
The return to the owner’s ultimate decisions (choosing managers, deciding how much to invest). It survives in the ERE, unlike profit.
Demonstrated preference
The principle that preferences are known only through actual choices, so utility can’t be measured or compared between people.
Total demand to hold
Buyers’ demand plus owners’ reservation demand. The market price equates it with the existing stock.
Regression theorem
Mises’s explanation of money’s value, traced back day by day to the money commodity’s value as an ordinary good under barter.
PPM
Purchasing power of the monetary unit: the whole array of goods-prices, not a measurable “price level.”
Pseudo warehouse receipt
A claim to money issued beyond the money held. Rothbard’s term for fractional-reserve notes and deposits.
Inflation
In the book’s definition, issuing money beyond any increase in the stock of specie, not the rise in prices that follows.
Malinvestment
Investment in longer processes than available savings can complete, induced by credit expansion. The crisis reveals it.
Individual sovereignty
Rothbard’s replacement for “consumers’ sovereignty”: each person rules his own person and property.
Monopoly
In the only usable sense, an exclusive privilege granted by the state (Coke’s definition). The monopoly price such a grant allows can be compared with a free-market price.
Restrictionist price
A wage raised by keeping other workers out, as by unions, licences or immigration laws, rather than by the seller withholding his own supply.
Autistic / binary / triangular
Force applied to one person; a forced exchange with the intervener; force applied to an exchange between two others.
Economic vs political means
Oppenheimer’s terms for getting wealth by producing and trading versus by taking it.
Contractual vs hegemonic
Social relations based on voluntary agreement versus those based on command and obedience.
Tax-consumers
Calhoun’s name for those who receive more from government than they pay: officials first, then subsidized groups.
Island of calculational chaos
Any part of the economy, such as a state enterprise or an integrated firm with no outside market, where prices can’t be calculated.
Private Product Remaining
Rothbard’s measure of output after deducting government’s absorption of resources.
First-user, first-owner
The homesteading rule: unowned land becomes the property of whoever first brings it into use.

Using it

A reading path through 1,400 pages

If you won’t read it cover to cover, this order gets the core argument in about a third of the length.

  1. MES ch. 1–2

    Method, value, capital and the logic of exchange. Everything else depends on these two chapters.

  2. MES ch. 5–6

    The structure of production and the pure time-preference theory of interest, the book’s most original economics.

  3. MES ch. 9 §3–5, ch. 10

    The limit on firm size and the case that “monopoly price” is an illusion, the most contested chapter.

  4. MES ch. 11 §6 and ch. 12 §11

    Money warehouses and the business cycle.

  5. P&M ch. 1–2

    Private defence and the typology of intervention.

  6. P&M ch. 4 §3, §7 and ch. 6

    Tax incidence, the canons of tax justice and the praxeological critique of antimarket ethics.

Read alongside

  • Ludwig von Mises, Human Action (1949): the book this one set out to explain.
  • Rothbard, “Toward a Reconstruction of Utility and Welfare Economics” (1956): the welfare theory behind P&M ch. 2.
  • Rothbard, What Has Government Done to Our Money? (1963): the monetary argument in 60 pages.
  • Rothbard, America’s Great Depression (1963): the cycle theory applied to 1929–33, with the PPR method.
  • Rothbard, For a New Liberty (1973) and The Ethics of Liberty (1982): the political programme and the natural-rights case this book deliberately leaves out.
  • Joseph Salerno’s and Joseph Stromberg’s introductions to the Mises Institute editions (2009, 2004): the book’s history.
  • Israel Kirzner, Competition and Entrepreneurship (1973): the other Austrian road, through entrepreneurial discovery.
  • Roger Garrison, Time and Money (2001): capital-based macroeconomics in diagrams.
  • Critics: Robert Nozick, Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974); Bryan Caplan, “Why I Am Not an Austrian Economist” (1997); Gordon Tullock, “Why the Austrians Are Wrong About Depressions” (1988).
