Man acts.

The action axiom. Nearly 1,400 pages of economic theory are deduced from this one sentence.

Man, Economy, and State with Power and Market

Murray N. Rothbard · 1926–1995

The whole book in one breath

Rothbard starts from one proposition he takes to be undeniable: people act, using scarce means to pursue ends. From that, plus two modest assumptions, he deduces all of economic theory step by step: value, exchange, money, prices, interest, capital, profit and loss. The free market that results is a web of voluntary trades, each expected to benefit both sides. Interest reflects the universal preference for sooner over later, and profit and loss steer entrepreneurs toward what consumers want. Power and Market then brings in force. Price controls, tariffs, licences, taxes, subsidies and inflation each leave at least one person worse off, destroy information the market would have produced, and invite the next intervention. Economics can’t choose your values, he concludes, but it can show that the market produces harmony and coercion produces conflict.

Origins

A textbook that became a treatise

In the early 1950s the William Volker Fund asked Murray Rothbard, a young regular at Ludwig von Mises’s seminar at New York University, to write a college-level version of Mises’s Human Action. The project outgrew its brief. What Rothbard delivered was a full treatise that rebuilds Austrian economics from the ground up, fills gaps Mises had left, and in several places breaks with him.

The publisher cut the long final part on government, so Power and Market appeared separately eight years later. Read together, the two books form one argument. Part I describes the market as it would work if no one used force. Part II adds force and traces its effects.

  1. 1949

    Mises publishes Human Action, the systematic treatise of Austrian economics.

  2. Early 1950s

    The Volker Fund commissions Rothbard’s textbook version. He works on it for most of the decade.

  3. 1956

    “Toward a Reconstruction of Utility and Welfare Economics” sets out the demonstrated-preference welfare theory the treatise relies on.

  4. 1962

    Man, Economy, and State is published by D. Van Nostrand in two volumes. The intervention material is cut down to a single chapter.

  5. 1963

    America’s Great Depression applies the book’s business-cycle theory to 1929.

  6. 1970

    The cut material appears as Power and Market: Government and the Economy.

  7. 2004

    The Mises Institute’s Scholar’s Edition puts both books back together in one volume.

Mises reviewed it as “an epochal contribution” to the science of human action. Alongside Human Action, it remains the most complete systematic statement of Austrian economics.

Method

Economics as logic, not lab work

Rothbard follows Mises in treating economics as a branch of praxeology, the general science of human action. Its laws aren’t hypotheses to be tested against data. They are deduced from premises known to be true, so the conclusions are true as long as the reasoning holds. The whole structure rests on one axiom and two small empirical postulates.

Axiom

Humans act.

People pursue ends with scarce means, through time, under uncertainty. Rothbard holds that this is self-evident: anyone can see it by reflecting on their own conduct, and arguing against it is itself an action.

Postulate

Variety.

People and natural resources differ. That is why the division of labour and trade exist.

Postulate

Leisure is a good.

People value free time, so work always has a cost: the leisure given up.

  • Verbal logic over mathematics. Equations describe simultaneous, functional relations; action is a causal sequence through time. Calculus assumes smooth, infinitely small changes, while real choices are discrete. Graphs can illustrate a point, but the mathematics does not explain it.
  • History illustrates; it can’t test. Every historical event has many causes and never repeats under controlled conditions, so statistics can apply a theory but never confirm or refute it.
  • Imaginary constructs isolate one factor at a time. The Crusoe economy shows action with no exchange. The evenly rotating economy removes uncertainty, so interest can be studied without profit and loss mixed in.
  • Value-free. Praxeology says what follows from an action, not whether it is good. Rothbard holds to this in both books. Even the critique of intervention is framed as “this policy produces these effects,” not as a moral verdict.

If you remember nothing else

Twelve ideas that carry the book

MES ch. 1

Everything starts from action

Economics is deduced from the fact that people act on purpose. Its laws are logical, not statistical, and hold in every time and place.

MES ch. 1, 4

Value is ranked, never measured

Utility is ordinal. People rank alternatives; they don’t add up units of satisfaction. The only preferences economics can see are the ones people show by choosing.

MES ch. 2

Free exchange benefits both sides

A trade happens only when each side values what it gets more than what it gives, so every voluntary exchange raises both parties’ expected well-being.

MES ch. 3, 4

Money is a market product

The most marketable commodity becomes the medium of exchange. States adopt money; they don’t invent it. The regression theorem traces money’s value back to its value as a commodity.

MES ch. 6

Interest is the price of time

Everyone prefers a good now to the same good later. That time preference, not the productivity of capital, sets the rate of interest, and interest shows up in every price spread, not just in loans.

MES ch. 5, 8

Production is a structure in time

Capital goods are waystations on the road to consumer goods. Saving lengthens the structure and raises output and real wages. Consuming capital shortens it.

MES ch. 8

Profit and loss steer the economy

Entrepreneurs bet on future prices. Right bets earn profit and pull resources toward what consumers want; wrong ones earn losses and push resources away.

MES ch. 10

“Monopoly price” is an illusion

On a free market there is no “competitive price” to measure a monopoly price against. The only real monopoly is an exclusive privilege granted by the state.

MES ch. 10

Firms can’t outgrow calculation

A firm that owned every stage of a product would have no outside prices to calculate with. That caps the size of firms, and it is the same reason socialism fails.

MES ch. 11, 12

Credit expansion causes boom and bust

New bank credit pushes interest below the rate time preference would set. Investment flows into projects that real savings can’t finish, and the bust is the correction.

P&M ch. 2–4

Force comes in three shapes

Autistic, binary and triangular intervention each make at least one person worse off, so no intervention can be shown to raise social welfare. No tax is neutral.

P&M ch. 1

Even defence can be a market service

Police, courts and arbitration are services like any other, and competing private agencies could supply them. The state’s monopoly is not an economic necessity.

Part I

Man, Economy, and State

1962, twelve chapters. The market economy is built up in order: from one person acting alone, to exchange, to money, to a full economy with capital, interest, entrepreneurs and a theory of money. Each chapter rests on the ones before it.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 1

Fundamentals of Human Action

What it means to act, and the laws that follow before a second person even appears.

  • Action is purposeful behaviour: an actor uses means they believe will reach ends they value. Means are scarce by definition. Anything that served every end without limit would not need to be economised.
  • Every action takes time and faces an uncertain future. People rank their ends on an ordinal value scale: they compare, and never measure.
  • Time preference: other things equal, a satisfaction now is preferred to the same satisfaction later. This is a universal feature of action, not a quirk of psychology.
  • The law of marginal utility follows from ranking. Each additional unit of a good goes to a less-valued use, so the value of any one unit equals the value of the least important end the supply serves.
  • Goods are ordered by distance from consumption: consumer goods first, then producers’ goods of successively higher orders. The original factors are labour and nature-given land; capital goods are produced factors.
  • The law of returns: factors have an optimum proportion, and beyond it, adding more of one factor yields less and less.
  • Robinson Crusoe shows capital formation in miniature. To make a tool he must first save, giving up consumption while he builds it. Accumulating capital, not technology alone, is what raises productivity.
  • Cost is the value of the best alternative given up. Every action trades a less satisfactory state for one expected to be better, which yields psychic profit or, if the actor guessed wrong, psychic loss.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 2

Direct Exchange

When two people meet they can trade or they can fight. Price theory is built out of the first choice.

  • Interpersonal action takes two forms, voluntary exchange and violence. A society of free exchange is contractual; one run by command is hegemonic. This distinction frames the whole book.
  • Exchange happens only when the parties value the goods in reverse order, each preferring what they receive to what they give up. Both expect to gain.
  • The division of labour rests on human and natural variety. Ricardo’s law of association (comparative advantage) shows that even the less productive partner gains by specialising.
  • In an isolated barter, the price falls somewhere in a zone set by the two parties’ valuations. As buyers and sellers compete, it narrows to the range set by the marginal pairs, the last buyer and seller who can just make a trade.
  • Supply and demand schedules come straight from individual value scales. Because a stock exists at any moment, “supply” is really the stock minus sellers’ own reservation demand. The market price is the one at which the total demand to hold the good equals the total stock.
  • Speculation speeds prices toward market-clearing levels. Speculators who guess wrong bear the losses.
  • The market presupposes property: ownership of oneself, title to unowned resources by first use (homesteading), and defence of both against invasion.
  • Barter needs a double coincidence of wants, and that limit is what drives the emergence of money.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 3

The Pattern of Indirect Exchange

How money emerges, and what a monetary economy looks like from the inside.

  • Following Carl Menger, people trade for goods that are easier to re-trade. The process snowballs until one or two commodities, historically gold and silver, become money. Money arises on the market, not by decree.
  • Once there is money, every good has a money price, and incomes and outlays can be added up in one unit. That is what makes economic calculation, the reckoning of profit and loss, possible.
  • Each person divides their money among consumption, investment and cash held, and ranks those uses by marginal utility like anything else.
  • Production takes time, so capitalists pay the owners of labour and land before the product is sold. Money flows from consumers back through the stages of production as incomes.
  • The chapter introduces the roles the rest of the book analyses: consumer, worker, landowner, capitalist and entrepreneur.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 4

Prices and Consumption

How consumer prices form in money, and why the value of money itself isn’t a circular argument.

  • Buyers and sellers rank units of money and units of goods on the same value scale. Those rankings produce money demand and supply schedules, and the market price clears them.
  • The Austrian circle. The demand for money depends on its purchasing power, and its purchasing power depends on demand. Mises’s regression theorem breaks the loop: today’s demand rests on yesterday’s purchasing power, back to the day the money commodity was valued only for its direct uses.
  • Durable goods have two prices: a rent for their services per period, and a price for the good itself, which tends to equal the discounted sum of its expected future rents. This is capitalization.
  • Indifference curves are rejected. Indifference can never show up in action, because to act is to choose. Economics has to rest on demonstrated preference, and it cannot compare utility between people.
  • Concepts that need measurable utility, such as consumer surplus, are rejected for the same reason.
  • Correct speculation smooths prices over time; mistaken speculation is punished by losses.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 5

Production: The Structure

Production is a sequence of stages, and the capitalist’s job is to pay the factors now and wait to be paid later.

  • The evenly rotating economy (ERE) is a construct in which the same things happen every period and nothing is uncertain. With profit and loss gone, what remains is wages, rents and pure interest.
  • Workers and landowners want to be paid now, but the product is ready only later. Capitalists buy their services with present money and sell the product in the future. They supply present goods and earn the time discount, which is interest.
  • Money flows down from consumers through the stages: retailers buy from wholesalers, wholesalers from manufacturers, and so on back to raw materials. Each stage buys from the one above it and pays its own labour, land and interest.
  • In the ERE every stage earns the same rate of interest. If one firm owns several stages, nothing changes: it simply earns the interest implicitly.
  • Businesses spend far more buying intermediate goods from each other than consumers spend on final goods. National-income statistics net that spending out, which hides most economic activity and the savings that sustain it.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 6

Production: The Rate of Interest and Its Determination

The analytical heart of the book: interest comes from time preference and nothing else.

  • Interest is not mainly about loans. It is the spread between what a capitalist pays for factors and what the product later sells for: the price of present goods in terms of future goods. The loan market is a small part of this wider time market.
  • On the time market, savers supply present goods in exchange for future ones. Workers and landowners, along with consumer borrowers, supply future goods in exchange for present money. The rate that clears this market is set by everyone’s time preferences.
  • The pure time-preference theory, after Frank Fetter and Mises. The productivity of capital explains why roundabout methods are used, not why interest exists, because that productivity is already priced into capital goods. Böhm-Bawerk’s “technical superiority of present goods” is dropped as a separate cause.
  • Lower time preference means more saving, a longer structure of production, higher productivity and higher real wages. Higher time preference reverses each step.
  • Arbitrage pulls the loan rate toward this natural rate and pushes interest toward one uniform rate across stages and industries.
  • Rival theories are critiqued along the way, including productivity and “abstinence” explanations and the treatment of capital as a permanent, self-renewing fund.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 7

Production: General Pricing of the Factors

Why each factor earns what it does: consumer demand, imputed back through the stages and discounted for time.

  • Factor prices derive from the expected prices of what the factors help produce. In the ERE each factor tends to earn its discounted marginal value product (DMVP): its marginal physical product, times the product’s price, discounted by the interest rate for the time until the product sells.
  • Rothbard widens “rent” to mean the price of any good’s services per period, not just land’s. Durable factors such as land and capital goods have capital values equal to their discounted expected rents.
  • Labour can’t be capitalised in a free society, because people own themselves. Only slavery puts a capital value on a person.
  • Capital goods earn no independent net return in the long run. Their gross rents are imputed back to the land, labour and time that produced them, so in the ERE all income resolves into wages, ground rents and interest.
  • The imputed shares add up exactly to the value of the product, so the “adding-up problem” that troubles mathematical marginal-productivity theory never arises.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 8

Production: Entrepreneurship and Change

Leave the evenly rotating economy, let the future be uncertain, and the entrepreneur appears.

  • Entrepreneurs buy factors at today’s prices as a bet on tomorrow’s product prices. Accurate forecasts earn profits and mistaken ones earn losses.
  • Rothbard’s central figure is the capitalist-entrepreneur, the person who puts up the money and bears the uncertainty.
  • Profits and losses are the signals that move resources to where consumers want them most. Competition erodes profits over time and pulls the economy toward the equilibrium that current conditions imply.
  • Total profits and losses track net saving. In a growing economy with positive net saving, total profits exceed total losses; in a stationary one they cancel out; in a declining one losses win.
  • Technology is rarely the binding constraint. Better methods sit unused until savings provide the capital to adopt them. Capital accumulation lengthens the structure of production and raises real wages.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 9

Production: Particular Factor Prices and Productive Incomes

The general theory applied to particular factors: land, natural resources, labour, location, and to cost.

  • Land prices follow the general capitalization rule. Owners of exhaustible resources use or conserve them according to their expectations of future prices, so private ownership handles conservation.
  • Wage rates tend toward each worker’s DMVP. Because most labour is non-specific, workers can move across many uses. Lasting involuntary unemployment can only come from wage rates held above the level that clears the market.
  • Idle resources are not waste in themselves. A factor sits unused either because it is submarginal or because its owner is holding out for a better price.
  • Cost is subjective: it is the value of the best alternative forgone. Costs don’t determine prices. Expected prices determine what producers will pay for factors, and so determine costs.
  • The price of the same good tends to equalise across places, apart from transport costs, and that governs where industries locate.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 10

Monopoly and Competition

The most provocative chapter: on a free market, “monopoly price” has no meaning.

  • “Consumers’ sovereignty” is the wrong frame, because producers are free to refuse, or to choose leisure or pleasant work over higher pay. Rothbard substitutes individual sovereignty: each person decides over their own property.
  • Cartels are voluntary joint ventures. They are unstable and tend to break up, and a cartel that restricts output can’t be shown to harm anyone by any standard outside the market itself.
  • One big cartel is impossible. A firm that owned every stage of production for a good would have no external market prices for what passes between its divisions, so it couldn’t calculate profit or loss. That limits the size of firms, and it is Mises’s case against socialism applied to business.
  • Monopoly price is an empty concept on the free market. Identifying one requires a “competitive price” to compare it with, and there is no such price apart from the market price itself. The meaningful sense of monopoly is the older one: an exclusive privilege granted by government.
  • “Perfect competition” is an unreal model, not an ideal. Monopolistic-competition theory (Chamberlin, Robinson) is wrong to treat product differentiation and “excess capacity” as waste, since both reflect what consumers actually want.
  • Predatory price-cutting is costly and self-defeating: the predator loses more than its rivals, and new entrants wait for prices to rise.
  • Unions can raise some wages only by restricting who may work, which pushes other workers into lower-paid jobs.
  • Patents vs copyright: a copyright can arise from contract, for example selling a book on condition that it isn’t copied. A patent can’t, because it bars independent inventors who never agreed to anything. Patents are state monopoly grants.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 11

Money and Its Purchasing Power

No single “price level”, no social gain from more money, and a gold standard as the market’s own outcome.

  • The purchasing power of money is set by the supply of and demand for money. It is an array of exchange ratios against every good, not one “price level” an index could capture.
  • The equation of exchange (MV = PT) and “velocity” treat averages as causes. Rothbard rejects the mechanical quantity theory but keeps its core truth: more money means lower purchasing power than there would otherwise be.
  • Any quantity of money is optimal. More money adds nothing to the services money provides. It only dilutes purchasing power and shifts wealth to whoever receives the new money first, before prices adjust.
  • “Hoarding” is not a problem. All money is always held by someone, and a rise in the demand for money simply raises its purchasing power. Falling prices are not harmful in themselves.
  • A receipt fully backed by gold is a claim on money, not new money. Issuing claims beyond the gold held, which is what fractional-reserve banking does, creates fiduciary media. Rothbard treats this as a breach of the warehouse contract and argues for 100% reserves.
  • Coinage can be a private business. Gresham’s law operates only when law fixes the exchange ratio between two moneys.
  • Exchange rates between currencies tend toward purchasing-power parity.
  • A long critique of Keynes covers the consumption function, the multiplier, the saving–investment apparatus, liquidity preference and “underemployment equilibrium.” Rothbard traces the last to money wages held rigid, not to any failure of the market.

Man, Economy, and State · Chapter 12

The Economics of Violent Intervention in the Market

The 1962 bridge to Power and Market, and the home of the book’s business-cycle theory.

  • This is a compressed survey of intervention that Power and Market later expands in full, following the same classification and arguments.
  • Inflation, meaning the creation of new money by government or banks, is treated as an intervention: the issuer acquires real resources without producing anything, at the expense of those who receive the money later.
  • Credit expansion produces the Austrian business cycle of Mises and Hayek, traced step by step below.
  • The policy corollary: in a depression, government should not prop up wages or prices, rescue failing firms or inflate again. Letting the liquidation run is the fastest route to recovery. Rothbard applies this to 1929–33 in America’s Great Depression.

Part II

Power and Market

1970, seven chapters. Now force enters. Rothbard borrows Franz Oppenheimer’s distinction between the economic means of getting wealth (producing and trading) and the political means (taking it), and traces what the political means does to the market Part I described.

Power and Market · Chapter 1

Defense Services on the Free Market

Before analysing what the state does, Rothbard asks whether it is needed at all, and answers no.

  • Defending people and property through police, courts and arbitration is a service like any other. Competing private agencies, paid voluntarily much like insurance, could supply it.
  • Disputes between agencies would go to arbitrators or appeal courts that both accept. The market would settle on a common legal code grounded in self-ownership and property rights.
  • The classical-liberal ideal of limited government is unstable, he argues. A body with a monopoly of force, funded by compulsory taxes, has no market check to keep it limited.
  • The state is identified by how it gets its revenue: taxation, taken by coercion, together with a claimed monopoly of force over a territory. The analysis of intervention that follows applies to whoever uses force, state or private criminal.

Power and Market · Chapter 2

Fundamentals of Intervention

A taxonomy of force, and a proof that no intervention can be shown to raise social welfare.

  • Intervention is the intrusion of force into free exchange. It comes in three shapes (see the figure below).
  • In a free exchange both parties expect to gain. Under intervention the intervener gains and at least one subject loses, because being coerced shows the subject would have chosen differently.
  • Economics can’t compare utility between people, so the only welfare judgement it can make is the unanimity one. The free market raises every participant’s expected utility; no intervention can be shown to raise social utility.
  • After the fact, the market corrects its own errors because mistaken entrepreneurs take losses. Government has no comparable test.
  • Interventions distort prices and production, and the problems they create invite further intervention.

Power and Market · Chapter 3

Triangular Intervention

Controls on prices and products, and the long list of privileges that restrict who may sell.

  • Maximum prices set below the market rate create shortages, queues, rationing, black markets and falling quality. Minimum prices set above it create unsold surpluses, and a minimum wage produces unemployment among the least productive workers.
  • Controls spread. A shortage in one market pushes demand, and then controls, into neighbouring ones.
  • Bribery under controls is analysed without moralising, as the market partly reasserting itself against the law.
  • Banning a product cuts consumers off from what they want and creates a black market.
  • Grants of monopolistic privilege each restrict entry to benefit one group at others’ expense: licensing, tariffs and quotas, immigration restrictions, child-labour laws, compulsory cartels, conservation laws, patents, franchises, quality and safety standards, and antitrust law. Antitrust penalises efficiency under a concept of monopoly that does not survive Chapter 10.
  • In every case, consumers and excluded producers pay so the privileged group can gain.

Power and Market · Chapter 4

Binary Intervention: Taxation

Who really pays, why no tax can be neutral, and how taxation divides society into two classes.

  • No tax is neutral. Every tax, even a uniform head tax, changes production away from what the market would have produced.
  • A general sales tax can’t simply be passed on to consumers, because prices are set by demand and the money stock. It falls on the incomes of the owners of labour and land, so in effect it is an income tax.
  • Income taxes penalise work relative to leisure. By taxing the returns to saving they also raise effective time preference, which means less saving and less capital. Progressive rates hit the most productive hardest.
  • Taxes on accumulation, such as property, inheritance and capital-gains taxes, eat directly into the capital stock.
  • Henry George’s single tax on land is rejected. Ground rent is not an “unearned” return, and taxing all of it would wipe out land’s capital value and the price signals that allocate it efficiently.
  • The standard “canons of justice” in taxation all fail. “Ability to pay” and “equal sacrifice” require utility that can be measured and compared between people. The benefit principle can’t apply to compulsory payments, and true neutrality is impossible.
  • Taxation splits society into net taxpayers and net tax-consumers: government employees and subsidised groups. Rothbard takes the idea from John C. Calhoun.

Power and Market · Chapter 5

Binary Intervention: Government Expenditures

Government spending doesn’t add to output. It redirects resources, and it can’t calculate.

  • Government spending diverts resources from the uses consumers would have chosen. National accounts that add it to output count a cost as if it were a product. Rothbard proposes Private Product Remaining: output net of what government absorbs.
  • Subsidies and transfer payments penalise the efficient and reward the inefficient. Subsidising a condition such as unemployment produces more of it.
  • Government enterprises can’t calculate. Their revenue is compulsory and taxes can cover their losses, so they face no profit-and-loss test. Free or underpriced services lead to overuse, shortages and rationing by politics.
  • Public ownership turns allocation into political conflict: who gets to use the road, the park or the school.
  • Complete government ownership, socialism, faces Mises’s calculation problem. With no market for capital goods there are no prices, and so no way to allocate rationally.

Power and Market · Chapter 6

Antimarket Ethics: A Praxeological Critique

The ethical case against the market, tested for consistency and economic error rather than answered with a rival ethic.

  • Immoral choices. If people choose badly, coercion can’t make them moral, since morality requires free choice. And the coercers are no less fallible than anyone else.
  • Human nature. The market doesn’t need saints. It works with people as they are and channels self-interest into serving others.
  • Equality. Human variety makes equal outcomes impossible without continual coercion.
  • Security. No system can guarantee security. The market supplies as much of it as can be supplied, through saving and insurance.
  • Power. “Economic power” is only the right to refuse an exchange. That is different in kind from political power, which is the power to use force.
  • Charges of luck, selfishness and materialism are taken one at a time and shown to rest on confusion about how the market works.
  • Human rights are property rights. Free speech, for example, is the right to use one’s own property and to make contracts.

Power and Market · Chapter 7

Conclusion: Economics and Public Policy

What a value-free science can still say about politics.

  • Economics can’t choose ultimate ends. It can say whether a policy achieves the ends its own supporters want, and interventions routinely fail that test.
  • There are two ways to organise society, by contract or by command. The market produces harmony, since every exchange benefits both sides. Intervention produces caste conflict between those who gain and those who pay.
  • The mixed economy is unstable. Each intervention creates problems that seem to call for another, pushing toward full statism unless intervention is rolled back toward the free market.

Context

Where Rothbard parts from Mises

The book set out to explain Human Action and ended up amending it. Their agreements run deeper than the disagreements, but the disagreements are what made Rothbard’s name.

Context

The arguments it started

Method

Most economists reject the idea that economic laws can be known a priori and want theories tested against data. The Austrian reply is that history never offers controlled repeats, so data can illustrate a theory but not test it.

Monopoly

Israel Kirzner and others hold that a single owner of a unique resource can charge a true monopoly price even on a free market, contrary to Chapter 10.

Banking

“Free banking” economists such as Lawrence White and George Selgin argue that competitive fractional-reserve banks are stable and legitimate. Rothbard’s heirs, including Joseph Salerno and Jesús Huerta de Soto, defend 100% reserves.

The state

Robert Nozick’s Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974) argued that competing protection agencies would converge on a dominant agency, in effect a minimal state. Rothbard replied in “Robert Nozick and the Immaculate Conception of the State” (1977).

The cycle

Critics ask why entrepreneurs would be fooled by cheap credit again and again, and dispute how well the theory fits the data. Defenders answer that entrepreneurs must act on the interest rate they can actually borrow at, whatever they suspect.

Terms of art

Glossary

Praxeology
The science of human action. Economics is its most developed branch.
Evenly rotating economy
An imaginary economy where every period repeats and nothing is uncertain. It isolates interest from profit and loss.
Time preference
Preferring a satisfaction sooner rather than later. It is the sole source of interest.
Natural rate of interest
The rate set by time preferences on the time market. Credit expansion pushes the loan rate below it.
Demonstrated preference
The principle that preferences are known only through actual choices.
DMVP
Discounted marginal value product: what a factor of production tends to earn.
Capitalization
Valuing a durable good at the discounted sum of its expected future rents.
Rent
In Rothbard’s wider sense, the price of any good’s services per period, not just land’s.
Structure of production
The stages goods pass through, from raw resources to the consumer.
Regression theorem
Mises’s explanation of money’s value, traced back to the money commodity’s earlier value as an ordinary good.
Fiduciary media
Money substitutes with no money commodity behind them, such as bank credit created from nothing.
Malinvestment
Investment in projects that available savings can’t complete. The bust reveals it.
Autistic / binary / triangular
Force applied to one person; a forced exchange with the intervener; force applied to an exchange between two others.
Contractual vs hegemonic
Social relations based on voluntary agreement vs those based on command and force.
Economic vs political means
Oppenheimer’s terms for getting wealth by producing and trading vs by taking it.
Private Product Remaining
Rothbard’s measure of output net of what government absorbs.

Using it

A reading path through 1,400 pages

If you won’t read it cover to cover, this order gets the core argument in roughly a third of the length.

  1. MES ch. 1–2

    The method and the logic of exchange. Everything else depends on these two chapters.

  2. MES ch. 5–6

    Capital and interest, the analytical core and the book’s most original economics.

  3. MES ch. 10

    Monopoly and competition, the most contested chapter.

  4. MES ch. 11–12

    Money, banking and the business cycle.

  5. P&M ch. 2–4

    The typology of intervention, price controls and taxation.

Read alongside

  • Ludwig von Mises, Human Action (1949): the book this one set out to explain.
  • Rothbard, “Toward a Reconstruction of Utility and Welfare Economics” (1956): the welfare theory behind P&M ch. 2.
  • Rothbard, What Has Government Done to Our Money? (1963): the monetary argument, short.
  • Rothbard, America’s Great Depression (1963): the cycle theory applied to 1929.
  • Rothbard, The Ethics of Liberty (1982): the natural-rights case this book deliberately leaves out.
