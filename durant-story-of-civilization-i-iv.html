The Story of Civilization, I–IV

From the first cities of Sumer to the death of Dante

“Civilization is social order promoting cultural creation.”

The first sentence of Volume I

Will Durant, with Ariel Durant · Volumes I–IV · 1935–1950

The whole book in one breath

Durant set out to do what professional history had stopped attempting: to tell each civilisation whole, its work, government, morals, religion, science, thought and art in one narrative, and to tell them all. These first four of eleven volumes, about 3,750 pages written between 1935 and 1950, run from the Old Stone Age to Dante. Volume I argues that Europe is “the spoiled child” of the Near East, India and China. Volume II follows Greece as it invents much of Western secular life and then destroys itself through faction. Volume III runs Rome and Christianity side by side until an emperor presides over a council of bishops, and blames Rome’s fall on decay within. Volume IV gives Byzantium, Islam, the Jews and Latin Europe their own histories, and credits Islam with leading the world for five centuries. The same convictions run through all four: order must come first, religion and morals move with economics, “a nation is born stoic, and dies epicurean,” and civilisation “does not die, it migrates.”

The method

Integral history

The first preface explains the plan and its folly. Durant had long felt that writing history in “separate longitudinal sections,” economic history, political history, the history of religion, of philosophy, of art, “does injustice to the unity of human life,” and that history “should be written collaterally as well as lineally.” He knew the price: “any man who sells his soul to synthesis will be a tragic target for a myriad merry darts of specialist critique.” Such a venture, he admits, “has no rational excuse, and is at best but a brave stupidity.” By Volume III he calls the method “synthetic history,” and by Volume IV “integral history”: “the presentation of all phases of a culture or an age in one total picture and narrative.”

The definition that opens the series drives the arrangement. “Civilization is social order promoting cultural creation,” and four elements constitute it. Each culture in these volumes gets its economy, its government, its morals and religion, and its letters, science and art, often in that order, so that a chapter on Athenian finance sits beside one on Athenian sculpture without apology.

The whole project

The series and its plan

Durant wrote in 1935 that he had laid the task on himself “some twenty years ago.” He planned five parts, from the Orient to “our own times.” The plan kept growing. By Volume IV he expected a volume on the Renaissance and Reformation within a few years and one on the Age of Reason carrying the story “to our own time” by 1960, after which he would be too close to “senility” to write about the Americas. In the event there were eleven volumes, the last in 1975, and the story stopped at 1815. Ariel Durant, to whom Volume I is dedicated, worked on the series throughout and was named co-author from Volume VII.

Read consecutively

What recurs across the four volumes

Durant does not call these laws, but he returns to them often enough that they work as the series’ theory. Each has a line or a chapter behind it.

Vol. I, Chapter I

Order comes first

Civilisation “begins where chaos and insecurity end.” His collapses, from Egypt to Rome, begin when the fiscal and political basis of order fails.

Vol. I, Chapter I

No race makes a civilisation

“It is not the great race that makes the civilization, it is the great civilization that makes the people.” Volume III’s epilogue strains against this; see “What has dated.”

Vol. I, Envoi

The West is a debtor

Farming, writing, law, coinage, the alphabet, the calendar, the arch and monotheism all appear first in the East. Greece was “the spoiled heir of three millenniums.”

Vol. I, Chapter IX

Born stoic, dies epicurean

“A nation is born stoic, and dies epicurean.” Said of Babylon, repeated of Crete, and the implicit shape of Rome.

Vol. I, Chapter IV

Morals follow economics, and religion lags

“Morals slowly adjust themselves to economic invention,” and religion, looking to the past, is left behind until the “conflict between science and religion.”

Solon, the Gracchi, Wang Mang

Wealth concentrates until it is redistributed

By law, as with Solon’s cancelled debts, by reform that fails, as with the Gracchi and China’s “experiments in socialism,” or by revolt.

Ptolemies, Diocletian, Wang An-shih

Managed economies recur, and disappoint

“The lesson of Ptolemaic socialism is that even a government may exploit.” Diocletian’s price edict’s “failure was rapid and complete.”

Vol. II, Epilogue

Freedom and order pull apart

Greece’s “tragic failure to unite liberty with order and peace”; Rome’s answer, monarchy, bought peace and killed the citizen’s civic sense.

Vols. II–IV

Religion returns when the state fails

Philosophy “surrendered” to religion in the Hellenistic age; the Oriental faiths filled imperial Rome; “an age of power gave way to an age of faith.”

Vol. III, Epilogue

Decay comes from within

“A great civilization is not conquered from without until it has destroyed itself within.”

Vol. II, Epilogue; Vol. IV, Chapter XIV

Civilisation migrates

“Civilization does not die, it migrates.” It is “polygenetic,” and some younger culture always snatches it “from the conflagration.”

Throughout

Great men, and great art, justify the story

The narrative moves through Hammurabi, Ikhnaton, Confucius, Pericles, Caesar, Jesus, Mohammed, Aquinas and Dante, and art is the element he writes about with most love.

Durant opens the series in the East, he says, not only because Asia had the oldest civilisations but because they “formed the background and basis” of the Greek and Roman culture that a Western reader wrongly takes for the whole story. Volume I · Introduction · Chapters I–VI

The Establishment of Civilisation

Before any civilisation, the primitive inventions: provision, government, law, the family, marriage, morals, religion, speech, science and art.

What civilisation is (Chapter I)

  • The book’s first sentence is its definition: “Civilization is social order promoting cultural creation.” Four elements make it up: economic provision, political organisation, moral traditions, and the pursuit of knowledge and the arts. “It begins where chaos and insecurity end.”
  • Conditions help or hinder but do not create it. Geology first (“Civilization is an interlude between ice ages”), then geography (tropical heat and parasites are hostile), then economics, which matter most: a hunting people, however orderly, never quite passes from barbarism to civilisation.
  • “There are no racial conditions to civilization.” It can appear on any continent and in any colour: “It is not the great race that makes the civilization, it is the great civilization that makes the people.”
  • Civilisations die when their conditions fail: a climatic change, an epidemic like the one that killed half the Roman Empire under the Antonines, exhausted soil, shifted trade routes, or “mental or moral decay.”

Economic elements (Chapter II)

  • Primitive man was improvident; the art of storing food, “the virtue of prudence, the concept of time,” had to be learned, and Durant notes that provision, providence and prudence are the same word.
  • While men hunted, “woman was making the greatest economic discovery of all, the bounty of the soil.” Hunting and fishing were never mere stages; they survived as modes of life, while tillage made settled society possible.
  • Fire began industry; weaving, pottery, building, transport and trade followed. Money came late, after long ages of barter in cattle and other goods.
  • Land was usually held in common among primitive peoples, and food shared. Private property grew with agriculture, the family and trade, and agriculture “led not only to private property but to slavery.” Through slavery and the division of labour the rough equality of natural society gave way to classes.

Political elements (Chapter III)

  • “Man is not willingly a political animal.” The simplest societies have hardly any government; the clan and the tribe come first.
  • “It is war that makes the chief, the king and the state, just as it is these that make war.” The state usually begins as the subjection of settled farmers by a tribe of hunters or herders, and soon becomes an indispensable prop to order.
  • Law grows through stages: private revenge, then fines and damages in place of revenge, then courts, ordeal and duel, and finally the state taking on the duty of preventing crime. The individual has fewer “rights” in natural society than under civilisation.
  • The family began with the mother. Women did most of the early economic work and made most early economic advances; the passage to the patriarchal family as farming grew more profitable “was fatal to the position of woman.”

Moral elements (Chapter IV)

  • Marriage has taken every form, from trial and group marriage to polygamy, capture and purchase. Individual marriage came, Durant argues, through the male desire for “cheap slaves” and for heirs, and there is “hardly a trace of romantic love” in the primitive forms.
  • Chastity, the double standard and modesty came with property and the patriarchate. Morals differ by time and place, but that does not make them worthless: old customs represent the natural selection of ways of living, and wisdom treats them with respect.
  • “Moral progress in history lies not so much in the improvement of the moral code as in the enlargement of the area within which it is applied.”
  • Religion springs from fear, wonder, dreams and the idea of a soul; it worships sun, stars, earth, sex, animals and ancestors; its methods are magic, sacrifice and festival, and “magic begins in superstition, and ends in science.” The priest “did not create religion, he merely used it.”
  • “Religion is not the basis of morals, but an aid to them.” It sanctions established customs, lags when conditions change, and in the later stages of every civilisation meets the “conflict between science and religion,” after which conduct, stripped of its supports, risks sliding into “epicurean chaos.”

Mental elements and prehistory (Chapters V–VI)

  • “In the beginning was the word, for with it man became man.” Language made thought and education possible; writing, a product of commerce, came very late.
  • Primitive science is counting, star-watching and medicine; primitive art begins in the painting of the body, clothing as ornament, pottery, dance and song.
  • A chart runs from Peking and Java man through the Chellean, Acheulean, Mousterian, Aurignacian, Solutrean and Magdalenian cultures (about 125,000 to 16,000 BCE on his reckoning) to the kitchen middens and the Swiss lake-dwellers.
  • The cave paintings unsettle any simple faith in progress: “The whole interpretation of history as progress falters when we consider” that the surviving work may be a tiny fraction of what paleolithic artists made.
  • Then the passage to history: copper, bronze and iron; writing, probably born of trade marks on pottery; the possibility of lost civilisations; and Central Asia, where the American geologist Raphael Pumpelly dug at Anau, as one candidate cradle.

Volume I · Book One, The Near East · Chapters VII–VIII

Sumeria and Egypt

The first extensive civilisation, and the one Durant thinks may have been the greatest.

The debt (Chapter VII opening)

Book One begins with a catalogue of what the Near East invented, from agriculture, the wheel and coinage to the alphabet, the calendar, the arch, monotheism and monogamy, “the checkers and dice, the ten-pins and income-tax.” The “Aryans” did not establish civilisation; they took it from Babylonia and Egypt. Greece “was the spoiled heir of three millenniums of arts and sciences.”

Elam and Sumeria (Chapter VII)

  • At Susa in Elam, French excavators found the oldest potter’s wheel and wagon wheel, and painted pottery ranked among the finest ever made.
  • The Sumerians, of unknown race and origin, built the city-states of Ur, Lagash, Uruk and Nippur, and with their Semitic neighbours in Akkad produced “the first extensive civilization known to history.”
  • Urukagina of Lagash was a royal reformer who curbed the priests and the rich; his decrees are, Durant says, “the oldest, briefest and justest code of laws in history.” A conqueror sacked his city, and one of the oldest poems mourns it.
  • The Semitic king Sargon of Akkad conquered the Sumerian cities; the “Golden Age” of Ur followed under Ur-Engur, who issued a law code in the name of the sun-god.
  • Sumerian writing (inscriptions from about 3600 BCE, clay tablets from about 3200 on his dates), business contracts and credit, temples, statuary and jewellery passed north to Babylonia and Assyria and west, Durant thinks, to Egypt. But “by its side Sumeria was but a crude beginning.”

Egypt (Chapter VIII)

  • The chapter opens as a travelogue, from the harbour of Alexandria upstream past the Pyramids, Memphis, Hatshepsut’s temple and Karnak, and then turns to history: Champollion and the Rosetta Stone, and the unearthing of prehistoric Egypt.
  • The Old Kingdom forged a state out of the “nomes” and built the Pyramids, paid for, Durant says following Herodotus, by “a hundred thousand slaves who toiled for twenty years.” Pepi II reigned, on his reckoning, ninety-four years.
  • After a feudal age, the Twelfth Dynasty and the Hyksos invasion, the Empire of Thutmose III and Queen Hatshepsut marks the zenith.
  • The Egyptian state took censuses and income-tax returns and forecast harvests from Nilometers, “a planned economy regulated by the state” at the outset of history. Scribes did the paperwork; royal marriage to sisters kept the blood pure; women had a status unusual in the ancient world.
  • Literature ranges from the story of Sinuhe and love poems to the moral “Instructions of Ptah-hotep” and the gloomy “Dialogue of a Misanthrope.” Science produced geometry, a solar calendar and surgery; religion produced Osiris and the Book of the Dead, whose “Negative Confession” is “one of the earliest and noblest expressions of the moral sense in man.”
  • “The greatest element in this civilization was its art,” mature at the threshold of history, “superior to that of any modern nation, and equaled only by that of Greece.”
  • Ikhnaton, “a Shelley called to be a king,” rejected Amon and his priests for a single god, the sun-disc Aton, and wrote the hymn Durant calls the fairest surviving remnant of Egyptian literature. The empire broke up while he wrote, and the priests restored the old gods under Tutenkhamon.
  • Rameses II built enormously; the clergy grew rich as the people grew poor; Egypt fell in turn to Libyans, Ethiopians, Assyrians, Persians, Greeks and Romans.
  • Yet “the sands have destroyed only the body of ancient Egypt.” Its bequest, in Durant’s closing list: better farming, metallurgy and engineering; the apparent invention of glass and linen, paper and ink, the calendar and the clock, geometry and the alphabet; orderly government with census and post; schools, even training for office; and “the first clear formulation known to us of individual and public conscience, the first cry for social justice, the first widespread monogamy, the first monotheism.”

“It is even possible,” Faure has said, “that Egypt … offers the spectacle of the greatest civilization that has yet appeared on the earth.” We shall do well to equal it.Volume I, the last words of Chapter VIII

Volume I · Book One · Chapters IX–XI

Babylonia, Assyria and a Motley of Nations

A civilisation of merchants, an empire of soldiers, and the small peoples who passed on coinage and the alphabet.

Babylonia (Chapter IX)

  • “Civilization, like life, is a perpetual struggle with death.” Babylon inherited Sumer and gave its heritage to Assyria, Persia, the Jews and Greece.
  • Hammurabi, whose dates Durant gives as 2123–2081 BCE, unified the land between the rivers and issued the great code, received, like all Babylonian law, from a god, which kept “its substance … through fifteen centuries.”
  • Its law moved “from supernatural to secular sanctions, from severity to lenience, and from physical to financial penalties.” The accused leapt into the Euphrates, “and the gods were on the side of the best swimmers”; an eye cost an eye, and if a house fell and killed the buyer’s son, the builder’s son died; later a commoner’s eye could be paid for with sixty shekels and a slave’s with thirty.
  • The code fixed surgeons’ fees and builders’ and labourers’ wages, divided inheritances equally among sons, and discouraged litigation with its very first law: an accuser who could not prove a capital charge was himself put to death. The state restored stolen goods when the thief was not found.
  • After Kassite rule and Assyrian conquest, Nebuchadrezzar spent a reign of forty-three years making Babylon “the largest and most magnificent metropolis of the ancient world.”
  • Religion was bound to the state and largely divorced from morals; Ishtar and Tammuz, the Creation and Flood stories, penitential psalms and magic pervade it. Sacred prostitution and easy morals mark, for Durant, a commercial culture’s relaxation.
  • In literature the Epic of Gilgamesh; in science the mathematics and astronomy that gave us the zodiac and the division of hours and minutes by sixty; in philosophy a “Babylonian Job” and a “Babylonian Koheleth.”
  • “A nation is born stoic, and dies epicurean.” The chapter ends: “In the end nothing is lost; for good or evil every event has effects forever.”

Assyria (Chapter X)

  • A military state three hundred miles north of Babylon, hardened by mountain enemies, conquered its parent cities and built an empire under Sargon II, Sennacherib, Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal.
  • If one grants the imperial principle, Assyria was an achievement in administration; but its annals are “a dull and bloody mess of war after war, siege after siege, starved cities and flayed captives,” and Durant quotes them at length.
  • Its art equalled Babylon’s and in bas-relief surpassed it; Ashurbanipal collected a great library.
  • Nineveh fell in 612 BCE. Two centuries later Xenophon’s Ten Thousand marched over its mounds without suspecting a city had been there. “Even Ashur, the everlasting god, was dead.”

A motley of nations (Chapter XI)

  • Among the Indo-European peoples: the Mitannians, the Hittites, the Phrygians with their mother goddess, and the Lydians, whose king Croesus is associated with the first coinage.
  • Among the Semites: the Phoenicians, whose ships circumnavigated Africa and whose alphabet spread through their trade; the Syrians, worshippers of Astarte and Adonis, a dying and rising god; and a culture that still sacrificed children.
  • The chapter ends by turning to the “numerically and geographically insignificant Jews, who gave to the world one of its greatest literatures, two of its most influential religions, and so many of its profoundest men.”

Volume I · Book One · Chapter XII

Judea

A small, poor country whose religion and literature outlived every empire that crushed it.

  • Palestine, 150 miles from Dan to Beersheba, lay on the road between the empires. The Jews, Durant says, were Semites like their neighbours: “it was their history that made them, not they who made their history.”
  • From Abraham and the sojourn in Egypt through the Exodus and the conquest of Canaan, the story runs to the judges and kings: Saul, David, and Solomon, whose wealth and Temple mark “the beginning of the class war in Israel.”
  • Yahveh begins as a tribal god among other gods and becomes, through the priests and prophets, the one God. Durant surveys sin, sacrifice, circumcision and the priesthood.
  • The Prophets are “the first radicals,” “eloquent members of the Opposition” who came up from the countryside to damn the corrupt wealth of the towns. Their predictions were “hopes or threats”; they spoke out rather than foretold.
  • Amos, a village shepherd, attacked the landlords who “sold the righteous for silver and the poor for a pair of shoes” and a God who despises feasts: “let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream.” “This is a new note in the world’s literature,” the social conscience entering religion. “With Amos begins the gospel of Jesus Christ.”
  • Isaiah, a statesman as well as a preacher, urged the kings of Judah to stay neutral and not to lean on Egypt, “that broken staff,” cursed the nations, and cried “Woe unto them that join house to house, that lay field to field”; he also foretold a Messiah. The book under his name, Durant notes, collects sermons by several authors from 710 to 300 BCE. The Prophets’ greatest effect was on the writing of the Bible.
  • Nebuchadrezzar destroyed Jerusalem and carried the leaders into captivity; Jeremiah, Ezekiel and the Second Isaiah gave exile its meaning; Cyrus let the Jews go home, and the Second Temple rose.
  • Without an army, the restored community organised itself around a book. Durant follows the critical scholarship of his day on the composition of the Pentateuch, and weighs the Mosaic law, the Ten Commandments, the sabbath and the Jewish family.
  • The Old Testament is history, poetry and philosophy “of the highest order”: the Psalms, the Song of Songs, Proverbs, Job, and the pessimism of Ecclesiastes, ending with the arrival of Alexander.

“The Jews, who are as old as history, may be as lasting as civilization.”Volume I, end of Chapter XII

Volume I · Book One · Chapter XIII

Persia

The largest empire before Rome, one of the best governed, and a religion of cosmic struggle.

  • The Medes helped destroy Assyria and then declined. Cyrus, “the most amiable of conquerors,” took Sardis and Babylon and “founded his empire upon generosity.”
  • His first principle was that conquered peoples should keep their gods, “for he fully understood the first principle of statesmanship: that religion is stronger than the state.”
  • Under Darius the empire had twenty satrapies from Egypt to India, royal roads and a royal post, a standing army of Persians and Medes, and gold “darics” whose ratio to silver Durant traces into modern bimetallism: “an achievement in administration.”
  • Zarathustra taught that the world is a battlefield between Ahura-Mazda, the good spirit, and Ahriman, “the Living Lie,” and that every person, having free will, is a warrior on one side. The code centred on its own Golden Rule and on a threefold duty: to make an enemy a friend, the wicked righteous and the ignorant learned.
  • Fire and the sun were revered; the dead crossed a “Sifting Bridge” to paradise or to a graded hell. The Last Judgment, Durant thinks, passed from Persian into Jewish belief; he calls the whole “a splendid religion, less warlike and bloody, less idolatrous and superstitious, than the other religions of its time.” Beneath it the cult of Mithra grew until it rivalled Christ, and Durant adds that Christmas began as a solar festival, “became a Mithraic, and finally a Christian, holy day.”
  • Yet the Persians remained brutal: Durant quotes Darius boasting of cutting off a rebel’s nose and ears. The art of Persepolis and the frieze of the archers show a fine borrowed style.
  • The empire “lasted hardly a century” in its vigour. Marathon, Salamis and Plataea broke its spirit; murders, harems and weak kings followed; and Alexander took the whole.

Volume I · Book Two, India and Her Neighbours · Chapters XIV–XVI

India: foundations, Buddha, and five thousand years of history

From the newly dug Indus cities through the Vedas and the Buddha to the Moghuls.

Foundations (Chapter XIV)

  • “Nothing should more deeply shame the modern student than the recency and inadequacy of his acquaintance with India.”
  • Mohenjo-daro, excavated in the 1920s, suggested an Indian civilisation as old as Sumer’s; Durant asks whether it might be “the oldest civilization.”
  • The Aryan invaders brought caste, which began as a colour line and hardened into warriors, priests, merchants, workers and outcastes; the village community endured beneath all governments.
  • The Vedas, four collections of hymns, chants, formulas and spells, with their prose commentaries, culminate in the Upanishads, whose theme is the identity of the self (Atman) with the world-soul (Brahman). Durant quotes Schopenhauer: the study of the Upanishads “has been the solace of my life.”

Buddha (Chapter XV)

  • Doubters, materialists and atheists already flourished; Mahavira founded Jainism, with its extreme reverence for life and its salvation even by starvation.
  • The Buddha’s legend runs from miraculous birth and a sheltered youth to the sight of age, sickness and death, flight, ascetic failure and enlightenment.
  • He taught by conversation and parable and wrote nothing; the Pali canon, the “Baskets of the Law,” was put into writing only about 80 BCE, so it gives “the teaching … of the Buddhist schools” as much as of the master.
  • The Four Noble Truths: life is pain; pain comes from craving, “the craving for passion, the craving for existence, the craving for non-existence”; pain ends when craving ends; and the way to that end is the Eightfold Way of right views, intention, speech, action, living, effort, mindfulness and concentration.
  • The five moral rules (do not kill, steal, lie, drink or be unchaste) are, Durant quotes, “perhaps more comprehensive, and harder to keep, than the Decalogue.” He stresses how much anticipates Christ (“Let a man overcome anger by kindness, evil by good”) and how agnostic, anti-clerical and “soul-less” the psychology was.
  • A man once abused the Buddha, who asked him to whom a refused present belongs, and then declined the abuse. “Unlike most saints, Buddha had a sense of humor, and knew that metaphysics without laughter is immodesty.”
  • The chapter ends with the dying Buddha telling Ananda to be “a lamp unto themselves.”

From Alexander to Aurangzeb (Chapter XVI)

  • Alexander’s raid in 327 BCE was followed by Chandragupta Maurya’s empire, a bureaucratic state described by Megasthenes and theorised by the minister Kautilya, “an older Machiavelli.”
  • Ashoka, his grandson, turned from conquest after the slaughter in Kalinga, joined the Buddhist order, and carved edicts of piety and tolerance across India. Durant calls him “the philosopher-king,” and counts both his failure (the empire fell soon after) and his success (Buddhism spread across Asia).
  • After six obscure centuries came the Gupta golden age (about 320–530 CE), the Chinese pilgrims Fa-Hien and Yuan Chwang, the chivalry of the Rajputs and the southern kingdom of Vijayanagar.
  • “The Mohammedan Conquest of India is probably the bloodiest story in history.” Its moral, for Durant, is that civilisation “is a precarious thing,” and that India, divided and unnerved, had “invited conquest” for four hundred years.
  • Akbar, tolerant, curious and a patron of every faith, tried to found a new religion; his successors Jehangir, Shah Jehan and Aurangzeb lost what he had won through cruelty, extravagance and intolerance, and a “little island” sent its traders, and then its guns.

Volume I · Book Two · Chapters XVII–XXII

India: the people, the gods, the mind and the arts

Caste and marriage, a paradise of gods, six systems of philosophy, epics and temples, and an epilogue on British rule and Gandhi.

The life of the people (Chapter XVII)

  • A jungle soil, famine and heavy taxes kept most Indians poor. The law-book of Manu codified caste, and the Brahmans rose to privilege and duty; Durant offers a qualified defence of caste as a system of order before condemning its later rigidity.
  • Morality was dharma, the rule of one’s caste. Child marriage, temple prostitution, purdah and suttee sat beside a people “pre-eminent in gentleness, decency and courtesy.”

The paradise of the gods (Chapter XVIII)

  • Buddhism peaked two centuries after Ashoka, split into the Two Vehicles, and then decayed in India while spreading to Ceylon, Burma, Tibet, China and Japan.
  • Hinduism, “a medley of faiths and ceremonies,” centres on Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva, Krishna and Kali, the sacred cow, reincarnation and karma, with life seen as evil and release as the goal.
  • The chapter ends with a wry forecast: the East may go in for science and industry just as a disillusioned West returns to “another mystic faith.” “There is no humorist like history.”

The life of the mind (Chapter XIX)

  • Hindu science gave the world the “Arabic” numerals and the decimal place system, along with algebra, astronomy and a medicine that, Durant claims, knew inoculation and anaesthetics.
  • The six orthodox systems of philosophy (Nyaya logic, Vaisheshika atomism, Sankhya dualism, Yoga discipline, Mimamsa ritualism and Vedanta monism) culminate in Shankara’s doctrine that the world is maya. Hindu philosophy, Durant says, “begins where European philosophy ends,” with the nature of knowledge and the limits of reason.

Literature and art (Chapters XX–XXI)

  • The epics: the Mahabharata and the Ramayana. In the Bhagavad-Gita, the Mahabharata’s philosophical heart, the warrior Arjuna refuses to kill his kinsmen, speaking “the philosophy of Gandhi and Christ”; the god Krishna answers that his caste duty is to fight, that only the body dies, and expounds a synthesis of Sankhya and Vedanta.
  • The drama, above all Kalidasa’s Shakuntala; fables, history and tales; the vernacular poets Chandi Das and Tulsi Das; and Kabir, who tried to unite Hindu and Muslim, and whose followers still form two sects, one Hindu and one Moslem.
  • Indian art runs from the Ajanta frescoes and the Gandhara and Gupta sculptures to the temples of the north and south, the “colonial” splendours of Angkor, and the Moghul masterpieces ending in the Taj Mahal.
  • His verdict is severe: the Hindu’s climate and religion enfeebled him in war; history punished him “for neglecting science”; and at Plassey in 1757 the British cannon “announced the Industrial Revolution.” “The old civilization of India is finished. It began to die when the British came.”

A Christian epilogue (Chapter XXII)

  • The British conquest, from Clive and Hastings to the Mutiny of 1857, gets an openly hostile account; Christianity, the Brahmo Samaj, Ramakrishna and Vivekananda answer it in religion; Rabindranath Tagore in poetry.
  • The last pages, written as the independence movement grew, portray Gandhi, “the ugliest, slightest, weakest man in Asia.” At his trial in 1922 he pleaded guilty and asked for the highest penalty: “Nonviolence is the first article of my faith. It is also the last article of my creed.” He was sentenced to six years and released after an operation for appendicitis.
  • His campaign against machine industry, symbolised by the spinning-wheel, “failed,” Durant judges; yet in Bombay alone 150,000 pieces of imported cloth were burned in a day, and the movement gave India “a symbol of revolt.” “It was Gandhi’s task to unify India; and he accomplished it. Other tasks await other men.”

Volume I · Book Three, The Far East · Chapters XXIII–XXVII

China

The age of the philosophers, of the poets and of the artists, a government praised by Voltaire, and a civilisation struck down in Durant’s own time.

The age of the philosophers (Chapter XXIII)

  • The Enlightenment discovered China; Durant quotes Diderot and Voltaire’s admiration. China is “the paradise of historians,” though its annals are trustworthy only from the eighth century BCE.
  • Under the feudal Chou kingdom came the Book of Odes, the yin and yang of the Book of Changes, and a Chinese enlightenment.
  • Lao-tze, whose authorship of the Tao-Te-Ching Durant notes is disputed, taught the Tao, nature’s silent order, against the intellectual who “wishes to construct a society like geometry.” “The more display there is of legislation, the more thieves and robbers there are”; the ideal is a little state whose people could see the next village and never visit it. Durant compares him to Rousseau.
  • Confucius, a teacher in search of a state, served briefly as a magistrate and minister of crime in Lu, then wandered for thirteen years; he left the Classics and a practical agnosticism.
  • His “Higher Man” unites intelligence, courage and good will: “What the Higher Man seeks is in himself; what the lower man seeks is in others.” Sincerity is the foundation; the mean is the path.
  • The Golden Rule appears “four centuries before Hillel and five centuries before Christ,” always negatively: “Not to do unto others as you would not wish done unto yourself.”
  • His politics is government by example, popular welfare and the right ordering of family and state. His success was posthumous, “but complete.”
  • Then the debaters: Mo Ti preaching universal love, Yang Chu egoism, Hsun-tze the natural evil of man and the need of law, and Chuang-tze the return to nature. Voltaire found in Confucius “nothing but the purest morality.”
  • Mencius, second only to Confucius, held that men are by nature good and that disorder comes from bad government. The ruler’s war should be against poverty; punishing crimes bred by want is “a dastardly trap”; “There has never been a good war.” Ministers should depose a prince who will not mend his faults, the root of the principle that a hated ruler has lost “the mandate of Heaven.” The first Ming emperor was so angry that he had Mencius’s tablet removed from the Confucian temple, and restored within a year.

The age of the poets (Chapter XXIV)

  • Shih Huang-ti, “China’s Bismarck,” whom Chinese historians painted with “the heart of a tiger or a wolf,” unified the warring states in 221 BCE. On the advice of his Legalist minister Li Ssu he broke the feudal barons, ruled by explicit law and a strong centre, built the Great Wall, and, when the Confucian scholars protested, ordered the histories burned so that China’s history “might begin with Shih Huang-ti.” Scientific books and Mencius were apparently spared.
  • Friendless in old age, he sat with a sword across his knees and slept in a different room each night; his corpse was brought home disguised by a caravan of rotting fish, and his tomb held a map of the empire in quicksilver. His dynasty barely outlived him.
  • The Han emperor Wu Ti and the usurper Wang Mang ran “experiments in socialism”: state monopolies, an income tax, price controls and land nationalisation, which ended in revolt.
  • Under the T’ang, T’ai Tsung and Ming Huang presided over a golden age. Li Po, “the Keats of China,” and Tu Fu get long, affectionate chapters, with Po Chu-i and T’ao Ch’ien beside them, and the historian Szuma Ch’ien in prose.

The age of the artists (Chapter XXV)

  • The Sung premier Wang An-shih nationalised commerce, fixed wages and prices, and provided state insurance against unemployment and old age, before his enemies undid it all.
  • Printing developed from seals to block printing to movable type; paper money appeared. Chu Hsi remade Confucianism.
  • “China might loosely be defined as philosophy and porcelain.” Bronze, lacquer, jade, pagodas, and above all painting and porcelain, which Durant calls “the summit and symbol of Chinese civilization, one of the noblest things that men have done to make their species forgivable on the earth.”

The people and the state (Chapter XXVI)

  • Marco Polo at the court of Kublai Khan; then the Ming and the Manchu dynasties, and Ch’ien Lung rejecting the West.
  • The peasant’s poverty, the handicrafts, guilds and canals; inventions, including gunpowder under the T’ang, the compass and printing, made more easily than they were used.
  • A “religion without a church,” ancestor worship and “the rule of morals”; and a government by examination, tentative under the Han and definite under the T’ang.
  • Any man could sit the district examination on the Confucian classics, poetry, history and moral and political questions; success led to triennial provincial tests and finally to Peking, where candidates lived for three days in ten thousand cells writing essays. “There was not a word in any of the tests about science, business or industry; the object was to reveal not knowledge but judgment and character.”
  • Durant, like Voltaire, admires the result as “an aristocracy in its finest form, … democratically selected from every rank.” Its destruction was “an act of high tragedy.”

Revolution and renewal (Chapter XXVII)

  • “The White Peril”: the opium trade and Opium Wars, the Taiping rebellion, defeat by Japan and the scramble for concessions; Sun Yat-sen and the revolution of 1911.
  • “Once everything changed except the East; now there is nothing in the East that does not change.” China, “the most conservative nation in history,” has become, after Russia, the most radical: “It is not merely the end of a dynasty … it is the moulting of a civilization.”

Volume I · Book Three · Chapters XXVIII–XXXI and Envoi

Japan, and the Envoi

An unfinished drama in three acts, written as the third act turned to conquest.

  • Japanese history is “an unfinished drama in which three acts have been played”: classical Buddhist Japan (522–1603), civilised by China and Korea; the feudal, peaceful Japan of the Tokugawa (1603–1868); and modern Japan, opened in 1853 by an American fleet and “threatening both the ascendancy of the white race and the peace of the world. By every historical precedent the next act will be war.”
  • Primitive origins, the imperial age, the dictators, Hideyoshi (“Great Monkey-Face”), and Iyeyasu, founder of the Tokugawa shogunate, whose long peace Durant, with Lafcadio Hearn, thinks may have been “the happiest in the long life of the nation.”
  • The divine emperor was kept poor and secluded by the shoguns. Under the lords served a million or more samurai, on the principle that “every gentleman was a soldier, and every soldier a gentleman,” the sharpest difference from China, where the gentleman was a scholar. Their code, Bushido, defined virtue as the power “to die when it is right to die, to strike when it is right to strike,” and its final law was ritual suicide.
  • A harsh law that tolerated revenge, the toilers, the family, the saints and the thinkers, including the nationalist scholars whose movement to “honor the Emperor and expel the foreign barbarians” Durant sees still alive in 1935.
  • The mind and art of old Japan: poetry from the Manyoshu to the haiku, Lady Murasaki’s Tale of Genji, the essays of Sei Shonagon, the No drama, swords superior to Damascus and Toledo, pottery, painting and prints.
  • The New Japan: Perry’s ships in 1853, the Meiji Restoration, the industrial and cultural revolutions, and an empire forced by crowded islands and scarce iron and oil toward Manchuria and China.
  • A section “written in 1934” asks “Must America fight Japan?” and answers from a theory of markets: “Usually in history, when two nations have contested for the same markets, the nation that has lost in the economic competition, if it is stronger in resources and armament, has made war upon its enemy.”

Envoi: the heritage totalled

The volume closes by listing, element by element, what the West took from the East, and then what Greece and Rome would add. Greece would “create democracy and invent the individual”; Rome would spread and preserve the whole. The last sentence sets up the next two volumes: “The stage was set for the three-fold drama of Plato, Caesar, and Christ.”

Volume II · Book I, The Aegean Prelude · Chapters I–III

Crete, Mycenae and the Heroic Age

A sea empire rediscovered in Durant’s lifetime, the fortresses Schliemann dug up, and the world of Homer.

Crete (Chapter I)

  • The Mediterranean is the arena; the Greeks lived around it “like frogs around a pond,” in Plato’s phrase. Climate, Durant insists, “did not draw civilization to Greece; probably it has never made a civilization anywhere.”
  • Arthur Evans bought the site of Cnossus and in nine weeks of 1900, with 150 diggers, uncovered the palace of Minos, a labyrinth of rooms that seemed to confirm the legends of Daedalus and the Minotaur. The tablets were still undeciphered: the volume was written thirteen years before Michael Ventris read Linear B.
  • The Cretans pictured in their frescoes, their clan society, sea power and trade, their goddesses, bulls and double axe, and their painting, “the most attractive” of their arts, are reconstructed with the caution of a man “playing Cuvier to the scattered bones.”
  • About 1400 BCE the palace of Cnossus burned. Durant lists the possible roads to decay (deforestation, luxury, lost trade with Egypt, civil war, earthquake, revolution) and notes that the thoroughness of the destruction suggests invasion. “In the history of civilization Crete was the first link in the European chain.”

Before Agamemnon (Chapter II)

  • Heinrich Schliemann, who resolved at eight to rediscover Troy and later spent a merchant’s fortune on the search, turned spadework into “one of the romances of the century.”
  • The fortress-palaces of Tiryns and Mycenae show a warrior culture heavily indebted to Crete. At Troy, the Trojans as pictured by their enemies seem “less deceitful, more devoted, better gentlemen, than the Greeks who conquered them.”

The Heroic Age (Chapter III)

  • Hittite tablets of about 1325 BCE mention the “Ahhijava,” perhaps the Achaeans. Their legends, of Heracles, Theseus, Oedipus and the Argonauts, preserve a memory of migration and conquest.
  • Homeric society, reconstructed from the poems for about 1300–1100 BCE, is a “patriarchal despotism tempered with the beauty and anger of woman,” ruled “in peace by the family, in crisis by the clan.”
  • Durant retells the siege of Troy and the homecomings, and grants that every Greek took the war for granted.
  • About 1104 BCE the Dorians came down from the north, and Greece fell into a dark age from which it had to begin again.

Volume II · Book II, The Rise of Greece · Chapters IV–IX

Sparta, Athens and the Greek world

Two opposite experiments in government, a great migration around the seas, and the common culture that made scattered cities one Greece.

Sparta (Chapter IV)

  • The Dorians of Laconia conquered and enslaved the southern Peloponnese. Before Lycurgus, Sparta was a city of song and art like others; after him it “would never” be again.
  • Lycurgus may be legend; his code was real. It began with “a ruthless eugenics”: infants judged defective by a state council were thrown from Mount Taygetus.
  • At seven a boy was taken from his family into a regiment that was also a class; he learned to read “barely enough,” to endure, and to find his own food, when stealing was allowed but being caught was flogged. At thirty, if he had survived with honour, he became a full citizen and dined in the public mess.
  • Girls ran, wrestled and threw the javelin to become strong mothers; men married at thirty and women at twenty; celibacy was a crime, and bachelors were marched naked through the streets. The citizens lived as a warrior caste on the labour of the helots.
  • The result was a man strong in body and at home with hardship, and a state that produced almost nothing else. Durant visits Corinth, Megara with its poet Theognis, Aegina and the theatre at Epidaurus on the way north.

Athens to Cleisthenes (Chapter V)

  • Hesiod’s Boeotia, Delphi and the “lesser states” lead to Attica, where the clear air and “three hundred sunny days” frame the city.
  • Under the oligarchs, debt enslaved the poor and civil war threatened. Solon, chosen to mediate in 594, cancelled “all existing debts,” freed those enslaved for debt and forbade the practice; he refused to redistribute land. The rich called it confiscation, “but within a decade opinion became almost unanimous that the act had saved Attica from revolution.”
  • Peisistratus, “the dictator who would first violate and then consolidate the Solonian constitution,” seized power with the help of a tall woman dressed as Athena; his sons lost it. Cleisthenes established democracy.
  • “It was fortunate for Greece, and for Europe, that Cleisthenes completed his work, and Solon’s, twelve years before Marathon”: men who owned the soil and ruled the state would fight for both.

The great migration and the West (Chapters VI–VII)

  • Greek colonists spread through the Cyclades, the coasts of Asia Minor, the Hellespont and the Black Sea.
  • In Ionia philosophy was born at Miletus with Thales, Anaximander and Anaximenes; Polycrates ruled Samos; Heraclitus of Ephesus taught the flux; Anacreon sang of wine; and on Lesbos Sappho wrote the love poetry Durant quotes at length.
  • In the West: Sybaris and its luxury, Pythagoras at Croton, Xenophanes the anticlerical at Elea, the rich cities of Sicily, and Cyrene in Africa. “In the colonies, rather than on the mainland, were born Greek poetry and prose, mathematics and metaphysics, oratory and history.”

The gods and the common culture (Chapters VIII–IX)

  • Greek religion had three layers: chthonian gods of the earth and the dead, the Olympians, and the mysteries of Eleusis and Orpheus. Oracles, festivals and a sea of superstition surrounded them.
  • It was “in origin a system of magic rather than of ethics,” though poets and sculptors ennobled it; in the end “Greek philosophy, hardly willing it, destroyed the religion that had molded the moral life of Greece.”
  • Separate city-states shared a language, a literature, the Olympic and other games, the arts of the vase painter and temple builder, music and dance, and, by the sixth century, the beginnings of drama.
  • By 490 BCE Greek life “had become unprecedentedly secular,” and what the Greeks defended at Marathon was that achievement: they “won for European civilization the trying privilege of freedom.”

Volume II · Book III, The Golden Age · Chapters X–XIII

Freedom won, and the Athenian experiment

Marathon and Salamis, Pericles, a democracy that was real but narrow, and the economy and morals beneath it.

The struggle for freedom (Chapter X)

  • Marathon in 490, Miltiades, and the rivalry of the upright Aristides and the brilliant Themistocles, who built the fleet.
  • Xerxes came with what Durant calls a “polyglot avalanche.” Salamis in 480 and Plataea in 479 saved Greece.

Pericles and the democratic experiment (Chapter XI)

  • Shelley thought the period from Pericles to Aristotle “undoubtedly … the most memorable in the history of the world.” Pericles, incorruptible and aloof, was elected general year after year for some thirty years.
  • It was “one of the outstanding experiments in the history of government,” and a limited one: the franchise belonged to sons of two Athenian parents over twenty-one, “43,000 citizens out of an Attic population of 315,000.” Within that circle, equality at law and in the Assembly was real.
  • Most magistrates and all jurors were chosen by lot; the ten generals were elected, so that Pericles could give the state continuity, and Thucydides could call it a democracy in name and in fact “government by the greatest of the citizens.”
  • The state paid jurors, financed drama, built the Parthenon and opened to its citizens the chance “not only to live, but to live well.” “History can afford to forgive it all its sins.”

Work, wealth and morals (Chapters XII–XIII)

  • Farming, mines and workshops, trade and banking; metics and freedmen ran much of the commerce; slaves were bought and rented out, and “even the poorest citizen has a slave or two.” The number of comfortably off citizens doubled between 480 and 431, and private property stayed secure.
  • Children, private education, dress, character, courtesans and philosophers, the “placid acceptance” of love between men and boys, marriage without romance, and a civilisation “brilliant without the aid or stimulus of women.”
  • The fear of old age and of a gloomy Hades “makes Greek life less bright and cheerful than is fitting under such a sun.”

Volume II · Book III · Chapters XIV–XVII

Art, science, philosophy and drama in the age of Pericles

The Parthenon, rational medicine, the Sophists and Socrates, and the four great dramatists.

Art (Chapter XIV)

  • Painting rose from vases to walls and panels; sculpture reached its classic form in Myron, Polycleitus and above all Pheidias, who with his assistants carved the Parthenon’s statues and reliefs from 447 to 438.
  • Ictinus and Callicrates built the Parthenon from 447 under Pheidias and Pericles, the Doric order at its most refined, with Ionian grace added to Doric strength. Pheidias’s enemies, aiming at Pericles, charged him with stealing gold and ivory from the Athena statue and convicted him; Olympia paid his bail on condition that he make its colossal gold-and-ivory Zeus.
  • The classic style meant “proportion in the parts and unity in the whole; the supremacy of reason without the extinction of feeling.” It is “good that we are freeing ourselves from the Greeks,” Durant adds, “even perfection becomes oppressive when it will not change.”

Science (Chapter XV)

  • The mathematicians; Anaxagoras, prosecuted for impiety for describing the sun as “a mass of stone on fire”; and Hippocrates, whose rational medicine was “the epochal event” of Periclean science.
  • Science was halted by “obscurantist legislation” and by the turn of Socrates and the Sophists from nature to ethics: “Science stood still for a century while Greece succumbed to the charms of philosophy.”

Philosophy and religion (Chapter XVI)

  • The idealists of Elea, the atomists and Empedocles; then the Sophists, Protagoras first, teaching rhetoric and doubt. None of the great thinkers before Socrates was Athenian.
  • Socrates, known only through Plato and Xenophon, a “gadfly” who taught by questions, died for a philosophy that was “elusive, tentative, unsystematic,” and became “for Greek history a martyr and a saint.”

Literature (Chapter XVII)

  • Pindar’s odes and the Dionysian theatre; Aeschylus, the Persians, the Oresteia and Prometheus, whose cosmic themes overshadow his persons.
  • Sophocles, who beat Aeschylus for first prize at twenty-seven, wrote 113 plays of which seven survive, won at the Dionysia eighteen times and “ruled the Athenian stage more completely than Pericles contemporaneously ruled Athens.” He added a third actor and was “almost modern in his flair for psychology”: Oedipus, Antigone.
  • Euripides, “the philosopher” and reformer more than the craftsman, whose Medea (431) is his most powerful play and whose Trojan Women appeared in 415, the year Athens sailed for Sicily; he ended in exile, and wrote the Bacchae at the end.
  • Aristophanes, “an unclassifiable mixture of beauty, wisdom, and filth,” who blamed Athens’s troubles on democracy and irreligion, and lampooned Socrates in the Clouds.
  • History begins with Herodotus and matures in Thucydides.
  • The Greek effort “to subordinate fancy to reason” makes this literature feel contemporary: “we find it hard to understand Dante or Milton, but Euripides and Thucydides are kin to us mentally.”

Volume II · Book III · Chapter XVIII

The Suicide of Greece

The Peloponnesian War, 431–404 BCE: plague, Alcibiades, Sicily and surrender.

  • The simple citizen looks for one person to blame for a war; Durant looks at trade rivalry, Athenian imperialism and Spartan fear.
  • Almost every state in the Peloponnese sided with Sparta; Athens relied on its fleet and empire. Plague killed a large part of the crowded city and Pericles with it; the Peace of Nicias became “a brief truce of six years.”
  • “It was the imagination of Alcibiades that ruined the work of Pericles.” The Sicilian expedition of 415 ended in the destruction of the fleet and army at Syracuse.
  • After the disaster “nearly half the citizen body was now enslaved or dead.” Athens held out until 404, when Sparta took the city, pulled down the Long Walls and installed the Thirty.
  • The restored democracy committed one cruelty. In 399 Anytus, Meletus and Lycon charged Socrates with not recognising the state’s gods, introducing new divinities and corrupting the young. Before some five hundred jurors he refused to give up his mission (“I shall obey God rather than you”) and called himself the city’s “gadfly.”
  • He was convicted by a majority of sixty; had his defence been more conciliatory, Durant thinks, he would probably have been acquitted. Offered the chance to propose a penalty, he at last proposed a fine, and the second vote condemned him by eighty more. He refused Crito’s plan of escape and drank the hemlock. Later stories that Athens repented and punished his accusers Durant reports with caution.
  • “The Golden Age ended with the death of Socrates,” in an Athens exhausted in body and soul, whose olive groves had been burned and whose character had been degraded by war.

Volume II · Book IV, The Decline and Fall of Greek Freedom · Chapters XIX–XXII

Philip, Plato, Aristotle and Alexander

A century of broken hegemonies ends in Macedonian rule, while art and philosophy reach their peak.

Philip (Chapter XIX)

  • Sparta’s empire was “another tragedy of success brought low by pride.” Epaminondas of Thebes broke it at Leuctra in 371 and died at Mantinea in 362.
  • Athens built a second empire and lost it; Syracuse under Dionysius I grew rich and powerful.
  • Philip of Macedon, while Demosthenes denounced him, destroyed Greek independence at Chaeronea in 338, and was murdered in 336 as he prepared to cross into Asia.

Letters and arts in the fourth century (Chapter XX)

  • The orators, Isocrates preaching Greek unity against Persia, and Xenophon.
  • In art the individual freed himself from the temple: Apelles in painting, the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus, Praxiteles, Scopas and Lysippus. “Scopas played Byron to Pheidias’ Milton and Praxiteles’ Keats.”

The zenith of philosophy (Chapter XXI)

  • A middling century for science, the heyday of philosophy: Aristippus and the pursuit of pleasure; Diogenes the Cynic.
  • Plato as teacher, artist, metaphysician, moralist, utopian and would-be lawmaker for Dionysius II of Syracuse; the Academy, founded in 386.
  • Durant gives Plato’s account of how constitutions decay at length: the plutocrats make “drones and paupers to abound in the state”; the poor overthrow them; democracy flatters the multitude until liberty becomes anarchy, and “the most aggravated form of tyranny arises out of the most extreme form of liberty.” His own gloss: “When liberty becomes license, dictatorship is near.”
  • Aristotle as scientist, logician, metaphysician and political thinker, “the first of the Schoolmen” and still “the master of those who know.”
  • His politics, built on the study of 158 Greek constitutions (only Athens’s survives): “man is by nature a political animal”; each form of government is good when it serves all and bad when it serves the rulers; democracy is best when dominated by peasant proprietors and worst when ruled by an urban “rabble”; “equality is just, but only between equals.” He even defended slavery, as the rule of mind over body.

Alexander (Chapter XXII)

  • Aristotle’s pupil loved reading and conversation with scholars, but “one quality in him dominated all the rest: ambition.” He charged first at Chaeronea and plunged into every fight; Durant judges that “he was not a great general; he was a brave soldier.”
  • He conquered Persia in a series of victories and went on to the Indus. After nine years in Asia “he had changed the continent by his victories less than it had transformed him by its ways.”
  • Back in Babylon he drank heavily, caught a fever and died “in the thirty-third year of his age” in 323; asked to whom he left his empire, he answered “To the strongest.” Within a year Aristotle and Demosthenes were dead too; Durant ends the Book with “the end of an age.”

Volume II · Book V, The Hellenistic Dispersion · Chapters XXIII–XXX and Epilogue

Greek civilisation dispersed, and the coming of Rome

Not destroyed but spread: kingdoms, libraries, science at its height, new philosophies of consolation, and Roman conquest.

The kingdoms (Chapters XXIII–XXV)

  • In mainland Greece: wars among Alexander’s successors, concentration of wealth, and revolutionary reform attempted at Sparta by Agis and Cleomenes. Rhodes rose on trade; when an earthquake destroyed it, the whole Greek world sent help, “one of the rare occasions when all the Greek world thought and acted as one.”
  • The Seleucid empire gave the Near East order and Greek cities; Pergamum became a centre of art and learning; in Judea, Hellenism met resistance, and the Maccabees won independence in 142 BCE.
  • Ptolemaic Egypt ran “an extensive experiment in state socialism,” with royal ownership of the land, state monopolies of oil and banking, and output fixed by the government. “The lesson of Ptolemaic socialism is that even a government may exploit.” Alexandria was its glory: laid out on a grid by Dinocrates, with two avenues a hundred feet wide lit at night and shaded by colonnades, some three miles long, and home by about 200 BCE to four or five hundred thousand Macedonians, Greeks, Egyptians, Jews, Persians, Syrians, Arabs and others; probably a fifth were Jews. Almost nothing of it survives above ground.

Books, art and science (Chapters XXVI–XXVIII)

  • Libraries and scholars at Alexandria and Pergamum; the Jewish writings of the age; Menander’s comedy; Theocritus’s pastorals; and Polybius, who “makes it a point of honor to be dull” but understood that facts are worthless without interpretation.
  • Hellenistic art, realistic and even humorous, “near their zenith to the end.” “There Rome would find it, and pass it on.”
  • “The fifth century saw the zenith of Greek literature, the fourth the flowering of philosophy, the third the culmination of science”: Euclid and Apollonius in geometry; Archimedes of Syracuse, “the greatest of ancient scientists,” who found his principle of displacement in a public bath and ran home naked crying “Eureka!”; Aristarchus, who put the sun at the centre; Eratosthenes, who measured the earth; Hipparchus; and the anatomists Herophilus and Erasistratus.

The surrender of philosophy (Chapter XXIX)

  • Three strains of Greek philosophy ended three ways: the physical “culminated in Aristotle,” the metaphysical in Plato, the ethical in Zeno. The Skeptics doubted everything.
  • Epicurus, born on Samos in 341, took pleasure from Aristippus and tranquillity (ataraxia) from Pyrrho, and from 306 taught in a garden near Athens, welcoming women to his lectures. What he taught was, Durant says, “Epicurean in name only”: a sober, frugal pursuit of peace of mind, not self-indulgence.
  • Zeno of Citium, a merchant of mixed Greek and Phoenician parentage, was shipwrecked near Athens about 314, read Xenophon on Socrates at a bookstall, and followed the Cynic Crates; his Stoicism offered duty, providence and brotherhood to a world in which the city-state could no longer lift men up.
  • In the end philosophy returned to religion. The Stoic sense of sin, its inclusiveness and its asceticism prepared the way: “From Zeno of Tarsus to Paul of Tarsus was but a step, which would be taken on the road to Damascus.”

The coming of Rome (Chapter XXX)

  • Pyrrhus fought Rome and won ruinously; Rome entered Greece as a liberator against Macedon and stayed as a conqueror.
  • In 146 BCE Mummius sacked Corinth, and Roman soldiers used famous paintings as boards for dice. “The turbulent cities had at last found peace.”

Epilogue: our Greek heritage

Greek civilisation lived on through Rome and Byzantium into every part of modern life: schools and athletics, the alphabet and most literary forms, geometry and medicine, the column and the classic style, and above all the habit of seeking natural explanations. “Civilization does not die, it migrates; it changes its habitat and its dress, but it lives on.” Durant admits the blemishes, including slavery and “its tragic failure to unite liberty with order and peace,” and asks the lover of freedom, reason and beauty to hear past them.

Volume III · 1944

Caesar and Christ

III

Roman civilisation and Christianity from their beginnings to AD 325, when Constantine presided over the Council of Nicaea.

Chapters
30, in an Introduction, five Books and an Epilogue
Argument
Rome decayed within; the Church inherited it

Written during the Second World War, the volume says outright that it is about the present. The rise of Rome, its two centuries of peace and its “long, slow crumbling” is “surely the greatest drama ever played by man; unless it be that other drama which began when Caesar and Christ stood face to face in Pilate’s court.” The class wars of the Republic, the moral and biological “decadence” of the Empire and the struggle to keep some freedom against a despotic state are, Durant writes, our own: “De nobis fabula narratur: of ourselves this Roman story is told.”

“A great civilization is not conquered from without until it has destroyed itself within.”Volume III, Epilogue, “Why Rome Fell”

Volume III · Introduction · Chapter I

The Etruscan Prelude

Italy, the Etruscans who ruled Rome for a century, and the kings.

  • The Etruscans are “among the irritating obscurities of history.” Their art is nearly all we know of them: tombs, bronzes, painted banquets, and a religion of augury that Rome inherited.
  • Villanovan settlers reached Latium about 1000 BCE; Rome grew under kings, and then under Etruscan kings, the Tarquins, who drained the marshes and built the first great temple.
  • The Republic was born, by tradition, in 508 with the expulsion of the Tarquins. Durant keeps the traditional story, though he notes the sceptics who reject everything before 443 BCE, and he declines to follow them all the way.

Volume III · Book I, The Republic · Chapters II–V

The Republic: conquest and character

A constitution of checks, the conquest of Italy, the war with Hannibal, the stoic Roman, and the Greek conquest of Rome.

The struggle for democracy (Chapter II)

  • Patricians and plebeians fought for two centuries; the plebs won their tribunes, the Twelve Tables of law and access to the offices.
  • The constitution that resulted was “one of the ablest and most successful governments that the world has yet seen”: two consuls, a Senate, assemblies by centuries and by tribes, and a college of colleagues for every office, each able to block the others.
  • It rested on “the most successful military organization in history,” an army of citizens. From a city-state of 350 square miles Rome conquered Italy, planting colonies that relieved unemployment and held down the conquered: “a political synthesis brutal in execution, majestic in result.”

Hannibal against Rome (Chapter III)

  • Carthage, a Phoenician trading republic, fought Rome for Sicily; Regulus’s story and Hamilcar’s revenge lead to Hannibal, who crossed the Alps, destroyed Roman armies at Trasimene and Cannae, and stayed in Italy until he was recalled to defend Africa.
  • Rome refused to make peace, and Scipio carried the war to Africa and won at Zama in 202.
  • Hunted by Rome to the end, Hannibal took poison in 184: “Let us relieve the Romans from the anxiety they have so long experienced, since they think it tries their patience too much to wait for an old man’s death.”

Stoic Rome (Chapter IV)

  • The family under the father’s absolute power, a religion of household gods and exact ritual, a morality of gravity, piety and discipline, a farming economy on poor soil, and a city whose census of 234 BCE counted 270,713 citizens.
  • Religion helped morals by binding the citizen to family and state, though its stress on ritual “suggested that the gods rewarded not goodness but gifts and formulas.”

The Greek conquest (Chapter V)

  • Rome conquered Greece and Macedon “not so much through conscious design as through the compulsions of circumstance and the ever receding frontiers of security.”
  • Greece conquered Rome in return: religion and comedy for the plebs, philosophy, morals and art for the rich. The Scipionic circle fostered Greek learning and Latin literature: Ennius, Plautus, Terence.
  • Cato the Elder met the invasion with “disgust and dread” and pressed for the destruction of Carthage, which fell in 146 BCE, the same year as Corinth.

Volume III · Book II, The Revolution · Chapters VI–X

The Revolution, 145–30 BCE

A century of class war that ended the Republic: the Gracchi, Marius and Sulla, Pompey and Cicero, Caesar and Antony.

The agrarian revolt (Chapter VI)

  • Durant lists the causes: slave-grown corn from the provinces undercutting Italian farmers; slaves displacing free labour; great estates, the latifundia, bought by senators flush with war spoils; and returning soldiers who preferred the city’s free corn and games to the farm.
  • Tiberius Gracchus proposed in 133 to redistribute public land and was killed; his brother Caius, pressing wider reforms, died in 121.
  • Marius reformed the army into a force of volunteers loyal to their general; the Italian allies revolted for citizenship and won it; Sulla marched on Rome, proscribed his enemies and dictated his own epitaph: “No friend ever served me, and no enemy ever wronged me, whom I have not repaid in full.”

The oligarchic reaction (Chapter VII)

  • The Senate ruled with the business class; millionaires like Crassus exploited the provinces; the “new woman” and loosening marriages alarmed moralists; Cato the Younger stood out as the lone exemplar of the old ways.
  • Spartacus led a slave rising; Pompey cleared the seas of pirates and conquered the East; Cicero, consul in 63, crushed Catiline’s conspiracy and executed citizens without appeal. “The revolution was not over. With Caesar’s consulate it would begin again.”

Literature under the revolution (Chapter VIII)

  • Lucretius’s poem of atoms and freedom from fear; Catullus, “Lesbia’s lover”; and Cicero, whose prose “re-created the Latin language” and made it the vehicle of learning in Europe for seventeen hundred years, a fame “second only to Caesar’s. It was an exception that he could never forgive.”

Caesar (Chapter IX)

  • The rake who began as Catiline’s secret ally “ended as the remaker of Rome”: consul, conqueror of Gaul, and after the civil war with Pompey, dictator.
  • As statesman he reorganised the provinces, extended citizenship to Cisalpine Gaul, allowed full liberty of worship, and had Sosigenes devise the Julian calendar of 365 days with a leap day every fourth year.
  • Brutus and the “Liberators” killed him on the Ides of March, 44 BCE. Durant closes: “One wish had been granted to the most complete man that antiquity produced.”

Antony (Chapter X)

The murder interrupted “a great labor of statesmanship” and led to fifteen more years of war. The aristocracy fought its last battle at Philippi; Antony and Cleopatra lost to Octavian at Actium in 31; in 30 Egypt was annexed and Octavian ruled alone.

Volume III · Book III, The Principate · Chapters XI–XVII

Augustus, the Golden and Silver Ages, and Rome at work

A monarchy dressed as a republic, the great poets, the bad emperors, and the material zenith of the ancient world.

Augustan statesmanship (Chapter XI)

  • Octavian took the powers of the magistracies into his own hands while keeping their forms; within a decade after Actium, Durant writes, the Mediterranean knew an economic quickening without precedent: Augustus had not “made a desert and called it peace.”
  • He tried to make Romans good as well as happy; “it was an imposition that Rome never forgave him. Moral reform is the most difficult and delicate branch of statesmanship.”
  • Worried, Durant says, by “the racial transformation of Rome” as freed slaves and aliens multiplied and old families shrank, he limited manumissions on a sliding scale, and by the Julian laws made marriage obligatory for men under sixty and women under fifty, penalised the childless and restricted marriage across the orders.
  • His economics worked better than his morals: when Egypt’s treasure reached Rome, Suetonius says, “money became so abundant that the interest rate fell” from twelve to four per cent, and once property was safe again “investment took courage, trade expanded.”
  • Heir to Caesar at eighteen, master of the world at thirty-one, he died at Nola in AD 14 with a line from comedy: “Since well I’ve played my part, clap now your hands, and with applause dismiss me from the stage.”

The Golden Age (Chapter XII)

  • Virgil, “the most lovable of Romans,” and the Aeneid; Horace, introduced by Virgil to Maecenas; Livy’s history; and the “amorous revolt” of Ovid, exiled by Augustus.
  • Classic ages exalt “moderation and good taste,” and produce literature “more finished and less powerful”; but in these three poets Latin as a poetic medium reached a zenith it would never reach again.

The other side of monarchy (Chapter XIII)

  • Tiberius, able and dour; Gaius (Caligula); Claudius, scholarly and undervalued; Nero; the year of three emperors; the sensible Vespasian; the “most fortunate” Titus, who died in his second year; and Domitian’s terror.
  • On 18 July 64 a fire began in the Circus Maximus, burned for nine days and razed two thirds of Rome. Nero hurried back from Antium, opened the Field of Mars to the homeless and fed the people, but, according to Tacitus, whose “Senatorial prejudice must always be remembered,” found a scapegoat in the Christians.
  • The system depended on the man. When “idiocy would again reach the throne” the armies would run riot and the structure Augustus built would topple.

The Silver Age (Chapter XIV)

  • Petronius’s Satyricon; Seneca, whose Stoicism found its “most doubtful expression in the life, its most perfect expression in the writings”; Roman science, Pliny’s encyclopedia; Quintilian on education; Statius and Martial.

Rome at work, its art and its pleasures (Chapters XV–XVII)

  • Farmers, artisans, carriers, engineers, traders and bankers. Men worked longer and less intensely, produced for local markets, and the state “lived on its income, not on its debts”; “it was the material zenith of the ancient world.”
  • “The Romans were not of themselves an artistic people,” but in architecture they defended themselves against the Greek invasion: the arch, the vault and concrete. Flavian Rome was “proud, powerful, brilliant, materialistic, cruel, iniquitous, chaotic, and sublime.”
  • Epicurean Rome: education, the sexes, a Roman day, the theatre and the games of the amphitheatre, where gladiators were classed by weapon (net-and-dagger men, pursuers with sword and shield, chariot fighters, beast fighters) and feasted the night before they fought. Then the new faiths from the East: Isis, Mithras, and Yahweh, whose worshippers in Rome included some “who worshiped his incarnate and resurrected son.”

Volume III · Book III · Chapters XVIII–XX

Roman law and the philosopher kings

Rome’s most lasting creation, and the century of adoptive emperors.

Roman law (Chapter XVIII)

  • “Law was the most characteristic and lasting expression of the Roman spirit. As Greece stands in history for freedom, so Rome stands for order.”
  • The great jurists, the sources of law, the law of persons, property and procedure, and the “law of the nations” that Rome developed to rule peoples with other codes.
  • Through Justinian and the canon law it became the basic law of most of Europe and, within the British Empire, of Scotland, Quebec, Ceylon and South Africa.

The philosopher kings (Chapter XIX)

  • With Domitian’s murder in 96 “the principle of heredity disappeared for a century from Roman monarchy”: Nerva, Trajan the conqueror of Dacia, Hadrian the builder and traveller, Antoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius.
  • Marcus Aurelius gets the fullest portrait: the boy Hadrian “saw in … the stuff of kings,” the Stoic whose Meditations list what he owed his teachers, the ruler made “old at forty-eight” by war, plague and domestic sorrow. Rome gossiped about Faustina’s lovers; Marcus “said nothing,” promoted the supposed paramours and had her deified when she died.
  • He died of illness in his camp at Vindobona, presenting Commodus to the army as his successor; the people had “already begun to worship him as a god.”

Life and thought in the second century (Chapter XX)

  • Tacitus’s fierce histories, Juvenal’s satires, the younger Pliny as “a Roman gentleman,” and the reign of Commodus.
  • “Stoicism, which had begun by preaching strength, was ending by preaching resignation,” and the upper classes turned from philosophy back to the altars. “The age was heavy with the resurrection of the provinces and the incredible victory of Christ.”

Volume III · Book IV, The Empire · Chapters XXI–XXV

The Empire beyond Rome

Italy’s towns, the civilising of the West, Roman Greece, the Hellenistic East, and Judea.

  • Italy. Pompeii, preserved by its burial, and a roster of prosperous towns that produced most of the great Latin authors. “The first two hundred years of our era saw the zenith of the great peninsula.”
  • The West. Africa, Spain, Gaul and Britain were Romanised by roads, towns and law. The decisions of Augustus and Tiberius not to conquer Germany “were among the pivotal events of European history.”
  • Roman Greece. Plutarch; Epictetus, the slave-born Stoic; and Lucian, who “laughed at belief.” Stoicism, now voiced by a slave, “was preparing for the theology and ethics of Christianity. The Oriental mood was capturing the European citadel.”
  • The Hellenistic revival. Roman Egypt, Philo, Ptolemy’s astronomy and Galen’s medicine, the Syrian cities, Asia Minor and Mithridates. Monarchy at the top produced individualism below, and the promise of personal immortality became “the final and irresistible attraction of the Oriental faiths.” “All the world seemed conspiring to prepare the way for Christ.”
  • Rome and Judea. Parthia; the Hasmoneans; Herod the Great; the Pharisees, Sadducees and the Essenes, some four thousand ascetics living in common by the Dead Sea; and the Messianic expectation.
  • “No people in history has fought so tenaciously for liberty as the Jews, nor any people against such odds.” After Herod’s death Varus crucified 2,000 rebels and sold 30,000 Jews into slavery; Caligula ordered his statue set up in the Temple; procurators such as Felix, who ruled “with the powers of a king and the soul of a slave,” provoked the Zealots and the dagger-men.
  • The revolt of 66 ended with Titus’s capture of Jerusalem and the burning of the Temple in 70; Tacitus put the besieged at 600,000, and Josephus, the rebel commander turned Roman client, described the crucifixions. Judaism became a religion without a state, and a last revolt under Bar Cocheba was crushed in 135.

Volume III · Book V, The Youth of Christianity · Chapters XXVI–XXVII

Jesus and the Apostles

A historian’s life of Jesus, and the apostles who turned a Jewish movement into a religion for the Greek world.

  • “Did Christ exist?” Durant weighs the pagan and Christian sources and the Higher Criticism, and concludes that the Gospels record incidents “that mere inventors would have concealed”: the apostles’ quarrels, Peter’s denial, the despairing cry on the cross.
  • He dates the birth to about 4 BCE, follows the baptism by John, the mission in Galilee, the preaching of the coming Kingdom, and the last week in Jerusalem.
  • What was the Kingdom? The first Christians expected an earthly one (“Thy Kingdom come … on earth”), and soon; at times Jesus spoke of it as a state of the soul, “within you.” Radicals can quote his warnings to the rich and the apostles’ common purse; conservatives can quote “render unto Caesar” and the parable that takes slaves and interest for granted.
  • His ethic was “predicated on the early coming of the Kingdom”: the Beatitudes, turning the other cheek, indifference to property and government were “not rules for ordinary life” but “a semimonastic regimen” for an imminent world without law, marriage or war.
  • Little of it was new, and Durant lists the Jewish sources, from Leviticus’s love of neighbour to Hillel’s Golden Rule: “Nothing is new except arrangement.” For long Jesus thought of himself “purely as a Jew,” sending his disciples only to Jewish cities. “Nevertheless, he transformed everything by the force of his character and his feeling,” bringing “religion back from ritual to righteousness.”
  • Peter led the first community; Paul, born at Tarsus about the tenth year of the era, was “the founder of Christian theology” as missionary, theologian and martyr, who became “all things to all men, that I might save some of them.”
  • Paul created, Durant argues, “a theology of which none but the vaguest warrants can be found in the words of Christ”: that every human being inherits Adam’s guilt and can be saved only by the atoning death of the Son of God, who is not Israel’s Messiah but the Logos, “the wisdom of God,” through whom all things were made. It suited pagans who already knew dying and saving gods better than it suited Jews.
  • John gave Christ a Greek philosophical form as the Logos, continuing “Paul’s work of detaching Christianity from Judaism.”
  • “Christianity did not destroy paganism; it adopted it.” The Greek mind lived on in its theology, the Greek mysteries in the Mass.

“That a few simple men should in one generation have invented so powerful and appealing a personality, so lofty an ethic and so inspiring a vision of human brotherhood, would be a miracle far more incredible than any recorded in the Gospels.”Volume III, Chapter XXVI

Volume III · Book V · Chapters XXVIII–XXX

The Church grows, the Empire collapses, Christ triumphs

An organisation modelled on the state, a century of anarchy and debasement, Diocletian’s managed economy, and Constantine.

The growth of the Church (Chapter XXVIII)

  • Congregations modelled on the synagogue, then bishops, heresies and Gnostics; the pagan philosopher Plotinus; the Fathers from Ignatius to Tertullian and Origen.
  • The Church took Rome’s framework of government: bishops in place of prefects, archbishops beside governors, synods in place of provincial assemblies. “Rome died in giving birth to the Church; the Church matured by inheriting and accepting the responsibilities of Rome.”

The collapse of the Empire (Chapter XXIX)

  • After Commodus came the Severan dynasty, then half a century of anarchy, invasions and soldier-emperors. Political anarchy and economic decline were “each … the cause and effect of the other.”
  • The emperors debased the coinage to pay armies (see the figure). Aurelian restored the frontiers; Diocletian, the son of a slave, made himself an Oriental monarch and divided the Empire among four rulers.
  • His managed economy fixed the gold coin, nationalised or regulated industries, and in 301 issued an edict of maximum prices and wages. “Its failure was rapid and complete”; goods vanished, and Constantine revoked it. Taxes rose, officials were made liable for their towns’ quotas, and sons were bound to their fathers’ trades: “a serfdom of office.”
  • Durant judges Diocletian gently: faced with enemies on every side, the state did “what all nations must do in crucial wars,” and his contemporaries called him the “Father of the Golden Age.”

The triumph of Christianity (Chapter XXX)

  • For two centuries persecution was local and intermittent. At Smyrna in 155 the crowd demanded the death of Bishop Polycarp, eighty-six years old: “how then can I blaspheme my King who saved me?” At Lyon in 177 forty-seven Christians died by tortures “equaled only by the Inquisition.”
  • Decius, facing invasion in 249, used a “fever of patriotism and fear” to order every inhabitant to offer homage to Rome’s gods; Christians who refused were punished. The great persecution under Diocletian and Galerius followed, and failed.
  • Constantine won at the Milvian Bridge in 312; his conversion was “probably” a stroke of political wisdom more than of belief.
  • Wealth softened the Church: “While Christianity converted the world, the world converted Christianity, and displayed the natural paganism of mankind.” Monasticism began as a protest.
  • To settle the Arian controversy Constantine summoned the first ecumenical council to Nicaea in 325; at least 318 bishops came. The emperor who presided so graciously had killed his son, his nephew and his wife.

Volume III · Epilogue

Why Rome Fell, and what it left

“Not one cause but many,” and “not an event but a process spread over 300 years. Some nations have not lasted as long as Rome fell.”

The Roman achievement

  • “It is easier to explain Rome’s fall than to account for her long survival.” Rome gave the Mediterranean order, prosperity and peace for two hundred years, held back barbarism for two centuries more, and passed on the classic heritage.
  • “Rome has had no rival in the art of government”: a law that secured life and property “from the Decemvirs to Napoleon,” checks and balances that inspired American and French constitution-makers, municipal self-government for hundreds of cities, and “the miracle of a lasting peace.”
  • She invented little in science or industry but spread roads, commerce, education, the arch and dome, the realistic portrait and a language that became the Romance tongues and, until the eighteenth century, “the Esperanto of science, scholarship, and philosophy.”
  • The Church inherited the pagan priesthood’s structure, titles and festivals, “and captive Rome captured her conqueror.” Writing in 1944, Durant ends: “May she rise again.”

Volume IV · Book I, The Byzantine Zenith · Chapters I–VI

The fall of the West and the zenith of Byzantium, 325–565

A pagan emperor’s last stand, the barbarian flood, the making of the Church, Augustine, and Justinian.

Julian and the barbarians (Chapters I–II)

  • Julian, Constantine’s nephew, whose family was murdered around him, became emperor and tried to restore paganism; he died fighting Persia in 363. The famous last words, “Thou hast conquered, Galilean,” Durant dismisses as a later legend. “Eleven centuries would pass before Hellenism would have another day.”
  • The Empire had a 10,000-mile frontier. The “savior emperors” held it for a time; then Stilicho was murdered, Alaric took Rome in 410, the Vandals sacked it in 455, and in 476 a barbarian general deposed the last Western emperor.
  • The Western Empire had “worn itself into senile debility.” As a thousand years before, northern invaders entered Italy, mingled with its people and borrowed its civilisation: “the beginning and the end were the same. But the end was always a beginning.”

The progress of Christianity (Chapter III)

  • “If art is the organization of materials, the Roman Catholic Church is among the most imposing masterpieces of history.” It fought heresies, produced Jerome, the translator of the Bible, and the monks of the Egyptian desert.
  • Augustine, sinner, convert, philosopher and bishop of Hippo, wrote about 230 treatises and was still at his post when the Vandals came in 430.
  • The Confessions (c. 400), “the first and most famous of all autobiographies,” is a hundred-thousand-word act of contrition addressed to God, frank even about the desires that still returned to the bishop in sleep: “Thou hast created us for Thyself, and our hearts know no rest until they repose in Thee.”
  • The City of God (413–26), written after Alaric’s sack of Rome to answer pagans who blamed the disaster on Christianity, is “philosophy in history,” as the Confessions is “poetry in prose.”
  • “An age of power gave way to an age of faith.” If reason and science should one day fail to improve conscience or purpose, Durant writes, men would understand why their ancestors once “took refuge for a thousand years in humble faith, hope, and charity.”

Europe takes form (Chapter IV)

  • Britain becomes England under the Anglo-Saxons; Ireland, converted by Patrick, keeps learning alive; Gaul passes to the Franks under Clovis; Visigoths rule Spain; and Theodoric’s Ostrogoths rule Italy, where Boethius and Cassiodorus preserved what they could of classical learning.

Justinian and Byzantine civilisation (Chapters V–VI)

  • Justinian, with Theodora (an actress and worse, according to Procopius’s malicious “Secret History”) as empress and Belisarius as general, reconquered Africa and Italy at ruinous cost. “History rightly forgets Justinian’s wars, and remembers him for his laws”: the Code, Digest and Institutes. Durant’s verdict: he and Belisarius “were the last of the Romans.”
  • Byzantine economy mixed private enterprise, state regulation and nationalised industry; Constantinople founded a university of thirty-one teachers in 425.
  • St. Sophia was Justinian’s “supreme achievement, more lasting than his conquests or his laws.” But the style of the age was Oriental: autocracy, state-dominated church, and “an Asiatic lassitude” that fell on the Greek world just as Persia and Islam challenged it.

Volume IV · Chapter VII (end of Book I) and Book II, Islamic Civilization · Chapters VIII–X

Persia, Mohammed, the Koran and the conquests

From a Sasanian empire to a faith that in a century ruled from the Indus to the Atlantic.

  • The Persians (VII). The Sasanian empire, 224–641: its society, kings from Shapur to Khosru, and its art, until the Arab conquest.
  • Mohammed (VIII). Born into a poor family five years after Justinian died, orphaned young and raised by his grandfather and his uncle Abu Talib, he worked for and married the rich widow Khadija. In the Kaaba at Mecca stood idols, among them Allah, chief god of the ruling Quraish, and his three daughters.
  • In 610, in a cave on Mount Hira, he heard the angel Gabriel command “Read!”; Khadija believed him first, then Abu Bekr and the six Companions. The conversion of Omar was “almost as significant for Mohammedanism as the conversion of Paul had been for Christianity.” In 619 Khadija and Abu Talib died; Taif stoned him; the night journey to Jerusalem made that city Islam’s third holy place.
  • Pilgrims from Medina, prepared by its Jews for monotheism and a Last Judgment, invited him; he fled there in 622, and returned as master of Arabia. “When he began, Arabia was a desert flotsam of idolatrous tribes; when he died it was a nation.” He built on Judaism, Zoroastrianism and native belief “a religion simple and clear and strong.”
  • The Koran (IX). Its form, creed, ethics and politics, and its sources, largely Judaic. “Three books made and almost filled the Age of Faith: the Bible, the Talmud, the Koran,” all Semitic, and the drama of medieval history would be their competition.
  • The sword of Islam (X). The first successors, the Umayyad caliphate of Damascus (661–750) and the Abbasid caliphate of Baghdad (750–1058), whose empire at its start ran from Sind to the Atlantic; and Armenia, caught between empires.

Volume IV · Book II · Chapters XI–XIV

The Islamic civilisation

Economy, faith, cities, science, philosophy, poetry and art, East and West, and why the lead ended.

  • The Islamic scene (XI). “Civilization is a union of soil and soul.” The creed was the simplest of all, “There is no god but Allah, and Mohammed is His Prophet”; government moved from an elective ideal to caliphs; Baghdad, a city of wealth and misery where even the poor man “numbered himself among the rulers of the world.”
  • The routes and the science (XI–XII). Caravans of up to 4,700 camels crossed the Islamic world on roads radiating from Baghdad to the Chinese frontier. The Greek heritage, still alive in Syria when the Arabs came, was translated from Syriac and Greek, and Hindu astronomy and numerals followed (the first known mention of the numerals outside India was by a Syrian abbot in 662): “one of the brightest threads in the skein of history.”
  • Thought and art in the East (XII). The caliphs had the Greek heritage translated; Islam “borrowed from Christian Syria the legacy of pagan Greece, and returned it through Moslem Spain to Christian Europe.” Physicians and philosophers, above all Avicenna, whose Canon of Medicine ran to a million words and whose eighteen-volume Book of Healing covered mathematics to music; the Sufi mystics, the poets, the Arabian Nights, the mosque and the arabesque.
  • Western Islam (XIII). Durant rejects the story that the Arabs burned the library of Alexandria as “now generally rejected as a fable.” Fatimid Cairo had lighted streets and a caliphal library of 200,000 volumes; al-Azhar, founded as a college in 988, is “the oldest existing university.” In Spain the revenues of Abd-er-Rahman III were “probably more than the united governmental revenues of Latin Christendom,” and Hakam II made Cordova’s university “the greatest educational institution of his time.”
  • Grandeur and decline (XIV). The Seljuq Turks, whose vizier Nizam al-Mulk founded a famous college at Baghdad; the Moors of Spain and the Alhambra; Omar Khayyam of Nishapur (born 1038), whom Europe knows as a poet and Persia as “one of the greatest mathematicians of medieval times”; Sadi; Moslem science; al-Ghazali’s religious revival against the philosophers; Averroes, whose Aristotle reached the Schoolmen; and the Mongols, who took Baghdad in 1258.
  • His balance sheet is careful. Islam led the world for five centuries; Christendom “probably excelled” it in sexual morality and the status of women; Moslems “seem to have been better gentlemen,” keeping their word and showing mercy more often than the Christians who massacred Jerusalem in 1099. “The West lost the Crusades, but won the war of creeds,” while Islam, “bled by its tardy victory, and ravaged by Mongols, fell in turn into a Dark Age.”
  • The Book ends with a credo: “Civilization is polygenetic … and no one who studies its history can be a bigot of race or creed.”

Volume IV · Book III, Judaic Civilization · Chapters XV–XVII

The Talmud and the medieval Jews

A people with “a law, but no state; a book, but no home.”

  • After the defeat of 135 the rabbis of Palestine and Babylonia produced the Mishna and the Talmuds; the academies of Sura and Pumbeditha, and the Gaonate, led Jewish life for centuries.
  • Jewish communities spread through Islam and Christendom. In Christendom they were pushed toward moneylending, taxed, expelled and massacred; the yellow badge “burned into the soul,” leaving in the northern Jew that “somber Judenschmerz.” Anti-Semitism’s main sources “have ever been economic, but religious differences have given edge and cover to economic rivalries.”
  • Jewish science and philosophy lived mostly within Islam: Saadia Gaon, the poets of Spain, and Maimonides (1135–1204), “the greatest of medieval Jews,” physician and philosopher of the Guide for the Perplexed, whose work set off a “Maimonidean war” among the faithful. Then the mysticism of the Cabala.
  • The Talmud is “above all a code of ethics,” and the Jews made it “the refuge, solace, and prison of the Jewish soul.”

Volume IV · Book IV, The Dark Ages · Chapters XVIII–XXII

The Dark Ages, 566–1095

Byzantium recovers, the West sinks and struggles up, the Church converts Europe, and feudalism organises it.

  • The Byzantine world (XVIII). Heraclius saves the empire from Persia; the iconoclast emperors fight the images; the Macedonian dynasty brings a “Byzantine renaissance”; the Balkans and the birth of Russia, with Kiev’s four hundred churches.
  • The decline of the West (XIX). Lombard and Norman Italy; Christian Spain’s long crusade against the Moors; and the Franks. Charlemagne’s missi dominici, inspectors sent to curb extortion, were “a Magna Carta for the people, four centuries before England’s Magna Carta for the aristocracy”; he studied furiously and kept writing tablets under his pillow, though he learned to write too late. On Christmas Day 800 Pope Leo III set a crown on his head in St. Peter’s, and the coronation “had results for a thousand years,” making civil authority seem to derive from the Church.
  • The rise of the North (XX). Alfred and the Danes in England; Irish civilisation; Scotland; the Northmen, their sagas and voyages; and Germany “laboriously struggling up to civilization.”
  • Christianity in conflict (XXI). Benedict opened Monte Cassino in 529, the year the Athenian schools closed; Gregory the Great; the Greek Church; the Christian conquest of Europe; the “nadir of the papacy” in the tenth century; reform; the great schism of 1054; and Gregory VII, before whose castle refuge at Canossa the excommunicated Henry IV came to ask absolution in the severe winter of January 1077. Henry later drove him from Rome; the Normans who rescued him sacked the city, and Gregory died in exile at Salerno: “I have loved righteousness and hated iniquity; therefore I die in exile.” His reforms prevailed after him.
  • Feudalism and chivalry (XXII). Feudalism grew as the military organisation “of a harassed agricultural society”: slave, serf and freeman, the feudal Church, the king, feudal law and private war, and chivalry, whose ideal of the knight outlived its practice.

Volume IV · Book V, The Climax of Christianity · Chapters XXIII–XXIV

The Crusades and the economic revolution

“The culminating act of the medieval drama,” and the commercial boom that followed it.

  • Durant’s causes mix religion, Turkish pressure on Byzantium, papal ambition, land hunger, adventure and Italian trade. Plenary indulgences drew multitudes to the First Crusade (1095–99); its disorderly vanguard under Peter the Hermit attacked the Jews of the Rhineland on the way. The main army besieged Antioch for eight months, where the Franks first tasted sugar cane, and reached Jerusalem with some 12,000 fighters in June 1099, to find that the Fatimids had already expelled the Turks they had come to fight. The city fell on 15 July, and its people were massacred.
  • The Latin kingdom lasted less than a century; the Second Crusade failed; Saladin retook Jerusalem in 1187; the Third Crusade of Richard and Philip Augustus won Acre but not the city.
  • The Fourth Crusade (1202–04), diverted by Venice, sacked Christian Constantinople, “the scandal” that, with later failures, ended with Acre’s fall in 1291.
  • “Of their direct and professed purposes the Crusades had failed.” But they spurred Italian and Flemish industry, new banking and credit, and “an economic revolution that heralded and financed the Renaissance.”
  • The economic revolution (XXIV): “Every cultural flowering finds root and nourishment in an expansion of commerce and industry.” Water mills (5,000 in Domesday England) and windmills, money and the fight over interest, merchant and craft guilds, communes that bought or won their charters, better farming learned partly from the Arabs and the monks, and a new class war.

Volume IV · Book V · Chapters XXV–XXVI

The recovery of Europe and pre-Renaissance Italy

Nation by nation, 1095–1300, and the Italian cities that set the stage for the Renaissance.

  • The East and the border states. Byzantium under the Comneni; the Armenians; Russia under the Mongol yoke; the Balkans; Poland, Hungary, Bohemia; Germany after the investiture struggle; Scandinavia.
  • England. William the Conqueror’s “masterly mixture of force, legality, piety, subtlety, and fraud”; Thomas Becket’s murder; Magna Carta; and the beginnings of Parliament.
  • France. Philip Augustus turned a harassed minor state into a power; St. Louis ruled as a saint; Philip the Fair broke the papacy’s political power.
  • Spain and Portugal. The reconquest advanced “as rapidly as the fraternal chaos of the Spanish kings would permit”; King Diniz made Portuguese a literary language.
  • Italy (XXVI). Norman Sicily; the Papal States; Venice triumphant; Lombardy and Genoa; Frederick II, the excommunicated crusader and patron of learning who fought the papacy for Italy; and the rise of Florence, a ferment “that sharpened men’s wits and tongues” and set the stage for the Renaissance.

Volume IV · Book V · Chapters XXVII–XXX

The Church at its summit

Faith, sacraments and canon law; the Inquisition; the friars; and the morals of Christendom.

  • The Roman Catholic Church (XXVII). The faith of the people, the sacraments, prayer and ritual, the worship of the Virgin, canon law, the clergy, the papacy supreme from Gregory VII to Boniface VIII, and the finances of a European “superstate.” “It is the tragedy of things spiritual that they languish if unorganized, and are contaminated by the material needs of their organization.”
  • The early Inquisition (XXVIII). The Albigensian heresy and its crusade; the Inquisition stamped out Catharism and made torture a normal part of legal procedure. Compared with it, the Roman persecution of Christians “was a mild and humane procedure”; Durant ranks it “among the darkest blots on the record of mankind.”
  • Monks and friars (XXIX). St. Bernard; St. Francis of Assisi, born in 1182 to a rich merchant, whose love “overflowed from men to animals, to plants, even to inanimate things,” who received the stigmata, and who, nearly blind, composed in 1224 the “Canticle of the Sun”: “Be Thou praised, my Lord, with all Thy creatures, above all Brother Sun.”
  • St. Dominic (not, Durant insists, the founder of the Inquisition), the nuns, the mystics, and the “tragic pope” Boniface VIII. The Church was “beyond question, the greatest civilizing force in medieval European history.”
  • Morals and manners (XXX). The Christian ethic, marriage, woman (churchmen’s theories “generally hostile,” Christian practice often improving her status), dress, the home, sport, and a “wider gap between moral theory and practice” than in other epochs. Yet the Church rejected the double standard, expanded charity, and for a century gave Europe “one moral law.”

Volume IV · Book V · Chapters XXXI–XXXIII

The arts reborn, the Gothic cathedral, and medieval music

Mosaic, miniature, stained glass and sculpture united in one building.

  • After the Norse and Saracen raids the arts revived: the adornment of life, mosaic, manuscript painting, murals and stained glass, and sculpture from the Romanesque portals to Niccolo Pisano. “The arts were united as never before or since.”
  • Why build so many churches for so small a population? “The population was small, but it believed; it was poor, but it gave”; towns needed to gather everyone under one roof, and communes competed. Then Romanesque, Norman England, and the evolution of Gothic, “a localization and balancing of structural strains, emphasizing vertical lines, ribbed vaults, and pointed forms,” in France, England, Germany, Italy and Spain.
  • “Gothic architecture was the supreme achievement of the medieval soul.” We forget the superstition and squalor and “thank a million forgotten men for redeeming the blood of history with the sacrament of art.”
  • Church and popular music, from chant to polyphony and dance: the instruments alone dispel the notion of “medieval hell-stricken gloom.”

Volume IV · Book V · Chapters XXXIV–XXXVII

Schools, Abelard, Aquinas and medieval science

The universities, the adventure of reason, and a science that began to experiment.

  • The transmission of knowledge (XXXIV). The vernaculars, books and scriptoria, the translators from Arabic and Greek, the cathedral schools, and the universities of Italy, France and England. The universities “ended the mental adolescence of the triumphant barbarians.”
  • Abelard (XXXV). The most celebrated teacher in Paris became, about 1117, tutor to Heloise, the sixteen-year-old niece of the canon Fulbert, who could already converse in Latin and was studying Hebrew; “under the pretext of study we spent our hours in the happiness of love.” Their secret marriage, Fulbert’s revenge, their parting into monastery and convent, Abelard’s condemnation as a rationalist at St. Bernard’s urging, the letters of Heloise, and the lovers’ reburial together in Paris in 1817.
  • The adventure of reason (XXXVI). The school of Chartres, Aristotle arriving in Paris, the freethinkers, the development of Scholasticism, Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas, who argued that God’s existence “can be proved by natural reason” from motion, causation, contingency, degrees of perfection and the order of the world, and whose system many contemporaries took as “a monstrous accumulation of pagan reasonings.” Scholasticism, later an obstacle, “was in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries a revolutionary advance.”
  • Christian science (XXXVII). A magical environment, the mathematical revolution of Hindu-Arabic numerals, geology, physics, medicine’s revival, Albertus Magnus, and Roger Bacon (c. 1214–92), “the most famous of medieval scientists.”
  • Durant refuses to make Bacon “a lone originator … crying out in the scholastic wilderness”: he inherited an Oxford tradition from Grosseteste, whom he ranked with Aristotle, and from Islamic science, and he planned a one-man encyclopedia of grammar, mathematics, natural and experimental science. His own motto was humility: “I have learned more important truths from men of humble station than from all the famous doctors.” The Church, Durant adds, allowed professors considerable latitude, and the friars made “no known objection” to the science of Albertus or Bacon.

Volume IV · Book V · Chapters XXXVIII–XXXIX and Epilogue

The Age of Romance, Dante, and the medieval legacy

Goliards, troubadours and romances, then the poem that closes the age.

  • The Age of Romance (XXXVIII). “Every age is an age of romance, for men cannot live by bread alone.” The Latin revival, the student songs of “wine, woman, and song” and the Carmina Burana, the rebirth of drama in the Church, the epics and sagas, the troubadours and minnesingers, the Arthurian romances, and the satire of Jean de Meung that left “the soul of man proud and tottering on the brink of reason.”
  • Dante (XXXIX). The Sicilian and Tuscan poets before him; Dante and Beatrice, “the most famous of all love affairs,” begun when both were nine, told in the Vita nuova; the poet in Florentine politics and exile; the Divine Comedy, Hell, Purgatory and Heaven. Dante achieves “sublimity,” a “half-barbaric force that foreshadows Michelangelo,” and all Europe rang with the story of the exile “who had gone to hell, and had returned, and had never smiled again.”

Epilogue: the medieval legacy

  • Evil as well as good: “modernity is a cloak put upon medievalism, which secretly remains,” and the Inquisition “made torture a recognized part of legal procedure.”
  • Religion above all; then law, the jury, habeas corpus, charters and parliaments; the conquest of the wilderness and the end of slavery in most of Western Europe; the guilds; the gentleman and chivalry; the universities and the vernacular languages; eyeglasses, the compass, the pendulum clock and distilled alcohol; and, next to moral discipline, the cathedrals.
  • “We shall never do justice to the Middle Ages until we see the Italian Renaissance not as their repudiation but as their fulfillment.”
  • A forecast in passing: “our age of coal and oil and sooty slums may some day be accounted medieval by an era of cleaner power and more gracious life.”

“The men of the Middle Ages were the victims of barbarism, then the conquerors of barbarism, then the creators of a new civilization.”Volume IV, Epilogue

Reading Durant

Forecasts, dates, critics and use

Checked against what happened

What held up

Durant wrote about the past with one eye on his own decade, and he made forecasts that can now be checked. Our verdicts:

Held

Japan would go to war Vol. I, 1935

“By every historical precedent the next act will be war.” A section marked “written in 1934” asks “Must America fight Japan?” and answers from rivalry for Asian markets: the loser in economic competition, “if it is stronger in resources and armament, has made war upon its enemy.” Japan invaded China in 1937 and attacked the United States in 1941.

Partly

Japan against Russia Vol. I, 1935

He expected Japan, once China was won, to strike at the Soviet Far East, and Russia to prepare “for the irrepressible conflict.” The two fought on the Manchurian border in 1938–39, and the Soviet Union attacked Japan in August 1945; but Japan’s main war was with the United States.

Held

India’s industrial future Vol. I, 1935

“India, too, will have her capitalism and her socialism, her millionaires and her slums.” Independent India had both, in that order, within two generations.

Partly

Roads and engines will end China’s famines Vol. I, 1935

When the gasoline engine had bound the villages together, he wrote, it would accomplish “the end of famine.” The worst famine in Chinese history came in 1959–61, caused by policy rather than transport; since then famine has in fact disappeared from China.

Held

The next centre on the Pacific Vol. II, 1939

Civilisations grew first along rivers, then around the Mediterranean, then on the Atlantic, “and … the fourth may appear on the shores of the Pacific.” Eighty years later the Pacific rim, from China and Japan to California, is the world’s economic centre of gravity.

Partly

A weary West returns to faith Vol. I, 1935

He imagined the East taking up science and industry just as the West, after “chaotic revolution or war,” went back “beaten, weary and starving, to the soil,” to forge “another mystic faith.” The first half came true; the West fought its war but did not go back to the soil.

Held

Islam led the world, 700–1200 Vol. IV, 1950

In 1950 this was a corrective aimed at Western readers. The “golden age of Islam” is now the textbook view, though historians such as George Saliba (2007) argue that Islamic science kept advancing well after 1200.

Partly

Christianity grew because Rome was dying Vol. III, 1944

The sociologist Rodney Stark (1996) modelled Christian growth at about 40 per cent a decade through family networks and care of the sick in epidemics, a steady rate that did not need imperial decline; but Durant’s point that plagues and insecurity helped the faith is part of Stark’s own account.

Missed

Rome fell to “barbarian multiplication within” Vol. III, 1944

His “more fundamental” cause, falling native birth rates and “Oriental strains physically inferior” to the Roman type, belongs to the eugenic thinking of his youth and has no support today. Modern accounts stress the fiscal and military state, civil wars and the pressure of invasion.

Partly

Coal and oil will look medieval Vol. IV, 1950

“Our age of coal and oil and sooty slums may some day be accounted medieval by an era of cleaner power and more gracious life.” The transition he imagined has begun and is far from finished.

Missed

His own timetable Vols. I and IV

In 1935 he planned five parts ending in “our own times”; in 1950 he expected to reach the present by 1960. The series took forty years, ran to eleven volumes and stopped in 1815.

Before radiocarbon and Linear B

What has dated

Volumes I and II were written before radiocarbon dating (1949) and before Michael Ventris deciphered Linear B (1952). Many of Durant’s early dates follow the “high” chronologies of Breasted and his contemporaries and have since come down by centuries.

Race, blood and “strains”

Volume I insists that “there are no racial conditions to civilization.” A footnote on the same page adds that a nation may be advanced or retarded by breeding from “the biologically (not racially) worse or better strains,” and Volume III’s epilogue explains Rome’s fall partly by the mixing of the Italian population with “Oriental strains physically inferior,” and by Germans “physically and morally superior” to the natives. The earlier summary on this page called his conclusions “consistently anti-racist”; they are not consistent, and the eugenic passages have aged worst of anything in the four volumes.

“The white man’s life”

Volume IV’s preface describes the series as a study of “the white man’s life,” and Volume III speaks of “half the white man’s world.” Yet the same books give India, China, Islam and the Jews more respectful space than any comparable popular history of their time, and Volume IV ends its Islamic Book by declaring that no student of civilisation “can be a bigot of race or creed.” Both things are true of Durant.

Great men, and missing lives

The narrative moves through kings, prophets and geniuses. Ordinary work, women’s lives and material conditions get real attention by the standards of 1940 (every culture has its economy, family and morals), and thin attention by ours.

Sources and footnotes

The notes are numerous but mostly to secondary works of the 1900s–1930s, and Durant relies on ancient figures (Herodotus’s hundred thousand pyramid builders, Xerxes’ 1,800,000 men) with only occasional warning. Read for the shape and the judgments; check specific numbers against something current.

Disputes

Where it’s argued with

Can one man write it all?

Durant (1935) answered in advance: synthesis is “a brave stupidity,” but specialism leaves no one to see the whole. Professional historians mostly ignored the series or treated it as popularisation; the closest academic equivalents, Braudel’s “total history” (1949) and later global histories, were written by teams or over narrower spans. The question the series still poses is whether understanding needs the whole picture more than it needs the footnotes.

Why Rome fell

Gibbon (1776–89) blamed Christianity and the barbarians; Rostovtzeff (1926) a war between army and cities; Durant (1944) a list headed by biology and morals, with barbarians last. Peter Brown (1971) and Walter Goffart (1980) recast the period as transformation and accommodation; Peter Heather and Bryan Ward-Perkins (both 2005) insisted on a real and violent collapse. Durant’s sentence about destruction from within remains the most quoted line on the subject.

Did Jesus exist?

Durant (1944) reviews two centuries of criticism from Reimarus and Strauss to the “Christ-myth” writers Arthur Drews, W. B. Smith and J. M. Robertson, and concludes that the Gospels’ embarrassing details prove a real man. Scholarship since has overwhelmingly agreed on the existence, while arguing, as Durant did, about almost everything else.

The Muslim conquest of India

“Probably the bloodiest story in history” (1935) is the most quoted sentence in all eleven volumes, often in modern Indian political argument. Durant drew his figures from medieval chronicles that boasted of slaughter; Richard Eaton (2000) and others read those boasts as rhetoric and count far fewer destructions. The same Durant praises Akbar, the Taj Mahal and Moghul art.

How dark were the Dark Ages?

Durant (1950) ends Volume IV by seeing the Renaissance “not as their repudiation but as their fulfillment,” in line with Charles Homer Haskins’s Renaissance of the Twelfth Century (1927). Most medievalists now go further and drop “Dark Ages” altogether; Durant kept the term for 566–1095 while filling those centuries with Alfred, Charlemagne, Benedict and the Byzantine renaissance.

Why Islam’s lead ended

Durant (1950) points to the Mongols, the victory of orthodoxy over philosophy after al-Ghazali, and exhaustion by the Crusades. Later historians such as George Saliba (2007) dispute the timing, pointing to Islamic astronomy and medicine flourishing for centuries after 1200; others look to shifting trade routes.

Terms

Glossary

Integral (synthetic) history
Durant’s method: every phase of a culture, from economy to art, in one narrative.
Civilisation
“Social order promoting cultural creation,” made of economic provision, political organisation, moral traditions and the pursuit of knowledge and the arts.
Envoi
The closing essay of Volume I that totals what the West took from the East.
Upanishads
Philosophical dialogues ending the Vedas, teaching the identity of the self (Atman) with the world-soul (Brahman).
Karma and dharma
The moral law of cause and rebirth, and the duty proper to one’s caste.
Tao
The “Way” of Lao-tze: nature’s order, followed by not striving.
Polis
The Greek city-state, governed by its citizens.
Helots
The serf population of Laconia on whose labour Sparta’s warrior citizens lived.
Metics
Resident foreigners in Athens, free but without the vote; 28,500 in Durant’s count.
Delian League
The anti-Persian alliance of 478 BCE that became an Athenian empire.
Sophists
Paid teachers of rhetoric and argument in fifth-century Greece, sceptics of tradition.
Hellenistic
The age of Greek culture spread over the East after Alexander, 323–146 BCE in Volume II.
Patricians and plebeians
Rome’s hereditary aristocracy and commoners, whose struggle shaped the Republic.
Latifundia
Great Roman estates worked by slaves, which ruined the small farmer.
Principate
Augustus’s monarchy dressed in republican forms.
Higher Criticism
The historical and textual study of the Bible that Durant surveys before his life of Jesus.
Caliphate
The rule of Mohammed’s successors: Umayyad at Damascus, Abbasid at Baghdad, Umayyad again at Cordova.
Talmud
The Mishna and its commentaries, the law and ethics of rabbinic Judaism.
Feudalism
“The military organization of a harassed agricultural society”: land for service, protection for obedience.
Lay investiture
The appointment of bishops by kings, which Gregory VII fought.
Scholasticism
The medieval method of reasoned argument within faith, from Abelard to Aquinas.
Gothic
“A localization and balancing of structural strains,” with ribbed vaults, pointed arches and flying buttresses.
Troubadours
Poets of courtly love in southern France, whose art Durant traces partly to Moslem Spain.

Using it

Reading order

  1. Start small

    The Lessons of History, the hundred-page distillation of all eleven volumes, summarised on this site, shows whether Durant’s voice suits you.

  2. If you read one volume

    Volume III, Caesar and Christ: the most tightly argued, and its epilogue on why Rome fell is the best-known thing Durant wrote.

  3. If you read two

    Add Volume IV, The Age of Faith, for its two hundred pages on Islam and its chapters on Gothic, Abelard and Aquinas.

  4. Volume II straight through

    The most conventional in coverage and the most enjoyable to read; Chapter XVII on the dramatists is a book in itself.

  5. Volume I by sections

    The Introduction on the elements of civilisation, then India and China. Check its early dates against a current source.

  6. Keep a modern history beside it

    Durant is a map and a set of judgments, not the latest scholarship. The rivals listed above are the easiest correctives.
