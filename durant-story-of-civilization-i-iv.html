The Story of Civilization, I–IV

From the first cities of Sumer to the death of Dante

“Civilization is social order promoting cultural creation.”

Will Durant’s definition, on the first page of the series

Will & Ariel Durant · Volumes I–IV · 1935–1950

The whole book in one breath

Durant set out to do what professional history had stopped attempting: to write the whole of each civilisation at once (its economy, government, morals, religion, science, art and thought) as one narrative. These first four of eleven volumes carry the story from the Palaeolithic to Dante. Volume I argues that Europe is a late heir to far older civilisations in the Near East, India and China. Volume II follows Greece as it invents much of Western intellectual life, then destroys itself. Volume III runs Rome and Christianity side by side until an emperor presides over a council of bishops. Volume IV gives Byzantium, Islam and Latin Europe equal standing as heirs to Rome. Across all four the same shapes recur: order comes first, wealth concentrates until it is redistributed, and only the heritage accumulates.

The method

Integral history

Durant’s premise, stated at the start of Volume I and kept for forty years, is that civilisation is social order promoting cultural creation. Order is the precondition, and creation is the point. Everything he narrates is either a form of that order, a threat to it, or something it made possible.

From the definition he derives four elements, and each volume gives all four their turn. That is why a chapter on Athenian finance can sit beside one on Athenian sculpture without apology. He called the approach integral history: refusing to split a people into departments. No specialist would write this way, but you leave each volume with the texture of a civilisation rather than a list of its kings.

The full series runs to eleven volumes and stops at 1815. A projected twelfth on the nineteenth century was never written. Volumes I–IV are the ancient and medieval half, the four in which Durant is least bound to the European archive and most willing to give Asia, Islam and Judaism their own weight. Ariel Durant worked on the series throughout, and is credited as co-author from Volume VII.

Read consecutively

What recurs across the four volumes

Durant does not call these laws, since the record is too thin for laws. But he returns to them often enough that they work as the series’ theory.

Throughout

Order precedes everything

Nothing is created where life and property are insecure. Civilisations decline because the fiscal and legal basis of order fails first, and anarchy follows.

Egypt · Rome · Han

Wealth concentrates until it is redistributed

By law, as in Solon’s cancellation of debts, or by revolt, as with the Gracchi or China’s Yellow Turbans.

Greece · Qin

Freedom and order trade off

Athens bought creativity with instability. Sparta and Qin bought stability by suppressing what makes a civilisation worth studying.

Throughout

Moral codes adapt, and lag

Hunting, farming and urban economies each have their morality, and each survives into the next age looking like a decline in virtue.

Greece · Rome · Middle Ages

Religion returns when the state fails

Declared finished after the Sophists and Lucretius, it came back each time, supplying meaning and discipline no state managed without.

Throughout

Only the heritage accumulates

Human capacity is flat and the inheritance is not. Teaching, not conquest, is the load-bearing activity of a civilisation.

Vol. I

The West is a debtor

Farming, writing, law, coinage, the alphabet, the arch and the calendar all came from Asia or Africa before Greece.

Vol. II

Freedom made Greece and unmade it

The Greeks invented the proof, tragedy and history, then fought each other to exhaustion because they couldn’t invent a federation.

Vol. III

Rome fell inward first

The barbarians arrived at a structure already hollowed out by debasement, taxes, depopulation and a mercenary army.

Vol. IV

Three heirs to one inheritance

Byzantium, Islam and Latin Europe divided Rome’s legacy, and for five centuries Islam led.

Volume I · 1935

Our Oriental Heritage

I

Egypt and the Near East to the death of Alexander, and India, China and Japan from the beginning to the 1930s.

Argument
The West is a debtor
Halves
Near East · Asia

The title is the thesis. Durant opens the whole series by arguing that Europe is a late province of a much older world. Only after four hundred pages on the Near East does he turn to India, China and Japan, taking each into the twentieth century in a single run.

Volume I · Book One

The Establishment of Civilisation

What must be true before a civilisation can exist at all.

  • The preconditions are mostly not achievements: a climate neither too harsh nor too kind, soil that rewards work, an economic surplus and, above all, security.
  • The move from hunting to farming is the great watershed, but Durant refuses to call it simple progress. The farmer lives shorter, eats worse and works longer than the hunter, and gains property (so theft), boundaries (so war) and storage (so taxes).
  • What the farmer gains is continuity: his grandson can begin where he stopped. Civilisation is justified not by making each life better, which it often didn’t, but by making lives cumulative.
  • Farming also demanded that people defer gratification across a season, a moral revolution before a technical one.

Volume I · The Near East

Sumer to Persia

A relay of empires, each inheriting the last one’s apparatus and adding one thing.

  • Durant gives Sumer the credit popular history tends to give Egypt: the first cities, writing, schools, law codes, arch and vault, and the base-60 arithmetic that survives in our clocks and circles.
  • Babylonia, Assyria, Judea and Persia follow in turn. Durant singles out Cyrus’s policy of religious tolerance as a genuine political invention, one the Greeks failed to copy.
  • Judea’s contribution is ethical monotheism, the idea of one just God, which reaches the West through Christianity and Islam.

Volume I · The Near East

Egypt: the Long Stillness

A civilisation that solved its problems early and then held the solution for three thousand years.

  • Egypt gets Durant’s most affectionate writing and his sharpest criticism. He is dazzled by the engineering and repelled by what it cost.
  • The same gods, script and canon of proportion lasted through more time than separates us from Homer.
  • The pyramids show what a state can do when it captures the whole surplus. The intermediate periods of collapse show what happens when it no longer can, and Durant treats those as the informative parts.
  • He separates the rule of law from the equality of persons: two inventions, thousands of years apart.

Volume I · Asia

India

From the newly excavated Indus cities to Gandhi, in one arc.

  • Mohenjo-daro and Harappa had only just been excavated, and Durant’s excitement at a civilisation suddenly pushed back two thousand years is audible.
  • He runs the subcontinent forward: Vedic religion, the inward turn of the Upanishads, Buddhism and Jainism as answers to the question the Upanishads posed, the Maurya and Gupta empires, the Muslim conquests, the Mughals and the British.
  • Ashoka, who converted to Buddhism after the carnage of his conquest of Kalinga and carved edicts of tolerance across India, is ranked among the greatest rulers in history.
  • The Gupta centuries (about 320–550 CE) are India’s classical age: Kālidāsa’s poetry, the decimal place-value system with zero, Āryabhaṭa’s astronomy. The volume ends in 1935 with a frankly anti-colonial account and a long, admiring portrait of Gandhi.

Volume I · Asia

China and Japan

Durant’s admiration is most open for China.

  • China produced, very early, a moral philosophy without a church, a governing class chosen by examination rather than birth, and inventions (paper, printing, the compass, gunpowder, porcelain, paper money) that reached Europe centuries later and remade it.
  • The Qin unified China on harsh Legalist principles and lasted fifteen years. The Han kept the Qin machinery and gave it Confucian language, and that hybrid governed China for two thousand years.
  • The dynastic cycle has a fiscal engine: tax-exempt great estates strangle the treasury, the same diagnosis Durant gives Egypt and will give Rome.
  • Japan gets the shortest and most ambivalent treatment: admiration for the Heian court that produced The Tale of Genji, for Zen-inflected arts and the long Tokugawa peace, and in 1935, alarm at Japanese militarism.

Volume II · 1939

The Life of Greece

II

Greece from the beginnings, and the Near East from Alexander’s death, to the Roman conquest.

Argument
Freedom created it and destroyed it
Peak
Athens, 480–404 BCE

Published as Europe went to war, and it reads like it. Greece invents much of what the West means by intellectual life, then destroys itself in a twenty-seven-year war because it cannot invent a federation. The Greeks fell not to a better civilisation but to a simpler one, after exhausting themselves.

Volume II

The Aegean Prelude and the Polis

Crete, a lost Dark Age, and the city-state.

  • Written before Linear B was deciphered, the volume treats Minoan Crete as a mysterious, apparently peaceable, strikingly modern-looking culture, destroyed around 1400 BCE by causes Durant can’t name.
  • In the Dark Age that follows, writing disappears for centuries and Greece has to begin again.
  • What emerges is the polis: small, fiercely independent, defined by its citizens rather than a king, and multiplied by colonies around the Mediterranean. A state you can walk across in a day can be governed by its members in person.
  • Sparta and Athens are contrasted without cheap moralising. Athens rested on slavery and tribute, and Spartan women were freer than Athenian ones, but Sparta bought stability by refusing to change and had nothing left when change came.

Volume II

The Great Age

Persia repelled, Athens ascendant, and a democracy that ran by lottery.

  • The Persian Wars matter less as military history than as the source of Greek self-belief. Quarrelling cities twice beat the largest empire on earth and concluded that free men fighting for their own laws beat subjects fighting for a king.
  • Athenian democracy was direct, not representative, and filled most offices by lot, on the theory that any citizen could serve and that elections favour the rich and famous (see the figure below).
  • The classical achievement is concentrated absurdly in one city over about seventy years.

Volume II

The Suicide of Greece

An alliance turns into an empire, and the empire causes the war that ends the age.

  • The Delian League, formed in 478 BCE against Persia, became within a generation an Athenian empire. The treasury moved to Athens in 454, allied tribute paid for the Parthenon, and secession was treated as revolt.
  • Durant is unsparing: the classical achievement was funded, in plain terms, by protection money, and the empire caused the Peloponnesian War.
  • Athens survived plague, invasion and the loss of Pericles. It couldn’t survive its own capacity for a bad decision taken enthusiastically.

Volume II

The Philosophers

One unbroken conversation, two centuries long.

  • Almost every position later philosophy takes was staked out within about two hundred years, in a territory smaller than Portugal, by men who mostly knew one another.
  • From the natural philosophers through the Sophists, Socrates, Plato and Aristotle to the Hellenistic schools, Durant writes as the historian of philosophy he first was.
  • Stoicism is the export that matters most to Rome. It gives an imperial governing class a private ethic compatible with obedience, and supplies vocabulary Christianity will borrow.

Volume II

Alexander and the Hellenistic World

Greek culture becomes portable.

  • Philip of Macedon settled the Greek question by conquest at Chaeronea in 338 BCE. His son carried Greek language, coinage and city-planning to the Indus and died at thirty-two with no arrangement for what followed.
  • Durant’s verdict is split: a destroyer of a great deal, and the accidental founder of the first cosmopolitan culture.
  • The successor kingdoms detached Greek culture from the citizen assembly that produced it and turned it into an administrative language, which made it available to Rome and to Christianity.
  • Hellenistic science, in Alexandria above all, reached heights not regained for centuries, and was left unused rather than destroyed.

Volume III · 1944

Caesar and Christ

III

Roman civilisation and Christianity from their beginnings to AD 325.

Argument
Rome fell inward first
Two subjects
An empire and a faith

The title names a collision. Durant runs two histories side by side, a state that solved the political problem Greece couldn’t and a religion born in a remote province, until in 325 a Roman emperor presides over a council of Christian bishops.

“A great civilization is not conquered from without until it has destroyed itself within.”Volume III, epilogue

Volume III

The Republic

A machine that worked for four centuries, then was broken by its own conquests.

  • The Greek historian Polybius, a hostage in Rome, thought the Romans had accidentally built the mixed constitution philosophers had only described: consuls, Senate and assemblies, each able to block the others.
  • Conquest made Rome rich and destroyed the society that won it. Cheap slaves and provincial grain ruined the small Italian farm, great slave-worked estates spread, the dispossessed crowded into Rome, and the army of smallholders became landless men loyal to their general.
  • The Gracchi tried to break the loop by redistributing public land, and both were killed. Durant marks those deaths, in 133 and 121 BCE, as the moment Roman politics turned lethal.

Volume III

The Revolution and the Principate

From Marius to Augustus, and the golden century after.

  • A century of civil war, Marius, Sulla, Pompey and Caesar, made individuals indispensable, and no clause had been written against that.
  • Caesar gets a long, admiring portrait. Durant thinks his assassination pointless, because the Republic being defended had been dead for two generations.
  • Augustus gave a republic-worshipping aristocracy a monarchy it could vote for, and bought two centuries of peace with a legal fiction no one had to believe and no one could deny.
  • The Antonine century (96–180) is the summit, but the system had no way of reproducing the good emperors who made it.

Volume III

Christ, and the Empire’s Second Government

The historical Jesus, Paul’s universal church, and why Christianity won.

  • Approaching the sources as a historian, Durant concludes that the case against Jesus’s existence asks more of us than the case for it.
  • He separates the founder from the institution. Jesus preached an imminent kingdom to Jews in Galilee. Paul built a universal religion for gentiles, dropped the ritual law and carried it into the Greek cities. Without Paul, Durant says, the movement stays a Jewish sect.
  • Christianity won because it was better organised and more inclusive than its rivals. Constantine’s conversion in 312 ratified a victory already largely won in the eastern cities.

“That a few simple men should in one generation have invented so powerful and appealing a personality, so lofty an ethic and so inspiring a vision of human brotherhood, would be a miracle far more incredible than any recorded in the Gospels.”Volume III, on the historicity of Jesus

Volume III

Why Rome Fell

Not one cause, but one process, and the barbarians came last.

  • Durant refuses a single answer. He offers simultaneous failures, each worsening the others (see the figure below).
  • His emphatic proviso is that none of them is the barbarians, who arrived at a structure already hollow.

Volume IV · 1950

The Age of Faith

IV

Medieval civilisation, Christian, Islamic and Judaic, from Constantine to Dante, AD 325–1300.

Argument
Three heirs, one inheritance
Unusual for 1950
Islam gets ~250 pages

The volume in which Durant is most clearly ahead of his time. Where mid-century surveys treated the Middle Ages as a European interval, he gives roughly equal standing to three civilisations dividing Rome’s inheritance, and for most of the period awards the lead to Islam.

Volume IV

Byzantium and Islam

The eastern empire preserved; the Islamic world led.

  • Byzantium preserved the Greek texts, held the eastern frontier for eight centuries, and under Justinian codified Roman law. Rediscovered at Bologna in the eleventh century, that code became the basis of most continental European law.
  • Islam gets about a quarter of a very long book, written in the tone of a man correcting an omission. Between roughly 700 and 1200, Durant judges the Islamic world the leading civilisation on earth.
  • Córdoba under the Umayyads had paved, lit streets, public baths and a royal library whose catalogue alone ran to forty-four volumes, when Paris had none of these.
  • Why the lead ended is more tentative: the Mongol destruction, the closing of independent reasoning in theology, shifting trade routes. Historians still dispute the weightings.

Volume IV

The West Rebuilds

Manor and Church, then a food surplus that sets everything moving.

  • With the Roman state gone, two institutions organised Western life: the manor, which solved food, and the Church, which ran courts, schools, hospitals, records, charity and the calendar, and supplied the only literate class.
  • Feudalism was never designed. It is the residue of one exchange repeated at every level: land for service, protection for obedience.
  • Durant dates the twelfth-century revival in law, architecture and philosophy to a food surplus produced by unglamorous inventions in ploughing, harnessing and crop rotation.

Volume IV

The Crusades

Totted up, the ledger is bleak.

  • Durant grants the sincerity of the motive, notes the mix of piety, land-hunger and papal politics, and totals the results: two centuries, enormous loss of life, and a lasting poisoning of relations between three religions.
  • The gains were territory held for less than a century, and knowledge that was already reaching Europe by the quieter routes through Spain and Sicily.
  • The sack of Constantinople in 1204, by a crusade that never reached a Muslim army, is where the enterprise convicted itself.

Volume IV

The Medieval Mind

Faith builds the instrument that will eventually dissolve it.

  • The age of faith produced, from its own pressures, a technique of systematic argument, developed to defend doctrine, that turned out to work on anything.
  • Aristotle returned through Arabic translations in Spain and through Norman Sicily, where Arabic, Greek and Latin were all court languages.
  • Aquinas’s settlement is generous to reason because he is sure it will agree with faith. Within three centuries others use the same licence without that confidence.
  • Durant reads the Gothic cathedral like the Summa: a solution engineered under constraint, where the aesthetic and the structural are the same fact.
  • The volume closes with Dante: vernacular, putting popes in Hell on his own authority, scholastic in structure and personal in feeling. The age of faith completes itself and hands over to something new.

Context

What has dated

The archaeology has moved on

Volume II predates the decipherment of Linear B, so its Mycenaeans aren’t yet Greeks. Volume I predates radiocarbon dating and most of what is now known about the Indus cities and Anatolia. Read for the shape, and check specific dates against something current.

The race language of the 1930s

Volume I uses a vocabulary of migrating races and national temperaments that was standard then and isn’t now. Durant’s conclusions are consistently anti-racist, since he explains cultural difference by circumstance rather than blood. The Indo-European migration model has since been substantially revised.

A sentence about India to know in context

Durant calls the Muslim conquest of India “probably the bloodiest story in history.” The line is often quoted in polemics. It sits in a volume unusually generous to Islamic civilisation, and it rests on chronicle figures modern historians treat as unreliable.

Great men do a lot of work

The narrative moves through Hammurabi, Pericles, Caesar, Muhammad and Aquinas. Ordinary lives, women’s lives and material history get real attention by the standards of 1940, and thin attention by ours.

Few footnotes

References are sparse and often to superseded secondary works. This is popular synthesis by a philosopher, a superb map and a poor citation, and Durant said as much.

Terms

Glossary

Integral history
Durant’s method of narrating a civilisation’s economy, politics, morals, religion and arts together.
Polis
The Greek city-state, governed by its citizens.
Delian League
The anti-Persian alliance that became an Athenian empire.
Latifundium
A great slave-worked Roman estate that displaced small farms.
Principate
Augustus’s monarchy dressed as a restored republic.
Dynastic cycle
The Chinese pattern of rise, fiscal decay and collapse, which Durant finds elsewhere too.
Corpus Juris Civilis
Justinian’s codification of Roman law, the basis of continental legal systems.
Scholasticism
The medieval method of systematic argument, used to reconcile reason and faith.
Heritage
The accumulated knowledge and technique a civilisation passes on, the one thing that reliably grows.

Using it

Reading order

  1. If you read one

    Volume III, Caesar and Christ: the most tightly argued and the most self-contained.

  2. If you read two

    Add Volume IV, The Age of Faith: the most original, on the subject readers know least.

  3. Volume I

    Best sampled: the opening on the establishment of civilisation, then India and China. The Near Eastern middle has the most dated archaeology.

  4. Volume II

    The most conventional in coverage and the most enjoyable straight through.

  5. Before or after

    The Lessons of History, the hundred-page distillation of all eleven volumes, summarised on this site.

Read alongside

  • The Lessons of History, summarised on this site: the distillation.
  • Livy, The History of Rome, summarised on this site: the Republic in its own historian’s voice.
  • Edward Gibbon, The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire (1776–89): the account Volume III argues with.
  • Guns, Germs, and Steel, summarised on this site: the geographic account of why civilisations diverged.
