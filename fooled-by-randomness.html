Fooled by Randomness

The Hidden Role of Chance in Life and in the Markets

Nassim Nicholas Taleb · 2001, second edition 2004

The whole book in one breath

“This book is about luck disguised and perceived as non-luck (that is, skills) and, more generally, randomness disguised and perceived as non-randomness.” We see one history, the one that happened, and read skill, cause and meaning into it. Taleb, an options trader who makes his living from rare events, argues that a result should be judged against all the histories that could have happened instead. A $10 million prize won at Russian roulette is not worth the same as $10 million earned drilling teeth. We see the survivors and never the graveyard, so a crowd of coin-flippers produces a few “geniuses”. The more often we look, the more of what we see is noise. We confuse how likely something is with how much it would cost, and our minds, built for the savannah, can’t hold probabilities at all. Rare events decide the long run: the trader who wins small and steadily is often carrying a hidden blow-up. Taleb doesn’t think reason can cure any of this, including in himself. His answer is to tie himself to the mast: rules that keep him away from ruin, a Popperian readiness to be wrong, silence in place of news, and Stoic dignity when luck turns.

The author

A trader who made his living from other people’s blind spots

Nassim Nicholas Taleb was born in 1960 in Amioun, in the Greek Orthodox north of Lebanon. His grandfather and great-grandfather had both been deputy prime ministers, and the family’s wealth largely went in the Lebanese civil war, which he lived through as a teenager. He took an MBA at Wharton, spent about two decades trading options in New York, London and on the floor in Chicago, wrote a technical book, Dynamic Hedging (1997), and earned a doctorate at the Université Paris-Dauphine under Hélyette Geman.

His trading style was the reverse of most of his colleagues’. He accepted many small losses in exchange for large payoffs from rare, violent market moves. The stock market crash of 1987, he writes, “made me as a trader”. When he wrote this book he was running Empirica Capital, his own “crisis-hunting” fund in the woods outside Greenwich, Connecticut, and teaching at New York University’s Courant Institute.

The book was first published by Texere in 2001, and revised as a second edition in 2004, which is the text summarised here. Taleb set himself rules for writing it: no library research, nothing he had not witnessed or worked out himself, and no quotation that did not come naturally from a writer he knew well. It is a set of essays built around composite characters from the trading floor, the classics he reads, and his own weaknesses. It became the first volume of what he calls the Incerto, followed by The Black Swan (2007), The Bed of Procrustes (2010), Antifragile (2012) and Skin in the Game (2018). Many of the ideas that made those books famous first appear here in rougher form.

“I am not capable of avoiding being the fool of randomness; what I can do is confine it to where it brings some aesthetic gratification.”Preface

The argument

We keep mistaking one column for the other

The Prologue sets out “Table P.1, Table of Confusion”, pairs of things people routinely mistake for each other: the left column taken for the right. “The best way to summarize the major thesis of this book is that it addresses situations (many of them tragicomical) where the left column is mistaken for the right one.” Here is the whole table.

  • Why not worry about the opposite error? Mistaking real patterns for noise is less common, since we already read messages in palms and coffee grounds, and less costly: “bad information is worse than no information at all.”
  • Why markets? Trading is where Taleb works, and where the confusion is greatest and most pernicious. A strategy, an entrepreneur or a trader can look excellent “only to realize that 99.9% of their past performance is attributable to chance, and chance alone.”
  • He isn’t against science. Like the cartoon bourgeois Monsieur Prudhomme, whose sword would defend the Republic and attack it if it strayed, the book defends science and attacks scientists who stray, especially those who lack a feel for standard error.
  • He counts himself among the fooled. The last part of the book concludes that “perhaps ridding ourselves of our humanity is not in the works; we need tricks, not some grandiose moralizing help.”

If you remember nothing else

Twelve ideas that carry the book

Part I

What came by luck can go by luck

Solon told Croesus to call no man happy until his end. Taleb’s version: a result tells you little until you know how much randomness produced it.

Ch. 1–2

Judge by the histories that didn’t happen

A decision is only as good as the whole range of outcomes it could have produced. The Russian-roulette winner is rich, not wise.

Ch. 3

The more often you look, the more noise you see

An excellent strategy is up in 93% of years and only 50.02% of seconds. Check it every minute and you mostly feel pain.

Ch. 3

For an idea, age is beauty

An idea that has survived many cycles has been filtered. News hasn’t. When in doubt, reject the new.

Ch. 5

The market selects the most fragile

At any moment the richest traders are those best fitted to the last cycle, often the worst traders for the next one.

Ch. 6

Probability isn’t payoff

Taleb could think the market 70% likely to rise and still be short, because a fall would be ten times larger. You are paid in dollars, not in how often you are right.

Ch. 7

Data can refute but never prove

Four thousand white swans don’t prove all swans are white. Use statistics to make bets, never to manage the risk of ruin.

Ch. 8–9

Count the monkeys

From 10,000 coin-flipping managers, 313 will have five perfect years. A track record means nothing without the size of the starting population.

Ch. 10

Life is unfair in a nonlinear way

A small head start, often pure chance, compounds through network effects into winner-take-all outcomes, from actors to QWERTY to Microsoft.

Ch. 11

We are probability blind

We can picture Paris or the Bahamas, never 50% of each. Doctors guess 95% where the answer is 2%.

Ch. 13

Don’t marry your ideas

Soros could reverse his view overnight without embarrassment. Most people, and most scientists, can’t: science moves “from funeral to funeral”.

Part III

Wax in the ears, dignity at the end

You can’t reason your way out of your wiring, so use tricks: turn the sound off, don’t read the reviews, and meet bad luck like Cavafy’s Antony.

Preface and prologue

Mosques in the clouds

  • The book is “the synthesis of, on one hand, the no-nonsense mathematical trader (self-styled ‘practitioner of uncertainty’) … and, on the other, the aesthetically obsessed, literature-loving human being willing to be fooled by any form of nonsense that is polished, refined, original, and tasteful.”
  • He wrote it as if talking at dinner with a curious cardiologist, his friend Jacques Merab. The real book, he says, started in an all-night conversation in the summer of 1987 with his friend Jamil Baz about “new” and “old” money. Parts were argued out on Wednesday nights at the bar of the Odeon in Tribeca, after his Courant Institute class.
  • A lucky fool is “a person who benefited from a disproportionate share of luck but attributes his success to some other, generally very precise, reason.” The confusion is endemic in politics, where a president talks about the jobs “he” created, and in business, where a company’s success is put down to the new president “at the helm”.
  • We are still close to our savannah ancestors. A tribesman scratches his nose, rain falls, and he develops a rain ritual of nose-scratching. We link prosperity to a Federal Reserve rate cut.
  • The literary mind is prone to finding meaning in noise, and does little harm, since few claim art is a tool for finding the truth. “Symbolism is the child of our inability and unwillingness to accept randomness.” Arthur Rimbaud “saw mosques in the clouds”, went to Abyssinia, gave up poetry at 19, lost a leg to gangrene and died anonymously in a Marseilles hospital in his thirties. Paul Valéry was surprised to hear commentators find meanings in his poems that had escaped him.
  • The financial statistician makes the same mistake with data, detecting “regularities” and “anomalies” in plain random series, and the harm there is real. “Probability theory is a young arrival in mathematics; probability applied to practice is almost nonexistent as a discipline.”
  • The three parts: Solon’s warning and visible and invisible histories; a collection of probability biases Taleb has met and suffered from; and the conclusion that we need tricks, from the elders, not moralising.

Part I

Solon’s Warning: Skewness, Asymmetry, Induction

Croesus, King of Lydia, was the richest man of his time, and was irked when the visiting Athenian lawgiver Solon showed no admiration. Asked whether he had known a happier man, Solon named a man who had lived nobly and died in battle, and went on in that vein until Croesus asked outright. Solon answered that we should not “admire a man’s happiness that may yet, in course of time, suffer change … and him only to whom the divinity has [guaranteed] continued happiness until the end we may call happy.”

Later, defeated by the Persian king Cyrus and about to be burned alive, Croesus called out “Solon, you were right”, and Cyrus, impressed, spared him. Taleb admits the meeting is legend; the two men could not have met. Yogi Berra’s “it ain’t over until it’s over” says the same and has “an advantage of being true”. Solon grasped three problems that run through Part I: what came with luck can be taken away by luck; the problem of induction, which Taleb calls the black swan or the rare event; and skewness: “it does not matter how frequently something succeeds if failure is too costly to bear.”

Chapter 1

If You’re So Rich Why Aren’t You So Smart?

Two neighbours on opposite sides of the street show how a scoreboard at one point in time says little about skill.

An illustration of the effect of randomness on social pecking order and jealousy, through two characters of opposite attitudes. On the concealed rare event. How things in modern life may change rather rapidly, except, perhaps, in dentistry.The book’s own summary

Nero Tulip

  • Hit by lightning. One spring day, visiting the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, Nero saw a red convertible Porsche screech to a halt at the entrance. A flushed, athletic man in his thirties leapt out and ran up the steps “as if he were chased by a tiger”, leaving it double-parked with the engine running, until a bored clerk in a yellow jacket strolled down and parked it. It was a coup de foudre. “This is for me!” Academia meant a silent office with rude secretaries; business meant slow thinkers who speak in full sentences.
  • Temporary sanity. Nero swings between the manners of a church historian and the abusive intensity of a pit trader; he can commit hundreds of millions without a blink and then agonise between two appetisers. He took ancient literature and mathematics at Cambridge, started a statistics doctorate at Chicago, switched to philosophy in “a moment of temporary sanity”, and wrote a thesis on statistical inference in the social sciences, “indistinguishable from a thesis in mathematical statistics — it was just a bit more thoughtful (and twice as long).” He left not because philosophy can’t feed its man, but because it “cannot entertain its man”.
  • He trained in the Chicago pits with an eccentric local in exchange for solving his equations, moved “upstairs” to a desk in New York, had an early moment of glory in quantitative products, and spent a couple of years shuttling to London in expensive suits, courted with guaranteed bonuses. Then he retreated into anonymity. He is allergic to “game plan”, “bottom line” and “our mission”, and settled into proprietary trading, trading the firm’s own money for 7–12% of the profits, with the freedom of self-employment.
  • Modus operandi. He has had good years and less good ones, but virtually no truly bad ones. His income runs from $300,000 to $2.5 million at the peak, about $1 million on average, of which he saves about $500,000 a year after tax. He exits trades after a predetermined loss, never sells “naked options”, and never risks more than about $1 million, whatever the odds. “I love taking small losses … I just need my winners to be large.” Asked why, he credits Stevo, the Chicago trader who taught him, “the most chicken of them all”; the real reason is his training in probability and his scepticism.
  • His goal is not maximum profit but never losing the entertaining money machine: every time his risk rises, he pictures the quiet university hallway and the bad coffee. “I am shooting for longevity.” To blow up “does not just mean to lose money; it means to lose more money than one ever expected, to the point of being thrown out of the business”, like a doctor losing his licence.
  • When Nero had a flat year in 1993 and was made unwelcome, he moved to an identical job at a friendlier firm. In the fall of 1994 the rivals who had been competing for the performance award “blew up in unison” in the worldwide bond crash that followed the Federal Reserve’s tightening. “This business has a high mortality rate.”
  • He keeps his own savings in Treasury bonds and cannot bring himself to own a stock, so he missed the great bull market. His wealth depends on his cash earnings, not on the market’s mood, so he never has to worry about a bear market. No work ethics: “Trading forces someone to think hard; those who merely work hard generally lose their focus”, and drown in noise. At 39, after 14 years in the business, he has several million dollars in Treasuries, has gone back and finished the statistics doctorate too, and teaches a half-semester seminar on the history of probabilistic thinking at NYU.
  • There are always secrets. A sharp observer might notice “a measure of suspicious exuberance, an unnatural drive” in Nero. He harbours a secret, revealed in Chapter 11.

John the high-yield trader

  • Across the street, through most of the 1990s, lived John, with a much larger house, two top-of-the-line German cars and two convertibles, one a collectible Ferrari. Nero has driven the same VW cabriolet for almost a decade. A brief conversation revealed that John had “the intellectual depth and sharpness of mind of an aerobics instructor (though not the physique)”.
  • Nero’s wife felt condescended to by John’s, who read People at the health club, owned a “library” of untouched leather-bound books, and couldn’t have said which continent the Seychelles were on. The only dark side of trading, Nero says, is watching money showered on people who are suddenly taught that Vivaldi’s Four Seasons is “refined” music. “Words and reason became ineffectual in front of an oversized diamond, a monstrous house, and a sports car collection.”
  • An overpaid hick. John was about five years younger than Nero and made at least ten times as much. “Intellectual contempt does not control personal envy.” Nero walks through Paris and Venice with a Nobel-calibre scientist who seeks his conversation, and a famous billionaire speculator calls him for his views, yet he found himself craving the respect of a man with a “Noo-Joyzy” accent. He tried Proust’s Swann, at ease with the Prince of Wales but anxious among the social-climbing Verdurins, and Kahneman and Tversky’s finding that most people prefer $70,000 when others make $60,000 to $80,000 when others make $90,000. “Economics, schmeconomics, it is all pecking order.”
  • Nero’s hope was that John was merely lucky and exposed to a hidden risk of blowing up that his short experience hid from him. The high-yield market “resembles a nap on a railway track. One afternoon, the surprise train would run you over.” Nero had seen option sellers blow up in 1987, 1989, 1992 and 1998, walked off the floor by oversized security men. Nero’s smaller house, with its 4,000 books, is his own; every loss is limited; “his trader’s dignity will never, never, be threatened.” John, for his part, thought prop traders like Nero were “passé”.
  • The red-hot summer. One morning in September 1998, Nero saw John in his front yard smoking a cigarette, out of his suit, his swagger gone. He had been fired and had lost almost everything (the details come in Chapter 5). Nero felt ashamed of his schadenfreude, which is also said to bring bad luck. His real elation was that his method now had credibility: he could raise money on a track record that such an event could never touch, and a repeat would pay him handsomely.

Serotonin and the dentist

  • “At any point in time, a large section of businessmen with outstanding track records will be no better than randomly thrown darts”, and because of a peculiar bias, “the least-skilled businessmen” will often be the richest. Lucky fools, by definition, don’t know they are lucky fools.
  • Success changes body chemistry. Monkeys injected with serotonin rise in the pecking order, which raises their serotonin further, until a kick from randomness starts the reverse cycle. A winning trader walks upright and talks more. Taleb’s father advised: “never ask a man if he is from Sparta: if he were, he would have let you know such an important fact — and if he were not, you could hurt his feelings.” A Chicago cab driver told Taleb that traders who are winning “get all puffed up”.
  • By a probabilistic accounting, Nero is very rich: “on the average of lives he could have led”, very few would be disastrous. “In expectation, a dentist is considerably richer than the rock musician who is driven in a pink Rolls Royce”, because “one cannot consider a profession without taking into account the average of the people who enter it, not the sample of those who have succeeded in it.”
  • Compare a janitor who won the New Jersey lottery with his neighbour, a dentist who has drilled teeth eight hours a day for 35 years. Relive their lives a million times: the dentist ranges from Park Avenue teeth to a half-deserted trailer town in the Catskills; the janitor wins the lottery in one life in a million.

Chapter 2

A Bizarre Accounting Method

Count the outcomes that didn’t happen. The quality of a decision can’t be judged by its result alone.

On alternative histories, a probabilistic view of the world, intellectual fraud, and the randomness wisdom of a Frenchman with steady bathing habits … On the difference between correctness and intelligibility.The book’s own summary

Alternative histories and Russian roulette

  • Performance in war, politics, medicine or investing should be judged “by the costs of the alternative”, the alternative histories in which things went differently. The point is usually made only by people who failed.
  • An eccentric, bored tycoon offers you $10 million to play Russian roulette: one bullet, six chambers, six equally likely histories. Five make you rich; one produces “a statistic, that is, an obituary with an embarrassing (but certainly original) cause of death”. Only one history is observed, and a journalist would admire the winner. The public sees the wealth, never the source, which Taleb calls the generator.
  • If a 25-year-old played once a year, he would very probably not see 50. But among thousands of such players there would be a handful of extremely rich survivors “(and a very large cemetery)”.
  • The example isn’t abstract for Taleb: he lost a comrade to the “game” during the Lebanese war, in his teens. Graham Greene claimed he once tried it to relieve the boredom of his childhood, “making me shiver at the thought that I had at least a one in six probability of having been without his novels.”
  • “$10 million earned through Russian roulette does not have the same value as $10 million earned through the diligent and artful practice of dentistry.” To an accountant, or a neighbour, they are identical. “Mathematics is not just a ‘numbers game’, it is a way of thinking … probability is a qualitative subject.”
  • Reality is more vicious. Its revolver has hundreds or thousands of chambers, so after a few dozen pulls you forget the bullet. And you can’t see the barrel: “One is thus capable of unwittingly playing Russian roulette — and calling it by some alternative ‘low risk’ name.”

Two bosses and two kinds of trader

  • Smooth peer relations. Don’t discuss any of this with an accountant: “For an accountant a number is a number.” If he were interested in probability he would be in a more introspective profession, and would make a costly mistake on your tax return. People with some scientific training tend to go “the extra mile” and look at the generator, especially those who left science because they couldn’t stay narrow; a few, trained in quantum mechanics, go too far and see only the alternative histories, ignoring the one that happened. Taleb lost a coin toss for dinner at the Odeon to Lauren R., a trader reading the draft, who stopped mid-thanks: “Reading your book you would say that I paid for half of it probabilistically”.
  • People fall between two poles: those who never accept randomness and those tortured by it. In the 1980s trading rooms were full of MBAs with a “business orientation”, “flat as a pancake”. When exotic options with counterintuitive payoffs arrived, “they dropped like flies”. Salvation via Aeroflot: in the 1990s came the scientists, mostly physicists, with Russian, French, Chinese and Indian accents, in that order; “every plane from Moscow had at least its back row full of Russian mathematical physicists en route to Wall Street (they lacked the street smarts to get good seats).” By the late 1990s you could hire someone trained by a world-class scientist for half the price of an MBA. MBAs, told by Wharton’s career counsellor that chess “sounds intelligent and strategic”, listed it on their résumés; Taleb’s desk pulled a chess set from a drawer: “Yuri will have a word with you”. The scientists failed only slightly less often, for the opposite reason: some had “the judgment (and social graces) of a door knob”, understanding “the letter but not the spirit of the math”. But lunch got longer and better than the 1980s talk of FASB standards.
  • Solon visits Regine’s night club. Taleb had two bosses, opposite in every trait. Kenny was a suburban family man who coached soccer on Saturdays, wore dark suits with flashy Hermès equestrian ties, had athletic good looks, listened superbly, won the chairman’s confidence and rose fast without knowing derivatives, his firm’s speciality. He admired traders who had done well in the 1993 European bond rally, whom Taleb called “random gunslingers”. Taleb tried to explain survivorship bias, in vain. They have all since left “to pursue other interests”, and so has Kenny, forced out after climbing high. He was “a perfect time bomb, ticking away”.
  • Jean-Patrice, the other, was a moody, explosive Frenchman in blue shirts, plaid jackets and gaudy pocket squares, who rarely came in before noon, seemed irresistible to women (perhaps as a New York Frenchman “with steady bathing habits”), called at three in the morning from Regine’s nightclub to discuss details of Taleb’s risk, once held a meeting in an unmarked Paris club between two scantily dressed women, and was “one of the very rare people who have the guts to care only about the generator, entirely oblivious of the results”. He made Taleb plan for a plane crashing into the office building and taught him to hunt for invisible risks of blow-up. About a decade later he turned up, smiling at the back of the room, at Taleb’s doctoral defence. His career fared worse than Kenny’s, which taught Taleb to “beware of mature financial institutions”. “The cemetery of markets is disproportionately well stocked with the self-styled ‘bottom line’ people.”

George Will is no Solon

  • Historians judge by the visible history. Hannibal and Hitler look mad because Rome doesn’t speak Phoenician and Times Square has no swastikas. But many equally foolish generals won, and Alexander and Julius Caesar may have “won only in the visible history”. Homer understood: in the Iliad heroes win or lose at the gods’ whim and are heroes for their conduct. Patroclus is a hero because he chose to die rather than watch Achilles sulk.
  • Taleb grew up without television and learned to operate a set in his late twenties. He watched the commentator George Will tell Robert Shiller, author of Irrational Exuberance, that people who had listened to his warnings would have lost money, since the market had more than doubled since he started calling it overvalued. Will wrote an article on Shiller’s bad “prophecy”. For Taleb it was every nightmare of his career: being publicly humiliated for having stopped someone from playing Russian roulette. “Mixing forecast and prophecy is symptomatic of randomness foolishness.”
  • “Every time I have been humiliated in a public discussion on markets by someone … who seemed to present more palatable and easier to understand arguments, I turned out (much later) to be right.” MBAs are trained to simplify one step too far, which is why they blow up; Taleb is “myself the unhappy holder of the degree”.
  • “Beware the confusion between correctness and intelligibility.” At his French school he recited Boileau’s maxim that what is well conceived is clearly expressed. Most such adages are wrong. Einstein called common sense “a collection of misconceptions acquired by age 18”, and “what sounds intelligent in a conversation or a meeting, or, particularly in the media, is suspicious.” Almost every great scientific result sounded mad at first: try telling a London Times journalist in 1905 that time slows down when you travel. (Einstein’s Nobel wasn’t for relativity.)

Risk managers, and Taleb’s paradox

  • Firms now employ risk managers to watch the generator, and the average risk manager earns more than the average trader once you count the traders thrown out. But the generator is invisible. They can’t stop profitable traders without being blamed by the George Wills for lost profits, and they are blamed for blow-ups, so, like a doctor torn between false positives and false negatives, they play politics and write vaguely worded memos. Taleb solved the problem by being both risk manager and boss.
  • The paradox of his career: he wants investors in general to stay fooled by randomness, so he can trade against them, and a few to be wise enough to give him capital. Donald Sussman backed the start of Empirica. “My greatest risk is to become successful, as it would mean that my business is about to disappear.”

Chapter 3

A Mathematical Meditation on History

History is one sample path among many. Think in Monte Carlo terms, prefer old ideas to new ones, and look at results as rarely as possible.

On Monte Carlo simulation as a metaphor to understanding a sequence of random historical events. On randomness and artificial history. Age is beauty, almost always, and the new and the young are generally toxic. Send your history professor to an introductory class on sampling theory.The book’s own summary

Monte Carlo as a way of thinking

  • The pure mathematician stereotype is a bearded recluse lost in abstraction, like the Unabomber, whose thesis was on “Complex Boundaries”. The Monte Carlo man is a suntanned “Europlayboy” who counts cards, “James Bond’s smarter lost brother”. Monte Carlo mathematics combines the realism of the second with the intuition of the first. “Mathematics is principally a tool to meditate, rather than to compute.”
  • The tools: a sample path is one realised sequence of events over time; the invisible histories are “alternative sample paths”. A random sample path isn’t equiprobable: some outcomes are likelier than others. Examples: a cousin’s temperature through typhoid, hour by hour; a tech stock that starts at $100 and ends at $20 after touching $220, or at $145 after touching $10; an evening at a casino that turns $1,000 into $2,200 or leaves $20 for a cab. Stochastic processes are “the mathematics of history”.
  • A Monte Carlo generator is a perfect roulette wheel in the attic. Run a simulated 18-year-old playing Russian roulette, or change the barrel to 500 chambers, and count how many get rich and how soon the rest reach the obituaries. The method was pioneered at Los Alamos during the building of the atomic bomb and became popular in finance in the 1980s.
  • “True” mathematicians call it “brute force”: you can compute pi by firing random shots at a circle drawn inside a square. Taleb is not a “native” mathematician; he learned probability because options forced him to, as compulsive gamblers learn card counting. Einstein’s 1905 paper on Brownian motion and Keynes’s Treatise on Probability show that great minds start with probability. Keynes, Taleb adds, blew up trading his own account.
  • In the early 1990s Taleb became addicted to building Monte Carlo engines, feeling like “a Demiurgus” generating history. The computer revolution’s dividend, for him, wasn’t email but fast processors producing a million sample paths a minute, which let a self-described “unenthusiastic equation solver” solve almost anything. His job as an option trader left him close to 95% of his day free to think, read and research, often from his attic. He simulated fast-mutating animals he called Zorglubs, cancer cells, and populations of “idiotic bull”, “impetuous bear” and “cautious” traders. The bears dropped out during rallies, the bulls were slaughtered when the music stopped, and the only traders with staying power were option buyers, who “could buy the insurance against blowup”. He calls the habit of seeing every outcome among its unrealised siblings “summing under histories”, after Richard Feynman, and compares rerunning history to the 1960s “new novels” of Alain Robbe-Grillet, which rewrite the same chapter with a different plot, like a new sample path.

Learning from history

  • Learning from others’ history doesn’t come naturally. Children only stop touching a hot stove when burned. Taleb himself is lax about medical screening, feeling special, and aggressive once something goes wrong.
  • Taleb wants history as anecdote, not as grand theory: not Hegel’s laws of history, nor “the end of history”, which draws theories from events that may have been random. He has two ways to learn from history, from the past by reading the elders, and from the future with his Monte Carlo toy.
  • “All of my colleagues whom I have known to denigrate history blew up spectacularly.” Those who blew up in the crash of 1987, in Japan in 1990, in bonds in 1994, in Russia in 1998 and in Nasdaq stocks in 2000 all said “these times are different”. They offered well-constructed economic arguments, with books on past crashes in every bookstore. Experts call one form of this historical determinism: we assume people living through 1929 knew history was being made, as if life were an adventure film with a soundtrack warning that something big is coming.
  • My Solon. Solon matters to Taleb personally. For Eastern Mediterranean Greek Orthodox families like his, prosperous minorities in the Islamic world since Constantinople fell some 500 years ago, wealth was always fragile; his family had swung between great opulence and penury within single generations, and he remembers his grandfather, a former deputy prime minister, living in a nondescript apartment in Athens after the family estate was blown up in the Lebanese civil war. “I find undignified impoverishment far harsher than physical danger”, which is why he fears financial risks more than physical ones. He is sure Croesus worried more about losing his kingdom than his life.
  • History can’t be experimented on, but over the medium to long run it delivers most of the possible scenarios, “burying the bad guy”. Ergodicity: under some conditions very long sample paths come to resemble each other. The lottery-winning janitor, living a thousand years, would not keep winning, and “the lucky fool … over the longer run … would slowly converge to the state of a less-lucky idiot”. As traders say, bad trades catch up with you.

Distilled thinking

  • Journalism is to history as noise is to information. An honest journalist would report that “today the market went up, but this information is not too relevant as it emanates mostly from noise” and lose his job. Worse, historians are becoming journalists.
  • “For an idea, age is beauty.” An idea that has survived many cycles has had noise filtered out. Progress means some new information is better than the old, not that the average new idea is, so “when in doubt” it is optimal “to systematically reject the new idea, information, or method.” We count the car, the aeroplane and the telephone and forget the Saturday newspaper’s lists of patents; actors look rich because we don’t count the waiters.
  • “The problem with information is not that it is diverting and generally useless, but that it is toxic.” Taleb reads The Economist and the society gossip of Tatler, Paris Match and Vanity Fair, and pities the commuters on the 6:42 train buried in the Wall Street Journal. “For a journalist, silence rarely surpasses any word.”
  • Shiller redux. Robert Shiller’s 1981 paper showed that stock prices are far more volatile than the dividends they are supposed to reflect, so markets overreact and can’t be efficient. Robert C. Merton attacked it on methodological grounds (Shiller’s use of dividends rather than earnings was rough), defending the doctrine that markets can’t hand out opportunities. Then he became a founding partner of a hedge fund built to exploit market inefficiencies, which later blew up. “It is as if the Pope converted to Islam.” Meanwhile “breaking news” arrives wirelessly, and the ratio of undistilled to distilled information keeps rising. Taleb names a few thoughtful journalists, Anatole Kaletsky, Jim Grant, Alan Abelson and Gary Stix, but says the medium has no way to separate them from the noise, and often penalises the smart ones.
  • Gerontocracy. His simulations favoured selecting traders by years of experience, given that they had survived, rather than by profits, because older traders have been exposed longer to rare events. “Survival of the fittest” is misunderstood: when regimes switch, it isn’t clear who is fittest, and the survivors are often simply the oldest. Evolutionary psychologists make a similar argument about grey hair as a signal of resistance to life’s vagaries. Renaissance Italian life insurers charged a man in his 50s the same as one in his 20s, since past 40 he had shown few ailments could harm him.

The dentist: noise and time scale

  • “The wise man listens to meaning, the fool only gets the noise.” In 1915 Cavafy wrote a poem on Philostratus’s adage: “For the gods perceive things in the future, ordinary people things in the present, but the wise perceive things about to happen.” In their meditation “the hidden sound of things approaching reaches them … while in the street outside the people hear nothing at all.”
  • Take a happily retired dentist who is an excellent investor: an expected return of 15% a year above Treasury bills, with 10% volatility, so that 68 years in 100 fall between 5% and 25%, and 95 between −5% and 35%. He sets up a trading desk in his attic with live prices and decaffeinated cappuccino.
  • That is a 93% chance of making money in any year, and a 50.02% chance in any second (see the figure). Watching minute by minute over an eight-hour day, he has 241 pleasurable minutes against 239 painful ones, 60,688 against 60,271 a year. Since a loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases, up to 2.5 times as much by some estimates, he is emotionally drained. Checking monthly, he gets eight pleasant surprises and four pangs a year. Checking yearly, he expects 19 pleasant surprises for every unpleasant one over the next 20 years.
  • The ratio of noise to performance is roughly 0.7 over a year, 2.32 over a month, 30 over an hour and 1,796 over a second. “Over a short time increment, one observes the variability of the portfolio, not the returns.” And: “When I see an investor monitoring his portfolio with live prices on his cellular telephone or his PalmPilot, I smile and smile.”
  • Taleb admits he is no better emotionally, so he denies himself information. He reads poetry: “If an event is important enough, it will find its way to my ears.” In trading rooms he talks to the mathematicians and the secretaries, not the traders. “My sole advantage in life is that I know some of my weaknesses.”

Randomness, Nonsense, and the Scientific Intellectual

Science wars and the reverse Turing test

  • The “science wars” set literary intellectuals against scientific ones. The split began in Vienna in the 1930s, when a circle of physicists decided science had earned a say in the humanities and set out to strip rhetoric from thinking, leaving it to literature and poetry, where it belonged. A statement was either deductive (“2 + 2 = 4”) or inductive, verifiable by experience (“it rains in Spain”, “New Yorkers are generally rude”); anything else was hogwash, and music a better replacement for metaphysics. From that milieu came Wittgenstein, Popper and Carnap.
  • Taleb’s caveat, developed later: inductive statements can be impossible to verify (the black swan problem), and empiricism “can be worse than any other form of hogwash when it gives someone confidence.” Still, making intellectuals supply evidence was a good start.
  • A scientific intellectual can recognise another’s writing; a literary intellectual can’t tell a scientist from a glib non-scientist who uses buzzwords like “uncertainty principle”, “Gödel’s theorem”, “parallel universe” or “relativity”. Taleb laughed out loud on a plane reading Alan Sokal’s Fashionable Nonsense. “Science is method and rigor; it can be identified in the simplest of prose writing.” Richard Dawkins’s The Selfish Gene reads “as if it were translated from the language of mathematics”.
  • Turing’s test says a computer is intelligent if a human can’t tell its conversation from a person’s. Taleb proposes the reverse: “A human can be said to be unintelligent if we can replicate his speech by a computer”. “Rhetoric can be constructed randomly, but not genuine scientific knowledge.”
  • You can generate Jacques Derrida or Camille Paglia at random. Sokal did it by hand, getting a hoax paper published. Andrew Bulhak’s Dada Engine at Monash University uses recursive grammars to churn out essays such as “the main theme of the works of Rushdie is not theory, as the dialectic paradigm of reality suggests, but pretheory”.
  • Taleb’s own CEO-speech generator strings together “our assets are our people”, “creation of shareholder value”, “a happy employee is a productive employee”, “commitment to excellence”. “If this bears too close a resemblance to the speech you just heard from the boss of your company, then I suggest looking for a new job.”

The father of all pseudothinkers

  • Hegel is Taleb’s case, via a passage Popper translated and mocked: “Sound is the change in the specific condition of segregation of the material parts … the heating up of sounding bodies … is the appearance of heat, originating conceptually together with sound.” Hegel’s “scientific” history led on to Marxist regimes, and he “should be given an undergraduate-level class on statistical sampling theory”.

Monte Carlo poetry

  • Taleb’s allergy to nonsense vanishes for art. After the First World War the surrealists, André Breton and Paul Éluard among them, played “exquisite cadavers”: each player writes a word in a fixed order (adjective, noun, verb, adjective, noun) without seeing the others’. The first result named the game: “The exquisite cadavers shall drink the new wine.”
  • Holy languages work the same way. Translating the Catholic liturgy from Latin into the vernacular may have cost the church believers. Taleb’s Greek Orthodox family in Antioch prays in a mix of dead Koiné Greek and no less dead Koranic Arabic, and is lucky to.
  • “We do not need to be rational and scientific when it comes to the details of our daily life — only in those that can harm us and threaten our survival.” Modern life inverts this, pushing rationality into trivia and emotion into big decisions. Taleb takes his nonsense from Baudelaire, Canetti, Borges and Saint-John Perse rather than a TV guru.
  • A Yiddish saying: “If I am going to be forced to eat pork, it better be of the best kind.” Likewise with being fooled by randomness: let it be the beautiful, harmless kind.

Survival of the Least Fit — Can Evolution Be Fooled by Randomness?

Carlos the emerging-markets wizard

  • Carlos, impeccably dressed at New York parties, came from a patrician Latin American family impoverished in the 1980s. (Taleb rarely meets anyone from a ravaged country whose family did not once own a province or supply the Czar with dominoes.) His Harvard doctoral adviser found him unimaginative, so he settled for a master’s.
  • In the early 1990s the bonds of Russia, Mexico, Brazil, Argentina and Turkey went from pennies to dollars. Emerging-market traders were urbane patricians with art collections and opera subscriptions, Mexicans in London trading Russian paper. “True traders”, Taleb says, “dress sloppily, are often ugly” and prefer the garbage can to the Cézanne.
  • Carlos just bought, “and he bought in size”. Shorting made no economic sense to him. He bought every dip, and the dips kept paying: the Mexican devaluation of 1995 and the false crash of October 1997 were “excellent buying opportunities”.
  • Averaging down. In June 1998 Russian bonds fell. The sell-off was put down to “liquidation” by a New Jersey mortgage-securities hedge fund run by a former Wharton professor, one of the “yield hogs” that hold a “diversified” basket of high-yielding paper. Carlos bought more: “Read my lips: It’s a li-qui-da-tion!”
  • The price was not fooled. The market’s veterans had noticed what the economists, talking only to each other, had missed: the Russian government wasn’t paying its own soldiers. Marty O’Connell calls this the firehouse effect: firemen with a lot of downtime who talk only to each other come to agree on things an outsider would find ludicrous.
  • Carlos was escorted out by a security guard. Louie, a 52-year-old Brooklyn-born veteran of three decades on the next desk: “Economics Schmeconomics. It is all market dynamics.” History may yet prove Carlos right about Russia, and it wouldn’t matter; he had the attributes of a bad trader. “At any point in time, the richest traders are often the worst traders.” Taleb calls this the cross-sectional problem: the most profitable traders at any moment are likely to be those best fitted to the latest cycle.

John and the quant

  • John, Nero’s neighbour, was 35, seven years on Wall Street since the Pace graduate business school, head of a team of ten after a well-timed move between firms, paid 20% of his profits and allowed to invest his own money in his trades. He wasn’t principally intelligent but was thought to have business sense: “pragmatic”, “professional”, never saying anything out of place. Monthly trips to his unit’s London satellite kept him in Savile Row suits and Ferragamo ties, and Nero always came away feeling badly dressed.
  • His trade: buy high-yield bonds paying about 10% while borrowing at 5.5%, earn the 4.5% difference, and multiply it with leverage, in several countries, over $3 billion face value, hedged by selling US, UK, French and other government bond futures so that he was betting only on the spread. Most of his profit didn’t come from the spread but from rising prices, as other traders copied his strategy and pushed the bonds towards what he believed was “fair value”. Henry, a foreign quant with incomprehensible English whose income rose from $50,000 to $600,000 in three years, ran the models. “His brains and my business sense.”
  • John’s last bonus was $10 million before tax. His net worth rose from $1 million at 32 to over $16 million at 35, and he kept about $14 million in his own trades: a $50 million position, $36 million of it borrowed.
  • In the high-yield meltdown of summer 1998 the $14 million went “into thin air” in a few days. Everything moved against him at once, and the hedges stopped working. At first he ignored it: “One would go crazy if one were to listen to the mood swings of the market”, meaning that the noise should reverse itself. It kept adding up in the same direction. The dip wasn’t large; his leverage was. Henry’s models had put such a move at one in 1024 years, a “ten sigma” event, and Henry doubling the odds made no difference. John was mad at him: perhaps there was a bug in the program. “As in a biblical cycle, it took seven years to make John a hero and just seven days to make him an idiot.”
  • John had earned his employers about $250 million over seven years and lost more than $600 million for the last of them in barely a few days. His remaining $1 million or so still made him richer than 99.9% of humanity, but there is “a difference between a wealth level reached from above and a wealth reached from below.” “The road from $16 million to $1 million is not as pleasant as the one from 0 to $1 million.” He is now a pariah whose calls go unreturned, ashamed to meet old friends. He will not recover, not because he lost money, which good traders are used to, but because he blew up, and because “John was never a trader in the first place. He is one of those people who happened to be there when it all happened.”

Naive evolutionary theories

  • Jacques Monod said everyone believes they are an expert on evolution. Businesspeople talk of Darwinian markets, but Darwin was about reproductive fitness, not survival, and companies don’t reproduce. Evolution isn’t smooth either: Stephen Jay Gould stressed “genetic noise”, and the old maxim natura non facit saltus (nature makes no leaps), used by Linnaeus and Leibniz, is false.
  • Animals are fit on average, not in every instance. “The longer these animals can go without encountering the rare event, the more vulnerable they will be to it”, and given enough time the rare event is certain. Evolution selects for fitness to the one time series that happened, “not the average of all the possible environments”. In the short run, John was an eligible mate.

The median is not the message

  • Stephen Jay Gould, once Taleb’s role model, was diagnosed with a deadly cancer of the lining of the stomach, as the book puts it, and was told that median survival was about eight months. His own research showed the median hid a long tail: those who die do so early, and those who survive go on to something like the 73.4 years of the insurance tables. He wrote it up as “The Median Is Not the Message”. (He lived another twenty years.)
  • Table 6.1: a bet that pays $1 with probability 999 in 1,000 and loses $10,000 with probability 1 in 1,000 wins almost every time and has an expected value of minus $9.001. “The frequency or probability of the loss, in and by itself, is totally irrelevant; it needs to be judged in connection with the magnitude of the outcome.” Schooling uses symmetric examples, coin tosses and the bell curve, where the two coincide.

Bull and bear zoology

  • At a weekly “discussion meeting” at a large investment house, salespeople and economists and strategists (“pure intellectual pollution”) offered views on the markets. Asked for his, Taleb said the market was 70% likely to go slightly up next week. Then it came out he was short a very large amount of S&P 500 futures. “I did not change my mind! I have a lot of faith in my bet! … I now feel like selling even more!”
  • Table 6.2 explains: 70% chance of a 1% rise, 30% chance of a 10% fall, expected move −2.3%. “Investors and businesses are not paid in probabilities, they are paid in dollars.” Being “bullish” or “bearish” is a statement about frequency and means nothing without magnitude. Commentators and “chief strategists” paid for how often they are right are entertainers.
  • A rich friend of Taleb’s father, just off the Concorde, asked for his view on the European bond markets by Christmas. Taleb had no view, and the friend complained to his father: “Your insolent and conceited 29-year-old son is playing prima donna and refuses to answer me about the direction of the market!”

Rare events

  • Taleb’s business is “skewed bets”: he loses small, often, and wins large, rarely, and “the rarer the event, the more undervalued it will be in price”. The crash of 1987 “made me as a trader”. Nero is defensive; Taleb is aggressive, positioned to profit from rare events.
  • Jim Rogers, Soros’s former partner, told an interviewer he never bought options, a road “to the poorhouse”, since a study for the SEC found 90% of options expire worthless, so he sold them instead. Taleb’s reply: if the 10% that pay off pay 50 times the premium, the buyer ends in “the palazzo rather than the poorhouse”.
  • Figure 6.1 is any time series: a trader’s record, the price of palazzo real estate in Renaissance Florence, the Mongolian stock market. If the world were deterministic, a 19th-century engineer trained under Laplace could extrapolate it; if its randomness were charted, a friendly econometrician with software costing under $999 could. We don’t know which world we are in, so Taleb rejects a single past series as a guide to the future. We read too much into shallow recent history (“this has never happened before”) and too little into history in general, which teaches that things that never happened before do happen.
  • Removing outliers is harmless where payoffs are even, as when a professor drops the highest and lowest grades. Finance borrowed the habit. So did early global-warming research, Taleb says: scientists who removed temperature spikes from their samples missed that rare spikes add disproportionately to the melting of the ice cap. The currencies of Malaysia, Indonesia and Thailand in the summer of 1997 had been pegged and showed great historical stability right up to their crash. “History teaches us that things that never happened before do happen.”
  • The mother of all deceptions is the “peso problem”, named after Mexico in the 1980s: a strategy that looks safe because the disaster hasn’t yet appeared in the data. “Beware of calm waters.” Hedge-fund letters explaining a loss as “an unforeseen and unexpected event, deemed a rare occurrence” miss that “rare events exist precisely because they are unexpected”. In the spring of 1998 Taleb explained the peso problem to a hedge-fund operator, who agreed and said they never touched the peso, only the Russian ruble. He blew up months later, when ruble holders lost close to 97%.

Why statisticians don’t see rare events

  • French mathematicians call statistics “cuisine”. Knowledge from a sample grows with the square root of its size: 20 draws are only 1.41 times as informative as 10. And the growth is asymmetric. One red ball proves an urn contains red balls, but ever more black draws only slowly suggest it doesn’t.
  • The mischievous child: now imagine a child who keeps changing the balls in the urn. Economic statistics assume stationarity, that the urn doesn’t change. Taleb had studied econometrics since he was 19. Robert Lucas’s critique, which won a Nobel, showed that people adapt: a known pattern of prices falling on Mondays gets ironed out by traders buying on Friday. More and better econometrics, as Hashem Pesaran proposed, doesn’t help: “a sum of zeros, even repeated a billion times, remains zero.”

The Problem of Induction

No number of confirmations proves a rule; one counterexample kills it. Taleb’s trading is built on that asymmetry.

On the chromodynamics of swans. Taking Solon’s warning into some philosophical territory. How Victor Niederhoffer taught me empiricism; I added deduction. From Bacon to Hume

  • Francis Bacon attacked the scholastics’ “cobweb of learning” and told people to look at data. Hume, the first modern epistemologist, pointed out that data can never establish a general rule. John Stuart Mill turned it into the black swan problem: “No amount of observations of white swans can allow the inference that all swans are white, but the observation of a single black swan is sufficient to refute that conclusion.” Finance is especially exposed, because randomness compounds.

Niederhoffer, Victorian gentleman

  • Victor Niederhoffer is finance’s Francis Bacon. He stood against the University of Chicago’s efficient-market “religion” of the 1960s, looked for anomalies in the data, wrote The Education of a Speculator, and trained the first “statistical arbitrageurs”. He “had a publicized hiccup”, while some of his trainees did well because they added methodology to his empiricism.
  • In 1996 Niederhoffer told Taleb that any testable statement should be tested, and Taleb has done so ever since. But testing needs care. If about 20% of accidents happen within 12 miles of home, a naive reader concludes driving near home is dangerous, when people may simply spend 20% of their driving time there.
  • The difference between them: “I can use data to disprove a proposition, never to prove one.” “The market never goes down 20% in a given 3-month period” can be refuted by one counterexample and is meaningless if the data agree.
  • Niederhoffer sells out-of-the-money options for steady income; Taleb buys them for lumpy, rare payoffs, and many of Victor’s trades end up in Taleb’s book. They share Victorian habits: books scattered about, scientists for company, daily athletics, no media. Victor’s hero is Francis Galton, Darwin’s cousin; Taleb’s are literary.

Popper, via Soros

  • Taleb is rarely changed by what he reads, but Karl Popper stuck. He came to him through George Soros’s The Alchemy of Finance: Soros, a trader ashamed of being a trader, called himself fallible and changed his mind with minimal shame, “which carries the side effect of making him treat people like napkins”. “He lived a Popperian life.”
  • In 1987, in the mildewed side room of the Barnes & Noble at 21st Street and Fifth Avenue, Taleb read 50 pages of The Logic of Scientific Discovery and bought every Popper title, as Paul Claudel was converted by a column in Notre-Dame. As an option trader he had been angry at academics whose failed models were his income; “it was not what they knew, it was how they knew it”.
  • Popper’s answer: there are only two kinds of theory, those known to be wrong and those not yet known to be wrong but exposed to being proved wrong. A theory is never verified. A theory with no conditions under which it would be wrong is charlatanism, like astrology, and “a trader who does not have a point that would make him change his mind is not a trader.” Newton’s physics was scientific because Einstein could falsify it.
  • Popper also doubted that knowledge always grows with information. Taleb calls himself “an exceedingly naive falsificationist. Why? Because I can survive being one.” His rule: “no rare event should harm me. In fact, I would like all conceivable rare events to help me.” He links Popper’s open society, where no truth is held to be permanent, with his friend Hayek’s idea that prices spread information that bureaucratic socialism would choke. Any Utopia is closed because it chokes its own refutations, and a model of society that can’t be falsified is totalitarian. From Popper Taleb took the difference between an open and a closed society, and between an open and a closed mind.
  • Nobody is perfect. Bryan Magee, an Oxford don and Popper’s friend of three decades, says Popper was unworldly, gave firm advice about careers and private lives he understood poorly, and was “brilliant, but self-focused, both insecure and arrogant, irascible and self-righteous. He was a terrible listener and bent on winning arguments at all costs.” Soros was “more Popperian than Popper in his professional behavior”. The gap is itself Taleb’s point: we like to emit rational ideas, but we aren’t built to execute them.

Pascal’s wager, adapted

  • Pascal argued it pays to believe in God: if God exists you are rewarded, and if not you have lost nothing. Taleb’s version for statistics: “If the science of statistics can benefit me in anything, I will use it. If it poses a threat, then I will not.”
  • Concretely: “I will use statistics and inductive methods to make aggressive bets, but I will not use them to manage my risks and exposure.” Every surviving trader he knows trades on ideas drawn from observation but makes sure the cost of being wrong is limited, with that limit not derived from past data. Carlos and John used past history both to make their bets and to measure their risk. The survivors know in advance which event would prove them wrong and get out when it happens, which is what a stop loss is: “a protection from the black swan. I find it rarely practiced.”
  • Thank you, Solon. Writing Part I pushed Taleb further from the media. He tries to live a low-information 19th-century life with modern medicine, Monte Carlo and social justice, and calls that evolution.

Part II

Monkeys on Typewriters: Survivorship and Other Biases

Whether a result is impressive depends on how many monkeys there were. How to stop the sting of failure

  • Marc lives on Park Avenue with his wife Janet and three children, and earns $500,000 a year as a corporate lawyer. He is rotund, in his late forties, and looks ten years older. He gets home at 9.30 p.m., sometimes midnight, drives three hours to “the house” in the country on Friday, and spends Saturday recovering.
  • Son of a Midwestern tax accountant who kept a pencil sharpener in his pocket, Marc went to Harvard College and Yale Law School and made partner in the usual seven years. After his first wife left him he married Janet, a paralegal.
  • On Park Avenue their apartment is among the smallest in the building, and their children’s schoolmates are the second (or third) families of corporate raiders. Janet feels poor. Her neighbours are worth hundreds of millions; people mention that Ronald Something got close to 1600 on his SATs.

Double survivorship: the millionaire next door

  • A best-selling book, The Millionaire Next Door, studied the rich and found them to be modest “accumulators” who defer spending; it costs time and money to look rich (trendy clothes, Bordeaux, expensive restaurants), so the wealthiest are the least suspected. Taleb isn’t moved by the moral: there is no heroism in accumulating money, and if austerity is the goal, one should “become a monk or a social worker”. “Becoming rich is a purely selfish act, not a social one.” He has no wish to give up his habits and pleasures to become a billionaire like Warren Buffett and live in a starter house. Capitalism’s virtue is that it harnesses greed rather than benevolence; there’s no need to praise the greed as well.
  • First bias: the study sees only the accumulators who got rich. Many accumulated the wrong things. Taleb’s own family saved in currencies that were about to be devalued and shares in companies that went bust. He would lower the average millionaire’s wealth by, say, 50%.
  • Second bias: the sample comes from the greatest bull market in history. “A dollar invested in the average stock would have grown almost twenty-fold since 1982.” Written in 1935 the book would read differently. His grandfather held Lebanese lira Treasury bills, his colleagues held Michael Milken’s junk bonds in the 1980s, others held Russian Imperial bonds signed by Czar Nicholas II, and his great-grandfather held Argentine real estate in the 1930s. Virtually all the book’s subjects got rich from the inflation of asset prices since 1982; accepting its thesis means believing that inflation is permanent, the belief that prevailed before 1929. “The highest performing realization will be the most visible. Why? Because the losers do not show up.”

A guru’s opinion

  • A finance guru and a colleague back-tested a “Robin Hood” policy: take money from the managers who did best and give it to those who did worst. On paper it beat the alternatives. The flaw was that the database contained only managers still in business, so the losers in it were exactly those who had recovered. The proper test would take the managers who existed five years ago and run the policy forward.

Placebo investors

  • People ask “Who do you think you are to tell me that I might have been plain lucky in my life?” Taleb’s answer is to manufacture pure nobodies, like a placebo drug, whose luck is known exactly. A broken clock is right twice a day.
  • Take 10,000 fictional managers, each with a fair coin: 50% chance of making $10,000 in a year, 50% chance of losing it.   • Nobody has to be competent. Now make them all incompetent: an urn of 45 black balls and 55 red, so each loses $1,000 a year on average. After five years, 184 still have perfect records, fewer than 2% of the cohort, but they get the attention and nobody mentions the other 98%.

  • Ergodicity. About 3% of the fair cohort make money five years running, and going forward they should be expected to break even. When Taleb told “A.”, a Master-of-the-Universe type, that track records mattered less than he thought, A. threw his cigarette lighter at him. A.’s “great traders” blew up in the harsh New York winter of 1994, in the bond crash after Alan Greenspan’s surprise rate rise, and six years later Taleb can hardly find any of them still trading. “Nobody accepts randomness in his own success, only his failure.”
  • A record means nothing without the size of the population it came from. If ten managers started, Taleb would give the best one half his savings; if 10,000 started, he would ignore the result. These days many graduates try trading, fail, and go to dental school. He books meetings with managers for 11.45 a.m., so that if one leans on his track record he can leave for lunch.

Life is coincidental

  • The mysterious letter. On 2 January an anonymous letter says the market will rise in January. It does, but that’s the January effect. On 1 February another says it will fall; it does. On 1 March, the same. By July you trust the writer enough to put all your savings into his offshore fund, and two months later the money is gone. Your neighbour got two such letters: the first right, the second wrong, and then they stopped.
  • An interrupted tennis game. Adverts for funds that have outperformed, shown during televised tennis, are adverse selection: a fund that did well by luck is exactly the one that will advertise. Picking from a cohort of 10,000 managers, Taleb has 2 chances in 100 of finding a spurious survivor; by answering his doorbell, closer to 100%.
  • The birthday paradox. A stranger has a one in 365.25 chance of sharing your birthday, but in a room of 23 people the chance that some pair shares one is about 50%, because any pair will do. The same goes for “it’s a small world” encounters, which are thousands of times likelier than they feel, and for the discovery that the stock market tracks women’s skirt lengths.
  • Evelyn Adams won the New Jersey lottery twice, odds reported as one in 17 trillion. Persi Diaconis and Frederick Mosteller at Harvard calculated it was 30 to 1 that someone, somewhere, would be that lucky. Michael Drosnin’s The Bible Code found a “prediction” of Yitzhak Rabin’s assassination in the text, which says more about how many ways a text can be searched.

Data mining, backtesting and the earnings season

  • Book jackets quote the best review ever written, the maximum of a distribution. The mathematician Paul Wilmott once printed a line from Taleb’s first bad review of his book on the jacket as praise; they later became friends. At 16 Taleb bought John Dos Passos’s Manhattan Transfer because Sartre called him the greatest writer of the age; Dos Passos has since faded.
  • The backtester. Test enough rules, like “buy Nasdaq stocks if they close more than 1.83% above their average of the previous week”, then 1.2%, then something else, and one will fit the past by chance. Somewhere there is a security 100% correlated with the temperature in Ulan Bator. A paper by Sullivan, Timmermann and White makes the point formally: across a wide universe of trading rules, some will produce superior performance by pure luck. With software like Omega TradeStation, day-traders have become “night-testers”.
  • Medicine has the same problem, since medical researchers are rarely statisticians; Taleb cites a study linking smoking to reduced breast cancer. Analysts dig through accounts for tricks but ignore randomness. Among 10,000 companies earning only the risk-free rate, 1,250 will beat forecasts three years running and be rated “strong buy”. And “markets will always go up in any 20-year period” depends on which market you stood in; an investor in Imperial Russia in 1900 ended up with “medium-quality wallpaper”.
  • Cancer cures. Late-night TV sells a vitamin cure for $14.95 on the testimony of a former throat-cancer patient. Spontaneous remissions happen, and a patient could as well credit Vermont spring water. Carl Sagan found the cancer cure rate at Lourdes was, if anything, below the spontaneous remission rate. Should one infer that a visit to Lourdes lowers your odds?
  • Randomness does not look random. Karl Pearson devised the first test of non-randomness and in July 1902 applied it to millions of runs of a “Monte Carlo”, the old name for a roulette wheel. The runs were not random, with an error of less than one in a billion. That tells us little, since no physical wheel is perfect, and a pattern worth $1 on 10,000 spins is a job for a janitorial agency, not a trader. The deeper point is that real randomness looks patterned: throw 16 darts at a board divided into 16 squares and the chance of exactly one per square is tiny. Clusters, like cancer clusters blamed on a power line, are what chance produces.
  • The dog that did not bark. Research that finds nothing rarely gets published, so “a finding of absence and an absence of findings get mixed together”. Sherlock Holmes solved Silver Blaze from the dog that didn’t bark: a non-result is information.
  • I have no conclusion. When is it truly not luck? “I am unable to answer it.” “I never said that every rich man is an idiot and every unsuccessful person unlucky, only that in absence of much additional information I prefer to reserve my judgment.”

Chapter 10

Loser Takes All — On the Nonlinearities of Life

Small, often random advantages compound into winner-take-all outcomes, so the winner needn’t be the best.

The nonlinear viciousness of life. Moving to Bel Air and acquiring the vices of the rich and famous. Why Microsoft’s Bill Gates may not be the best in his business (but please do not inform him of such a fact). Depriving donkeys of food.The book’s own summary

  • “Life is unfair in a nonlinear way”: a small advantage, or “a very, very small help from randomness, can lead to a bonanza”.
  • The sandpile effect. Building a sand tower on Copacabana beach in Rio with a child’s toys, Taleb watched the last grain collapse it: the straw that broke the camel’s back. “Chaos theory” is a misnomer for such effects, of which the famous example is a butterfly in India causing a hurricane in New York. Pascal said the world would have changed had Cleopatra’s nose been shorter; Plutarch said it was her conversation, not her looks, that captivated Caesar and Antony, “and I truly believe it”.
  • Enter randomness. Of equally talented actors waiting for an audition, the few who win move to Bel Air, and the rest serve foamed caffè latte at Starbucks. Fame feeds on itself, and the initial pick can turn on something as small as the examiner having fallen in love the day before with someone of a similar-sounding last name.
  • Learning to type. The QWERTY keyboard was designed to slow typists down so the keys wouldn’t jam, and it survived because habits stick. Microsoft’s success owes much to “network externalities”: Bill Gates has high standards and above-average intelligence, but “is he the best? Does he deserve it? Clearly not.” Nobody claimed Windows was the best software. Brian Arthur of the Santa Fe Institute showed how “unexpected orders, chance meetings with lawyers, managerial whims” decide which firms get early sales, and early sales decide which firms dominate.
  • Mathematics inside and outside the real world. In a Brownian random walk each step is independent. In a Polya process, the chance of success depends on past successes, as with an urn that adds a ball of whatever colour you draw. The result is huge variance: astonishing successes and many failures. Economics was mathematised by Léon Walras, Gérard Debreu and Paul Samuelson with tools that assume away such feedback. Monte Carlo methods free us from the equations.
  • Buridan’s donkey. A donkey exactly as hungry as it is thirsty, placed between food and water, dies of both, unable to choose. A random nudge saves it, which is why optimisers use random perturbations. Jean Buridan, the 14th-century philosopher it is named after, was thrown into the Seine tied in a bag and drowned; his contemporaries took the donkey for sophistry and missed the point about randomisation.
  • When it rains, it pours. When Taleb opened his fund, he found that funds, like books, either attract all the money or none. It offends his “antiquated Mediterranean sense of measure”: “Too much success is the enemy … too much failure is demoralizing. I would like the option of having neither.”

Chapter 11

Randomness and Our Brain: We Are Probability Blind

We can’t picture probabilities, so we swap them for images, stories and feelings, and the professionals do it too.

On the difficulty of thinking of your vacation as a linear combination of Paris and the Bahamas. Nero Tulip may never ski in the Alps again. Some discussion of behavioral discoveries. Some manifestations of probability blindness taken out of a textbook. A little more on journalistic pollution. Why you may be dead by now.The book’s own summary

Paris or the Bahamas?

  • You are torn between a March holiday in Paris, standing in the Musée d’Orsay before a grey Pissarro sky with an umbrella under your arm, and the Caribbean, on a towel with Tom Clancy and Ammianus Marcellinus and a banana daiquiri. It’s 50–50. The rational picture is a “linear combination”, half in each. “Our brain can properly handle one and only one state at once.” Now try 85–15.
  • A fair $1,000 bet leaves you with $0 or $2,000 tomorrow. Its expected value is $1,000, but you can’t picture having $1,000; one of the two images dominates, and you bet accordingly.
  • Nero’s secret. At 35, in an Upper East Side doctor’s office overlooking a bland pink backyard, after reading the diploma on the wall a dozen times, Nero heard: “It’s … It’s cancer.” He read the fear in the doctor’s eyes: the news was worse than he was being told. That night, soaked from walking for hours in the rain, he sat in a medical library and read “72% 5-year actuarially adjusted survival rate”.
  • A 28% chance of dying and a 72% chance of living are the same fact. To Nero, the first meant his funeral and the second meant skiing in the Alps. “At no point during his ordeal did Nero think of himself as 72% alive and 28% dead.”

A brain built for somewhere else

  • Probability blindness spawned behavioural finance and funds built on it: contrarians who bet people overreact, momentum players who bet they underreact. Randomness gives both periodic victories, which prove neither right. A yearly conference in Boston now gathers doctors and psychologists to discuss market strategy.
  • Our natural habitat. When markets move abruptly, Taleb feels the rush of adrenaline he would feel at a leopard prowling near his trading desk; colleagues who break telephone handsets are closer still to our origins. Greek statues show men like us, and the Homeric heroes of 30 centuries ago were genetically identical to the pudgy man carrying groceries across a parking lot. We have been a species for about 130,000 years, mostly on the African savannah, and “civilized” for some 80 centuries, since settlements between south-eastern Syria and south-western Mesopotamia.
  • In a Fertile Crescent town 3,000 years ago, information came in small batches, you knew few people, and a crime had few possible suspects. Your defence would be simple: “I was not there as I was praying in the temple of Baal and was seen at dusk by the high priest”, and Obedshemesh son of Sahar had more to gain. The space of probabilities was narrow. It wasn’t religion that delayed probability theory until the gambling literature; we didn’t compute odds “because we did not need to.” We left that habitat much faster than our genes, which “have not changed at all”.
  • Kafka in a courtroom. “We are capable of sending a spacecraft to Mars, but we are incapable of having criminal trials managed by the basic laws of probability.” In the O.J. Simpson trial, the defence argued that at least four people in Los Angeles would share the DNA, and a Harvard lawyer that only 10% of men who beat their wives go on to murder them. The right question is conditional: of women murdered by their husbands, about half had been battered before. Evidence compounds: a 1 in 500,000 chance of innocence from the blood alone, combined with his being the husband and the other evidence, puts the odds against him at “several trillion trillion”. Taleb switched off the trading-floor television in disgust. “I am glad to be a trader taking advantage of people’s biases but I am scared of living in such a society.”
  • In Kafka’s The Trial, Joseph K. faces a system he can’t understand; Taleb fears a glib lawyer before a randomness-illiterate jury, caught between the Charybdis of the lawyer who knows no maths and the Scylla of the mathematician who misuses it.

Kahneman and Tversky

  • By Taleb’s reckoning, the most influential economists of the century, measured by citations, were not Keynes, Marshall, Samuelson or Friedman but two psychologists, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, who from the early 1970s tested students and professors and showed how they depart from rational choice. Their work founded behavioural finance and economics, in open contradiction with the neoclassical economics of efficient markets and rational expectations. That economics is normative, prescribing how people should behave, “clearly a self-contradictory concept” for a science; physics is positive, describing how things are observed to behave.
  • Neurologists are starting to confirm these results by studying patients with localised brain damage. Antonio Damasio’s Descartes’ Error and Joseph LeDoux’s The Emotional Brain show it. “We think with our emotions and there is no way around it.” Evolutionary psychology says “we are not designed to understand things. We are built only to survive and procreate.”
  • Examples of biases. Graduate students asked about a young woman with a liberal-arts education rated her more likely to be a feminist bank teller than a bank teller, which is impossible: the joint event can’t be likelier than either part. In a quiz from Deborah Bennett’s Randomness, doctors were told a disease strikes 1 in 1,000, the test has 5% false positives, and a random patient tests positive. Most said 95%. The answer is about 2%, and fewer than one in five got it right (see the figure).

We are option blind

  • A stock is at $100. A one-month option to buy it at $110 is worth $10 if the stock ends at $120, and nothing if it ends below $110. Say it costs $1: 90% of the time it expires worthless, and 10% of the time it is worth $10 on average. Its expected value is $1, its most likely value zero.
  • Option sellers collect steady premiums, the “flow”; buyers lose small most months and win big occasionally. “Option sellers, it is said, eat like chickens and go to the bathroom like elephants.” Most option traders are sellers, and when they blow up, “it is generally other people’s money”. Few people will accept losing $1 at most expiries to make $10 once in a while, which is why the rare event stays underpriced.

Probabilities and the media

  • Absence of evidence isn’t evidence of absence. If a researcher reports that Fluorouracil raises survival in upper-respiratory-tract cancer from 21 to 24 in 100 but that there is “no evidence of improved survival (as yet)”, a journalist writes that “Professor N.N. Taleb found evidence that Fluorouracil does not help”, and a doctor in Smalltown stops prescribing it.
  • CNBC at lunchtime. Taleb heard a commentator at his gym say the index was only 10% off its highs while the average stock was close to 40% off its own. There’s no contradiction: stocks peak at different times, and “the maximum of an average is necessarily less volatile than the average maximum”.
  • You should be dead by now. A TV guru said the average American lives to 73, so a 68-year-old can expect five more years. Wrong: life expectancy is conditional on having survived so far, and a healthy 73-year-old might expect nine more. The same confusion in reverse: “our operation has a mortality rate of 1%. So far we have operated on 99 patients with great success; you are our 100th, hence you have a 100% probability of dying on the table.”
  • The Bloomberg explanations. Headlines like “Dow is up 1.03 on lower interest rates” explain moves of under 0.01% with the Dow at 11,000, which is noise; Michael Bloomberg should stop paying journalists for commentary. A bike race across Siberia won by 1.3 seconds over 3,000 miles doesn’t warrant a story about the winner’s diet; a week’s margin would. Taleb, addicted to the terminal for everything else, would keep it and drop only the commentary.
  • Even a significant move needs a cause, and post hoc ergo propter hoc (it came after, so it was the reason) is no cause: if hospital A delivered 52% boys one year and hospital B 48%, you didn’t have a boy because of the hospital. Markets respond to a dozen factors at once, from interest rates and the yen to inflation and European stocks. A journalist would have to test each alone and together, check its stability, and drop any story whose confidence came in under 90%. No wonder Hume was obsessed with causality.
  • Taleb’s trick. His screen shows the percentage change of every relevant price, currencies top left, stock markets on the right, always in the same place, and he looks only at moves larger than usual. The reading is nonlinear: “a 2% move is not twice as significant an event as 1%, it is rather like four times.” Today’s 1.3-point Dow move has less than a millionth of the significance of the 7% drop of October 1997. Filtering methods: engineers separate a voice from telephone static by treating small changes in amplitude as likely noise, with the chance of signal rising steeply with size, using a “smoothing kernel” (Figures 11.1 and 11.2 show data before and after). Our ears and our brains can’t do this for themselves, least of all when pounded with unsmoothed journalism.
  • Confidence levels. A forecast of 60 degrees means different packing if the range is ±10 (Arizona) or ±30 (Chicago). “It is not the estimate or the forecast that matters so much as the degree of confidence with the opinion.”
  • An admission. “I consider myself as prone to foolishness as anyone I know … I was born to be fooled by randomness.”

Part III

Wax in My Ears: Living with Randomitis

In Book 12 of the Odyssey, warned by Circe, Odysseus has his sailors plug their ears with wax and has himself tied to the mast, so he can hear the Sirens without steering onto the rocks near Scylla and Charybdis. Taleb’s first lesson is not even to try to be Odysseus. “I can merely reach the rank of a sailor who needs to have his ears filled with wax.”

His epiphany: he isn’t intelligent or strong enough to fight his emotions, and he needs them for his ideas and his energy. “I am just like every single character whom I ridiculed in this book … The difference … is that I try to be aware of it.” “If my brain can tell the difference between noise and signal, my heart cannot.” The trick he is proudest of is the Odyssean mute command: the trading-room television runs CNBC all day with the sound off. Silent, the excited pundit looks ridiculous, like Bergson’s gentleman about to step on a banana skin. The idea came from listening, jet-lagged, to a speech in Cantonese, a language he doesn’t know: the orator lost his dignity. One bias, prejudice, can offset another, taking information seriously.

Chapter 12

Gamblers’ Ticks and Pigeons in a Box

Even a professional probabilist grows superstitions. You can’t argue your wiring away, so remove the temptation instead.

On gamblers’ ticks crowding up my life. Why bad taxi-cab English can help you make money. How I am the fool of all fools, except that I am aware of it. Dealing with my genetic unfitness. No boxes of chocolate under my trading desk.The book’s own summary

Taxi-cab English and causality

  • Early in his career Taleb worked at Credit Suisse First Boston on the block between 52nd and 53rd Streets, Madison and Park, and lived in a book-choked, otherwise bare apartment on the Upper East Side with nothing in the kitchen but a defective espresso machine.
  • One morning a cab driver who spoke no language Taleb knew, taxi-cab English included, overshot 53rd Street and dropped him at the 52nd Street entrance. That day turmoil in currencies gave him the best day of his young career. The next morning he caught himself telling a new driver to take him to the north-east corner of 52nd and Park, and in the elevator mirror saw he was wearing the same coffee-stained tie as the day before.
  • He found more of these gamblers’ ticks: his income had risen after he started wearing glasses he barely needed, so he kept them on. A spurious association from a tiny sample, and his expertise in hypothesis testing didn’t touch it. A brilliant former colleague went to Las Vegas so often that casinos comped his suites, and consulted a fortune teller before large trades, then tried to expense it.

The Skinner pigeon experiment

  • At 25 Taleb thought superstition was cultural and would yield to reason. But he was growing more sophisticated and more superstitious at once, so it had to be biological.
  • In 1948 the Harvard psychologist B.F. Skinner set his box to deliver food to hungry pigeons at random. Each bird developed its own ritual, one swinging its head against a corner, others spinning anti-clockwise, hard-wired to its feeding. We are not made to see events as independent; when A and B happen together we assume one causes the other. It is emotionally harder to reject a hypothesis than to accept one, and harder to act ignorant than smart. “It is very hard for us to just shut up.”

Philostratus redux

  • Pyrrho, who preached indifference, lost his composure when chased by an ox and said it was hard to shed his humanity. Taleb’s computations register in his head but not in his conduct, like a doctor who knows the disease is 2% likely and treats it as 95%.
  • He can calculate that a $100,000 profit on a strategy gives only a 2% probability that it is genuinely profitable and 98% that it is noise, while a $1,000,000 gain makes it 99% likely. Yet he has leapt for joy at noise and despaired over results with no statistical significance. His energy comes from his emotions, so the answer is not to tame his heart.
  • Instead he denies himself his performance report unless it crosses a set threshold, just as he makes sure there are no boxes of chocolate under his trading desk. “It is the execution that is the problem, not the absence of knowledge.” He is tired of moralisers telling him to floss and eat his apple: “we are mere animals in need of lower forms of tricks, not lectures.”
  • Smokers show the gap best. Dozens of cancer nurses smoke outside the service entrance of Memorial Sloan-Kettering while patients are wheeled in for treatment.

Chapter 13

Carneades Comes to Rome: On Probability and Skepticism

Probability began as a measure of belief among the sceptics. Hold opinions loosely, change them without shame, and distrust scientists more than science.

Cato the censor sends Carneades packing. Monsieur de Norpois does not remember his old opinions. Beware the scientist. Marrying ideas. The same Robert Merton helping the author start his firm. Science evolves from funeral to funeral.The book’s own summary

Carneades comes to Rome

  • Ask a mathematician to define probability and he shows you how to compute it. For the ancients it was a subjective, fluid measure of belief, “not about the odds, but about the belief in the existence of an alternative outcome, cause, or motive.”
  • Around 155 BC Carneades of Cyrene came to Rome as one of three Athenian envoys asking the Senate to cancel a fine. He represented the New Academy, the hotbed of ancient scepticism. One day he gave a spellbinding speech in praise of justice. The next day he refuted it just as convincingly, to the same audience.
  • Cato the Elder, the old censor, who had banned Greek rhetoricians from living in Rome, persuaded the Senate to send the envoys home before their argumentative spirit weakened the military culture of Roman youth.
  • Carneades never committed to his premises or conclusions. In rigorous scepticism he is rivalled only by al-Ghazali, Hume and Kant, and only Popper turned scepticism into a scientific method. The sceptics taught that nothing is certain but conclusions of varying probability can guide action.
  • Probabilistic argument goes back further, to Greek Sicily in the sixth century BC, where the first rhetorician, Korax of Syracuse, taught people to argue from the most probable: in the absence of evidence, land should go to the person whose name it is best known by. His indirect student Gorgias took the method to Athens.
  • Probability, the child of scepticism. The Romans had flexible, syncretic superstitions rather than one truth. After monotheism, the West waited a dozen centuries for critical thinking; in the Middle Ages the Arabs were the critical thinkers, and after the Renaissance the roles reversed. Cicero preferred probability to certainty, which let him contradict himself, and Taleb respects him for it. The Paris students of 1968 wrote on the walls: “We demand the right to contradict ourselves!”

Monsieur de Norpois and path dependence

  • In Proust, the diplomat Monsieur de Norpois contradicts his earlier view on a Franco-German rapprochement and doesn’t remember holding it. Proust mocks him: “One forgets rather quickly what one has not thought about with depth.” Taleb’s objection: Proust never considered that Norpois might simply have changed his mind. We are supposed to be faithful to our opinions, or be traitors.
  • Norpois should have been a trader. Nigel Babbage, one of the best Taleb knows, will buy a currency on impulse hours after arguing it will fall, and doesn’t feel he owes anyone an explanation.
  • George Soros is the public example. In Jean-Manuel Rozan’s autobiography, disguised as a novel, Rozan plays tennis in the Hamptons with “Georgi Saulos”, who is bearish one weekend. The market rallies to record highs; asked if he was hurt, Saulos says “We made a killing. I changed my mind. We covered and went very long.” Later Soros gave Rozan $20 million to start a trading firm (Taleb was almost dragged into it) and pulled it days later, after a lunch in Paris, without explanation. Real speculators are free of their past actions: “Every day is a clean slate.”
  • We may be built for loyalty to ideas we have invested in, as to children, or to a spouse no one re-evaluates each morning at 8 a.m. An academic famous for a view won’t devalue it; people who switch parties are traitors, and apostates were once put to death. Some researchers find purely rational behaviour linked to damage in the amygdala. “Could Soros have a genetic flaw that makes him rational as a decision maker?”

Computing instead of thinking

  • Probability entered mathematics through gambling, where the rules are known and odds computable. Nature gives no such rules: “we do not even know how many colors there are” in the deck. Yet an industry of “risk measurers” measures risks anyway, especially when paid to.
  • Harry Markowitz received the economics prize “which in fact is not even a Nobel Prize”, since the Swedish central bank grants it in Nobel’s memory, for computing future risk as though markets had defined rules. A cab driver laughed at the idea.
  • For Taleb, an “immediate result” was the near-collapse of the financial system in the summer of 1998 through Long-Term Capital Management, a Greenwich fund whose principals included two more laureates, Robert Merton (the man who attacked Shiller in Chapter 3) and Myron Scholes. They made no allowance for their methods being wrong, and afterwards blamed a “rare event” and the vultures who pounced on them. “How did they know it was a rare event?”
  • Their failure put Taleb on the map. Capital started flowing to people who did the opposite of what they did, and Merton and Scholes “caused the birth” of his firm, Empirica. His lesson from Soros: start every meeting by convincing everyone “that we are a bunch of idiots who know nothing and are mistake prone, but happen to be endowed with the rare privilege of knowing it.”

From funeral to funeral

  • “Science is great, but individual scientists are dangerous.” They have human biases, perhaps more, since stubbornness is what gets you through 18-hour days on a thesis, which is “defended” like a lawyer’s case. “It was said that science evolves from funeral to funeral.” After LTCM a new financial economist will emerge who absorbs the lesson; the old ones will resist, but they are closer to their funeral dates.

Chapter 14

Bacchus Abandons Antony

Randomness has the last word. What’s left to you is how you behave: Stoicism as dignity, not as a stiff upper lip.

Montherlant’s death. Stoicism is not the stiff upper lip, but the illusion of victory of man against randomness. It is so easy to be heroic. Randomness and personal elegance.The book’s own summary

  • When the French writer Henry de Montherlant was told he would lose his sight to a degenerative disease, he took his own life, “the end that becomes a classicist”. The Stoics, and the Epicureans too, taught that we always have one option against fate: to choose between no life and the life we are given. Suicide was recommended only in narrow circumstances and was otherwise cowardice.
  • Heroism can be small. Epic heroes were judged by their actions, not the results. “No matter how sophisticated our choices, how good we are at dominating the odds, randomness will have the last word. We are left only with dignity as a solution — dignity defined as the execution of a protocol of behavior that does not depend on the immediate circumstance.” Grace under pressure; refusing to toady; fighting a duel; or telling a person you’re smitten with that the slightest snub and they will never see you again.
  • Before Mediterranean monotheism, the ancients didn’t believe their prayers could change destiny. Their world was full of invasions and reversals, so they needed practical rules for dealing with luck.

Notes on Jackie O.’s funeral

  • C.P. Cavafy, an Alexandrian Greek civil servant with a Turkish or Arabic surname, wrote almost a century ago, in a mix of classical and modern Greek, a lean poetry that Taleb thinks escaped fifteen centuries of Western sentimentality, from Dickens’s novels to Verdi’s operas. Taleb came to him through his Grecosyrian poems.
  • Maurice Tempelsman, Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis’s last companion, read Cavafy’s “The God Abandons Antony” at her funeral. Antony has lost to Octavius and been deserted by Bacchus, his protecting god (by legend even his horse went over to the enemy). The poem tells him to bid farewell to Alexandria, the city he is losing, not to mourn his luck, not to pretend his eyes and ears deceive him, and to listen “while shaken by emotion but not with the coward’s imploration and complaints.”
  • “No stiff upper lip. There is nothing wrong and undignified with emotions.” What matters is following the dignified path: Stoicism “is the attempt by man to get even with probability.” Taleb can’t resist adding that Cavafy, dying of throat cancer decades later and deprived of his voice by surgeons, fell into undignified fits of crying and clung to visitors to keep them from leaving.
  • Stoicism was founded by a Phoenician Cypriot, Zeno of Citium, and by Roman times had become a system of virtues, virtue being its own reward: wisdom, upright dealing and courage, a model like the Victorian gentleman. Cato took it to the point of disdaining feelings. Seneca’s Letters from a Stoic is the humane version, and Taleb hands it to trader friends. (Seneca, too, took his own life when cornered by fate.)

Randomness and personal elegance

  • Advice and sermons don’t work against our wiring, and self-help books are largely useless. Stoicism works because it plays on dignity and personal aesthetics, which are in our genes. Practise sapere vivere, knowing how to live, in every circumstance.

Twenty-five years on

What held up

The book makes few dated forecasts, but several of its claims could be tested by what came after 2001–04. Where the evidence comes from outside the book, it is from general knowledge and should be checked before being relied on.

Held

“Safe” strategies hide a blow-up Ch. 1, 5, 6

In August 2007 Goldman Sachs’s finance chief David Viniar said the firm was “seeing things that were 25-standard deviation moves, several days in a row”, Henry’s “ten sigma” in a new suit. The 2008 crisis ruined firms that had earned steady spreads on mortgage paper. In February 2018 the short-volatility note XIV lost over 90% of its value in a day and was shut down. Each was the John pattern: small steady gains, one large loss.

Held

Shiller against George Will Ch. 2–3

The Nasdaq fell about 78% from its March 2000 peak to October 2002, and the S&P 500 roughly halved. Shiller shared the 2013 economics Nobel with Eugene Fama, the leading efficient-markets theorist, and Lars Peter Hansen, which says the argument between them was not settled.

Held

Most star records are luck Ch. 8–9

S&P’s SPIVA persistence scorecards repeatedly find that few top-performing active funds stay on top, roughly what chance predicts. Eugene Fama and Kenneth French (“Luck versus Skill in the Cross-Section of Mutual Fund Returns”, 2010) found little evidence that more than a few funds had enough skill to cover their costs. Brad Barber and Terrance Odean (2000) found the most active individual traders did worst.

Held

Test enough rules and one will fit Ch. 9

Campbell Harvey, Yan Liu and Heqing Zhu counted 316 published return “factors” in 2016 and argued most were probably false discoveries, proposing a far higher bar for significance. The replication crisis in psychology and medicine, including the Open Science Collaboration’s 2015 attempt to repeat 100 studies, made the “dog that did not bark” a mainstream worry.

Partly

Bell-curve risk models will fail Ch. 6, 13

Value-at-Risk, which assumes well-behaved tails, failed badly in 2007–08, and the Basel Committee’s market-risk rules (finalised 2016) replaced it with expected shortfall, which looks further into the tail. But banks still run statistical risk models, and the replacement still measures from the past, as Chapter 6’s mischievous child would predict.

Held

“The market never falls 20% in three months” Ch. 7

The chapter’s example of a statement that no amount of data can prove: the S&P 500 fell about 34% in 23 trading days in February–March 2020, and about 57% from its 2007 peak to March 2009.

Partly

Behavioural economics wins Ch. 11

Kahneman won the economics Nobel in 2002 (Tversky had died in 1996), and Richard Thaler in 2017; Kahneman’s Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011) made the field famous. But parts of the bias literature failed to replicate, and Taleb himself later turned on it: in Skin in the Game (2018) he argued that many “biases” are rational once you account for the risk of ruin, siding in part with Gerd Gigerenzer.

Open

Buying rare events pays Ch. 6, 11

Taleb’s own fund, Empirica, closed in the mid-2000s. Universa Investments, run by his former colleague Mark Spitznagel with Taleb as an adviser, reportedly made very large gains on its tail hedges in March 2020. Whether paying for protection pays over full cycles is still argued (see Debates).

Held

Winner-take-all through network effects Ch. 10

Brian Arthur’s increasing returns became the standard account of platform markets. The dominance of Google, Facebook and Amazon, and the long reign of Windows that Taleb cites, fit the Polya-process picture better than a random walk.

Missed

The book’s own slips Ch. 9

Taleb says John Dos Passos received the Nobel Prize in literature; he never did. It’s a small error, but in a chapter about trusting the praise on book jackets.

Context

Where it has been argued with

Does it leave room for skill?

Warren Buffett’s talk “The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville” (1984) answered the coin-flipping argument before Taleb made it: if the winners of a national coin-flipping contest among 225 million orangutans came disproportionately from one zoo, you would want to know what that zoo fed them. Buffett’s winning investors came from one intellectual village, Graham and Dodd’s. Taleb’s claim is narrower than critics suppose: “I never said that every rich man is an idiot”, only that without more information he reserves judgement. Michael Mauboussin’s The Success Equation (2012) turned the question into a luck–skill continuum, with investing near the luck end over short periods.

Does tail protection pay?

Taleb’s posture, paying small costs for protection against rare disasters, depends on the price. Over long calm stretches the premiums add up. In a public argument in 2020–21, AQR’s Cliff Asness questioned whether tail hedging pays for itself over time, and Mark Spitznagel of Universa, where Taleb is an adviser, defended it, including in his book Safe Haven (2021). The disagreement is partly about how to account for the crashes that haven’t happened yet, which is Chapter 7’s problem.

Efficient markets

Taleb sides with Shiller against the University of Chicago. The Nobel committee split the difference in 2013. Efficient-market theorists can also claim Chapter 9: if most managers’ records are luck, that is what an efficient market predicts, and it is why index funds grew so large.

Was LTCM Markowitz’s fault?

Taleb calls the 1998 collapse an “immediate result” of Markowitz’s theory. Most accounts, such as Roger Lowenstein’s When Genius Failed (2000), blame leverage, crowded convergence trades and the liquidity panic after Russia’s default more than portfolio theory. The New York Fed organised a rescue by fourteen banks, putting in about $3.6 billion. The shared point is Taleb’s: the fund treated its models’ worst case as the real worst case.

Biases, or rational rules?

The book follows Kahneman and Tversky in treating departures from probability theory as errors. Gerd Gigerenzer (Simple Heuristics That Make Us Smart, 1999) argued that simple rules often beat calculation in an uncertain world. Taleb later moved toward that view: in Skin in the Game (2018) he argued that avoiding ruin can make “irrational” caution correct, drawing on the physicist Ole Peters’s work on ergodicity economics.

The style

The book is anecdotal, digressive and openly contemptuous of journalists, economists and MBAs, and its characters are composites. Taleb’s rule of writing only what he had witnessed or worked out himself, with no library research, is its strength and its weakness: vivid, but hard to check, and occasionally wrong (the Dos Passos Nobel). Admirers find the voice bracing; other readers find it self-regarding. Taleb anticipates the charge himself: he is, he says, as fooled as anyone.

Terms of art

Glossary

Lucky fool
Someone who got a disproportionate share of luck and credits it to a precise reason of their own.
Alternative histories
The outcomes a decision could have produced but didn’t. Taleb’s yardstick for judging it.
Generator
The process that produces results, as opposed to the results you happen to see.
Sample path
One realised sequence of random events over time. History is one sample path among many possible.
Monte Carlo simulation
Generating many random paths from the same odds to see the range of what could happen. Named after the casino; developed at Los Alamos.
Blow up
To lose more than you ever expected, to the point of being thrown out of the business.
Ergodicity
Here, the tendency of very long sample paths to resemble each other, so that luck washes out over time.
Noise and signal
Random fluctuation and real information. The shorter the time scale, the more of what you see is noise.
Survivorship bias
Drawing conclusions from the winners who remain visible while the losers have left the sample.
Cross-sectional problem
At any one time the richest traders are those best fitted to the last cycle, often the worst for the next.
Firehouse effect
Marty O’Connell’s term: people who talk only to each other come to agree on things an outsider would find absurd.
Skewness
Lopsided outcomes, where one side is rarer but much larger. It makes frequency a poor guide to expected value.
Peso problem
A strategy that looks safe only because its disaster hasn’t yet appeared in the data.
Stationarity
The assumption that the process generating data doesn’t change. The “mischievous child” who swaps the balls breaks it.
Problem of induction
Hume’s point that past observations can’t prove a general rule about the future.
Black swan
In this book, the single observation that refutes a rule built on many confirmations. It became Taleb’s next book.
Naive falsificationism
Taleb’s stance: act on theories, but know in advance what would refute them, and make sure no refutation can ruin you.
Data mining, data snooping
Testing so many rules on past data that some fit perfectly by chance.
Adverse selection
The investments that come looking for you are disproportionately those that did well by luck.
Birthday paradox
In a room of 23 people there’s about a 50% chance two share a birthday. Coincidences are commoner than they feel.
Polya process
A random process in which each success raises the odds of the next, producing runaway winners.
Network externalities
A product becomes more valuable as more people use it, so an early lead can lock in.
Option blindness
Undervaluing payoffs that are usually zero and occasionally large.
Premium seller
A trader who sells options for steady income, and “eats like a chicken and goes to the bathroom like an elephant”.
Conditional probability
The probability of something given what you already know. Neglecting it produces the 95% answer where 2% is right.
Gamblers’ ticks
Superstitious habits formed by linking random good outcomes to incidental actions.
Path dependence of beliefs
When the first idea you held decides which you keep. Test: would you buy the painting today?
Odyssean mute command
Taleb’s trick of watching financial TV with the sound off, so the pundits lose their authority.
Stoicism
Not the stiff upper lip but dignity in conduct whatever the outcome: “the attempt by man to get even with probability.”

Using it

A reading path

The chapters are essays and can be read out of order. If you want the core fast:

  1. Prologue and Chapters 1–3

    The table of confusion, Nero and John, Russian roulette and the dentist: the whole argument in miniature.

  2. Chapters 5–6

    Carlos and John blowing up, then skewness. The chapter that changes how you think about bets.

  3. Chapter 7

    Swans, Niederhoffer, Popper and Pascal: the book’s philosophy, and the seed of The Black Swan.

  4. Chapters 8–9

    Survivorship, the coin-flipping managers and the mysterious letter.

  5. Chapters 12–14 and the Epilogue

    How to live with it: pigeons, Carneades, Cavafy and Nero’s end.

Read alongside

  • Nassim Nicholas Taleb, The Black Swan (2007): the rare-event argument, fully developed. Then Antifragile (2012) and Skin in the Game (2018).
  • Daniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011): the psychology behind Chapter 11, from its source.
  • Roger Lowenstein, When Genius Failed (2000): the LTCM story behind Chapters 5 and 13.
  • Robert Shiller, Irrational Exuberance (2000): the book George Will mocked.
  • Michael Mauboussin, The Success Equation (2012), and Leonard Mlodinow, The Drunkard’s Walk (2008): gentler, more systematic tours of luck versus skill.
  • Karl Popper, Objective Knowledge, and Marcus Aurelius, Meditations: both summarised on this site, the Popperian and Stoic sides of the book.
  • Seneca, Letters from a Stoic, and C.P. Cavafy, “The God Abandons Antony”: the texts Chapter 14 hands to traders.
