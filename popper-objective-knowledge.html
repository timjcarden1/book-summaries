Objective Knowledge

An Evolutionary Approach

“I think I have solved a major philosophical problem: the problem of induction.”

The opening of Chapter 1

Karl Popper · Clarendon Press · 1972

The whole book in one breath

Popper’s solution to the problem of induction is not a better way to justify generalising from experience. It is the claim that we never generalise from experience at all. We guess, and then we try to destroy the guess. Knowledge grows from problems, through bold conjectures, by eliminating errors, to new and better problems, and the same cycle runs through evolution, perception and science. Observation never comes first: a theory or interest tells us what to look for, like a searchlight rather than a bucket. Once a guess is written down it becomes an objective thing, part of a “World 3” of theories and arguments that exists outside anyone’s head, where it can be criticised and allowed to die in our place. We can never be certain, but we can get closer to the truth.

Origins

Vienna, 1919: two theories with opposite structures

Popper was seventy when this book appeared, and it collects papers from 1965 to 1971. Nearly everything in it develops positions he held since his twenties, formed in interwar Vienna. Born in 1902 to a family of Jewish descent, he left school early, worked as a cabinetmaker’s apprentice, trained as a teacher and took a doctorate in the psychology of thought.

In 1919, at seventeen, he broke with Marxism after police shot unarmed young socialists at a demonstration. What appalled him was how easily the theory absorbed the deaths as a necessary stage, confirmed rather than tested. The same year, Eddington’s eclipse expedition tested Einstein’s prediction that starlight would bend by a specific amount near the sun. Popper saw that the two cases had opposite structures, and spent his life on the difference.

He is often called a member of the Vienna Circle, and wasn’t. The Circle’s logical positivists held that a statement means something only if experience can verify it. Popper disagreed about nearly everything, and Otto Neurath called him “the official opposition.” Where they sought a criterion of meaning, Popper sought a criterion of scientific status, and he insisted that unscientific doesn’t mean meaningless. His 1934 Logik der Forschung reached English only in 1959, which is why English-speaking philosophy met his ideas out of order.

The idea he is known for

What makes a theory scientific

A theory belongs to empirical science if some conceivable observation would count decisively against it, and its author can say in advance what that observation is. It is not a theory of meaning, not a theory of truth, and not a claim that unfalsifiable ideas are worthless.

Underneath observations there is no bedrock. Popper’s “basic statements” are themselves theory-laden and accepted by agreement among investigators. His image, from The Logic of Scientific Discovery, is science as a building on piles driven into a swamp: we stop driving when the piles seem firm enough for now. What holds the structure up is not a foundation but the continual attempt to knock it down, which is why he called his position critical rationalism.

If you remember nothing else

Seven ideas

Ch. 1

There is no induction, and nothing is lost

We never reason from instances to laws. We guess laws and hunt for the instance that kills them. The asymmetry between confirmation and refutation does all the work.

Appendix

Observation is always selective

“Observe!” isn’t an instruction anyone can follow. A theory, interest or problem must say what counts as data.

Politics

Ask how to find errors, not who has authority

The question about the sources of knowledge is a leftover of asking who should rule. Ask how to arrange things so errors are found fast.

Ch. 5

The best theories are the least probable

Content, testability and explanatory depth rise together as probability falls. Safety and informativeness pull in opposite directions.

Ch. 7

Progress means better problems

The problem you end with is never the one you started with. A field that stops producing harder problems has stopped.

Ch. 3

Knowledge exists outside heads

Once a guess is written down it becomes an object others can attack. That, for Popper, is what language is for.

Ch. 7

Let the theory die instead of you

The one-line summary of the book, and why Popper thought his epistemology was also a moral and political position.

The book, in order

Nine essays and an appendix

A collection of lectures and papers rather than a book written straight through, so it repeats itself. The argument runs from induction through realism and World 3 to evolution and truth.

Chapter 1

Conjectural Knowledge: My Solution of the Problem of Induction

Hume was right about the logic and wrong about the psychology.

  • Hume showed that no number of observed cases can justify a universal law, and then explained our confidence as habit. Popper accepts the logic and rejects the psychology: we don’t form expectations by repetition, we bring them to experience, and experience’s job is to knock them down.
  • The key is an asymmetry. No number of singular observations entails a universal statement, but one contradicts it. Many white swans can’t prove all swans are white, and one black swan disproves it.
  • Popper splits the problem into answerable pieces. We can’t justify believing a theory true, but we can justify preferring one theory to another because it has survived severer tests.
  • That preference is what he calls corroboration: a report on past performance, explicitly not a forecast or a probability of future truth.

Chapter 2

Two Faces of Common Sense

For commonsense realism; against the commonsense theory of knowledge.

  • Common sense is right that there is a real world independent of us, and Popper defends realism against idealists and positivists.
  • Common sense is wrong about how we know that world. It pictures the mind as a bucket filled by the senses, and that “bucket theory” is the root of the problem of induction.
  • Truth and certainty come apart. Truth is objective and absolute, while certainty is a feeling that is easy to have and worth nothing. Popper keeps the first and abandons the second.

Chapter 3

Epistemology Without a Knowing Subject

The knowledge that matters isn’t a mental state.

  • Since Descartes, epistemology has asked about a knowing mind: what do I believe, and am I justified? Popper says that is the wrong object of study.
  • He distinguishes three worlds (see the figure below). The content of a theory, its logical consequences and the problems it leaves open belong to World 3. They exist whether or not anyone has noticed them.
  • His test case: destroy all machines and all human know-how but keep the libraries, and civilisation can restart. Destroy the libraries too, and it can’t.
  • World 3 is made by us, yet partly autonomous. We invent the natural numbers, and the primes and unsolved problems about them follow without our leave. That is a precise sense in which mathematics is both invented and discovered.

Chapter 4

On the Theory of the Objective Mind

How to understand a text, an action or a historical event.

  • The German tradition (Dilthey, and Collingwood in England) said understanding a past agent means re-living their mental states through empathy. Popper thinks that is exactly wrong, and it makes the humanities look unrigorous for no reason.
  • His alternative is a World 3 operation: reconstruct the problem situation, meaning the problem the agent faced, their background assumptions, the options open to them and what would have counted as a solution. Then you can argue, with evidence, about whether their move fitted the problem.
  • He calls this situational analysis, with a “rationality principle” (the agent acted appropriately to the situation) as a deliberate simplification. When an explanation fails, suspect the reconstruction of the situation first.

Chapter 5

The Aim of Science

To find satisfactory explanations. Prediction is a by-product and a testing device.

  • Popper sets himself against essentialism, which seeks ultimate explanations and so becomes a stop sign, and against instrumentalism, which treats theories as calculating tools that are neither true nor false.
  • An instrument can’t be refuted: a hammer that fails is just the wrong tool. If theories were instruments, anomalies would be notes on their limits rather than discoveries about the world, and criticism would lose its point.
  • Science seeks theories of ever greater depth and content. Since more content means lower probability, high probability can’t be the aim. The safest theory says as little as possible, the opposite of good science.

Chapter 6

Of Clouds and Clocks

Human freedom and rationality in a world that is neither pure clockwork nor pure chance.

  • Physical systems range from regular “clocks” to disorderly “clouds.” Classical physics treated all clouds as secretly clocks. Popper argues all clocks are, in some degree, clouds.
  • Neither determinism nor randomness explains free, rational action. Pure chance is no more freedom than pure clockwork.
  • His motive is moral as much as physical. If determinism were true, arguing with someone would just be pushing their atoms about, and persuasion and manipulation would be the same thing. He wants a world in which reasons can be causes without being compulsions: plastic control, by ideas, of behaviour.

Chapter 7

Evolution and the Tree of Knowledge

From the amoeba to Einstein, one method.

  • All knowledge, biological, perceptual and scientific, grows by the same cycle of trial and error elimination.
  • The difference between the amoeba and Einstein is that the amoeba’s errors die with it, while Einstein can state his theory, criticise it and let it die instead of him.
  • Language makes a theory a World 3 object outside the organism, something that can be examined, attacked and discarded. Popper thinks this is the largest change in the history of life.
  • Where the tree of life branches outward into ever more specialised forms, the tree of knowledge grows the other way: many specialised problems converge toward unified theories.

Chapter 8

A Realist View of Logic, Physics, and History

Realism applied to three fields.

  • Logic is best understood as the theory of valid transmission of truth from premises to conclusion, and of falsity back from conclusion to premises: the logic of criticism.
  • Physics aims to describe a real world, against instrumentalist readings of quantum mechanics.
  • History is understood through reconstructing problem situations, as in Chapter 4. Technical in places, and safe to skip on a first reading.

Chapter 9

Philosophical Comments on Tarski’s Theory of Truth

Why Popper stopped being embarrassed about truth.

  • Until the 1930s Popper was uneasy about truth as correspondence to the facts, which seemed empty or paradoxical. Alfred Tarski’s 1933 work showed how to define truth for a formal language without contradiction, by speaking about it in a richer metalanguage.
  • The schema (“snow is white” is true if and only if snow is white) looks trivial, and that is the point: correspondence can be stated precisely and consistently.
  • Popper builds on it his idea of verisimilitude, or closeness to the truth: a theory can be false yet closer to the truth than its rival. His formal definition of it was later shown not to work (see the objections below).

Appendix 1

The Bucket and the Searchlight: Two Theories of Knowledge

The most readable thing Popper wrote, and the picture at the top of this page.

  • The bucket theory: the mind is an empty container filled by the senses, and knowledge is what accumulates.
  • The searchlight theory: we always approach the world with expectations, questions and theories, and these light up what we notice. Every experiment answers a question and every instrument is built to detect something specific.
  • Popper once began a lecture by telling the audience simply to “observe” and write down what they saw. The instruction fails, because observation needs a chosen object, a task and a point of view.

Context

Why this is also a political theory

Popper never separated his epistemology from his politics, worked out in The Open Society and Its Enemies (1945). Both fields made the same error: asking a question about authority when they should ask about correction.

The wrong question

Who is the authority?

Knowledge
What are the sources of knowledge: reason, the senses, authority?
Politics
Who should rule: the wisest, the best, the majority?

The right question

How do we correct errors?

Knowledge
How can we find out whether a belief is wrong? Its pedigree is irrelevant.
Politics
How can bad rulers be removed without violence, and limited while in office?

Popper to Deutsch

David Deutsch says plainly that his epistemology is Popper’s. Nearly every foundation of The Beginning of Infinity (no induction, fallibilism, conjecture and criticism, problems as the engine) is in this book first.

Deutsch’s “hard to vary” criterion is stricter than testability. It rules out carefully stated conspiracy theories and just-so stories that Popper’s criterion admits. See the Deutsch summary on this site.

Context

What was built on it

  • George Soros studied under Popper at the LSE and has long said his approach to markets is applied Popper: state why a market is mispriced, say in advance what would prove you wrong, and close the position when it arrives. His concept of reflexivity, where beliefs change the facts they are about, marks where markets strain Popper’s schema. His Open Society Foundations take their name from Popper’s other book.
  • Evolutionary epistemology. Donald Campbell’s “blind variation and selective retention” carried Chapter 7’s argument into psychology and the study of creativity.
  • Where the word got worse. “Unfalsifiable” became a debating weapon for ruling claims out of court, which inverts Popper’s intent twice: he denied unfalsifiable ideas are nonsense, and he never meant the test to be applied sentence by sentence.
  • On an ordinary Monday. Write the problem as a question, write two or three rival answers including one you dislike, name for each the observation that would kill it, and go looking for that observation. Beware the ad hoc rescue, an amendment that saves a theory by making it say less.

Context

The standing objections

You can’t falsify a single hypothesis

The Duhem–Quine problem: a test confronts a theory together with assumptions about instruments and conditions, and logic alone doesn’t say which to blame. Popper answered with methodological rules against ad hoc rescues, but rules aren’t deductions. This is the deepest crack.

Scientists don’t behave this way

Thomas Kuhn observed that anomalies are usually tolerated and theories dropped only when a replacement exists. Imre Lakatos tried to rescue Popper with research programmes that protect a hard core. Popper replied that he describes what makes science rational, not what scientists happen to do.

If corroboration says nothing about the future, why act on it?

Wesley Salmon pressed hardest: either corroboration carries forward-looking weight, and something like induction is back, or it doesn’t, and Popper has no account of rational action. We do board the plane. This is the objection Popper answered least well.

Verisimilitude failed

David Miller and Pavel Tichý showed in 1974 that Popper’s formal definition of closeness to truth doesn’t work, and Popper conceded. Later repairs exist and none commands agreement, which matters because “closer to the truth” does real work in his account.

World 3: trivial or mysterious?

If it just means what books and arguments say, it is an unremarkable way of talking. If it means more, it needs a metaphysics Popper never supplies.
