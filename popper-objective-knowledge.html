Objective Knowledge

An Evolutionary Approach

“Of course, I may be mistaken; but I think that I have solved a major philosophical problem: the problem of induction.”

The opening sentence of Chapter 1

Karl Popper · Clarendon Press · 1972, revised 1979

The whole book in one breath

Popper’s answer to Hume is not a better way to justify generalising from experience. It is the claim that nobody, animal or scientist, ever generalises from repeated experience at all. We guess, and then we try to kill the guess, and we are entitled to prefer the guess that has survived the hardest tests, though never to call it certain. Knowledge grows from problems, through tentative theories and error elimination, to new and deeper problems, and the same cycle runs from the amoeba to Einstein. The difference is that Einstein can write his theory down. Once written, a theory becomes an object in a “third world” of theories, problems and arguments, made by us yet partly independent of us, where it can be criticised and allowed to die in our place. Common sense is right that the world is real and wrong that knowledge is poured into the mind like water into a bucket; observation is a searchlight that a question switches on. Truth is correspondence with the facts, which Tarski showed can be said without paradox, and science aims to get nearer to it. The world is neither pure clockwork nor pure chance, and our ideas steer our bodies by a flexible, “plastic” control. The book ends, in the 1979 edition, with Popper admitting that his formal definition of nearness to truth had failed.

Origins

A book of papers written over 23 years

Popper turned seventy in the year this book appeared, and it gathers work from almost his whole career. He was born in Vienna in 1902, left school early, served an apprenticeship as a cabinetmaker, trained as a teacher and took a doctorate in the psychology of thought under Karl Bühler, whom the book still calls “my late teacher”. His first book, Logik der Forschung (1934), reached English only in 1959 as The Logic of Scientific Discovery, which is why English-speaking philosophy met his ideas out of order.

He is often filed with the Vienna Circle and was in fact its most persistent critic; Otto Neurath called him “the official opposition”. The Circle wanted a criterion of meaning. Popper wanted a criterion of scientific status, and he insisted that an unscientific idea need not be meaningless. He spent the war years in New Zealand, where, the book notes, the images of the bucket and the searchlight were born, and from 1946 he taught at the London School of Economics.

The preface is signed at Penn, Buckinghamshire, on 24 July 1971, and the book is dedicated to Alfred Tarski. Popper says he left each essay very nearly as it was written, overlaps included, which is why Chapters 3 and 4 speak of the “first, second and third world” while Chapter 2 uses “world 1, 2 and 3”, a numbering he took from the neuroscientist John Eccles. A footnote admits that Chapter 2 was written before Chapter 1. The revised edition of 1979 added corrections, many suggested by David Miller, and a second appendix answering the critics of the first edition.

What the preface says

Human knowledge, Popper begins, is “no doubt the greatest miracle in our universe”, and he doubts the book solves it. But he hopes to restart a discussion stuck for three centuries. Since Descartes, Hobbes and Locke, including Hume and even Reid, the theory of knowledge has been subjectivist: knowledge was a specially secure kind of belief. The essays break with that “commonsense theory of knowledge”, which he calls a subjectivist blunder going back to Aristotle, while holding to the “essential truth of commonsense realism”. In its place he offers an objective theory of essentially conjectural knowledge, and says he does not apologise for the boldness of the claim.

The idea he is known for

What makes a theory scientific

The book assumes the reader knows Popper’s older solution to what he called the problem of demarcation, which he says he formulated and solved in the winter of 1919–20 and did not think worth publishing. A footnote to Chapter 1 gives it in one line: a statement is empirical if some finite set of observation statements could contradict it. An isolated existential claim such as “there exists a sea-serpent somewhere in the world at some time” is therefore not empirical, though it may still shape an empirical problem.

It is not a theory of meaning, not a theory of truth, and not a claim that unfalsifiable ideas are worthless. In this book Popper calls realism itself metaphysical and irrefutable, and still argues for it at length; he calls a large part of Darwinism “a logical truism” and still builds on it.

Underneath observation there is no bedrock. Observation statements are themselves conjectural and “theory-impregnated”, and Chapter 2 ends with the image that all our knowledge is “built on sand; but its foundations can be improved by critically digging deeper”. What holds the structure up is not a foundation but the continuing attempt to knock it down, which is why Popper called his position critical rationalism. He adds that “in this context I know no better synonym for ‘rational’ than ‘critical’.”

If you remember nothing else

Twelve ideas

Ch. 1

There is no induction, and nothing is lost

No number of observations proves a law, but one can refute it. That asymmetry is enough to choose between theories, so we never need an inference from instances to laws.

Ch. 1

Corroboration looks backward

A theory’s corroboration is a report on how it has stood up to tests so far. It says nothing about how it will do tomorrow, and it is still rational to act on the best-tested theory.

Ch. 2

Common sense has two faces

It is right that the world is real and wrong that knowledge comes in through the senses. Keep the realism; replace the theory of knowledge.

Ch. 2

Truth yes, certainty no

Truth is correspondence with the facts and the aim of inquiry. Certainty is a feeling that depends on what is at stake, and the quest for it has to be abandoned.

Ch. 2 · 9 · A2

Nearer to the truth

A false theory can be closer to the truth than another, as Einstein’s is to Newton’s. Popper’s formal definition of this failed, and he said so in 1978; the idea survives.

Ch. 3

Knowledge without a knower

The knowledge science deals in is not a state of anyone’s mind. It is theories, problems and arguments in books and libraries, which exist whether or not anyone is thinking them.

Ch. 3 · 4

Man-made, yet autonomous

We invent the natural numbers; the primes and their unsolved problems follow without our leave. World 3 is our product and it outgrows us.

Ch. 4

Understand by reconstructing the problem

To understand Galileo, or an emperor’s edict, reconstruct the problem situation he faced. Reliving his mental states is optional.

Ch. 5

The best explanations correct what they explain

Newton explained Kepler and Galileo by contradicting them. A theory that corrects the laws it explains has gone deeper, and cannot have been induced from them.

Ch. 6

All clocks are clouds

The world is neither clockwork nor dice. Human freedom needs something in between: ideas that steer us by a flexible, “plastic” control.

Ch. 3 · 6 · 7

Let our hypotheses die in our stead

The amoeba dies with its mistaken expectation. We can write ours down, criticise it and discard it, and survive our errors. That is what language and science add to evolution.

App. 1

Observation is a searchlight

We do not have observations; we make them, because a question tells us where to look. Theory always comes first.

The book, in order

Nine essays and two appendices

The first two essays restate and defend the solution of the problem of induction and the realism behind it. The next two introduce World 3 and apply it to understanding in history and the humanities. Three more turn to science and evolution: the aim of science, freedom and determinism, and the growth of knowledge as natural selection. Two defend realism in logic and physics and Tarski’s theory of truth. The first appendix is the oldest piece in the book; the second, from 1978, answers its critics.

Chapter 1 · 1971

Conjectural Knowledge: My Solution of the Problem of Induction

Hume was right about the logic and wrong about the psychology. Restated in objective terms, the problem has a solution, and it is negative: we never justify a theory, but we can justify preferring one.

The claim, and the motto

  • The chapter’s motto is from Bertrand Russell: “The growth of unreason throughout the nineteenth century and what has passed of the twentieth is a natural sequel to Hume’s destruction of empiricism.” Popper means to show that the sequel was not necessary.
  • He says he reached his solution “in 1927 or thereabouts”, having started on induction in 1923; he had solved the problem of demarcation in the winter of 1919–20. He first published the solution in a note in Erkenntnis in 1933 and in Logik der Forschung (1934). Few philosophers accept it, he complains; many books on induction ignore his work or criticise views he never held. The chapter is meant as a full answer to his critics.
  • He calls the traditional question, “What is the justification for the belief that the future will be (largely) like the past?”, wrongly put twice over. The future is not like the past except in a sense so loose it says nothing, and asking how inductive inference is justified already assumes there is such a thing.

Hume’s two problems

  • The commonsense theory of knowledge, which Popper also calls “the bucket theory of the mind”, says there is nothing in the intellect that did not enter through the senses. (Popper traces the idea to Parmenides, who stated it satirically.) Its answer to “how do our expectations arise?” is repetition: we believe the sun will rise tomorrow because it always has. Philosophers since Aristotle and Cicero have called this origin-plus-justification epagogē, induction.
  • Hume’s logical problem: are we justified in reasoning from repeated instances we have experienced to instances we have not? Hume’s answer is no, however many the repetitions, and no again if we ask only for probable conclusions.
  • Hume’s psychological problem: why, then, do all reasonable people expect the unexperienced to resemble the experienced? His answer is “custom or habit”: conditioning by repetition and the association of ideas.
  • The two answers clash, and the clash turned Hume, “one of the most rational minds ever”, into both a sceptic and a believer in an irrationalist theory of knowledge. Russell drew the moral in 1946: unless there is an answer to Hume, “there is no intellectual difference between sanity and insanity. The lunatic who believes that he is a poached egg is to be condemned solely on the ground that he is in a minority.”
  • Popper’s method is to translate the subjective vocabulary (belief, impression, justification of a belief) into an objective one (statement, test statement, justification of the claim that a theory is true). Having solved the logical problem, he carries the solution over to psychology by a “principle of transference”: what is true in logic is true in psychology. He calls this “a somewhat daring conjecture”. His conclusion is blunt: “there is no such thing as induction by repetition”; the idea is “a kind of optical illusion”.

The logical problem, restated three times

  • A negative answer to L1 means every law is a hypothesis. This was not yet the orthodox view: Gilbert Ryle argued in 1937 that “we are often sure, and warranted in being sure, of a law proposition.” Popper turned against that view because of Einstein: no theory was ever as well established as Newton’s, and Einstein taught us to see it as a conjecture.
  • His second case is Harold Urey’s discovery of heavy hydrogen in 1931. The atomic weights of hydrogen and oxygen were the very standards of chemical measurement, and every chemist would have staked his life on them; yet here was a refutation.
  • Even the three textbook examples of inductive knowledge turn out to have been refuted in the sense originally meant. The sun rises and sets once in 24 hours: refuted when Pytheas of Marseilles found “the frozen sea and the midnight sun”, and was disbelieved for centuries. All men are mortal: the Greek means “bound to die”, part of Aristotle’s theory that every generated creature must decay, refuted by bacteria, which divide rather than die (and cancer cells, which go on living). Bread nourishes: refuted when a French village was poisoned by ergot in its ordinary bread.
  • P. F. Strawson had written that if Hume posed the problem of induction, Hume also solved it, by showing our inductive habits are forced on us by nature: reason, Strawson paraphrased, “is, and ought to be, the slave of the passions”. Popper notes that Hume wrote “ought only to be”, and that a positive answer to induction is self-refuting: if our science is roughly true, it tells us we are clever animals in an improbable corner of the universe, with slim chances of finding true regularities.
  • Russell’s lunatic can now be answered. Some of the madman’s claims are refuted by experience; others are untestable, which is where the problem of demarcation comes in. And the result satisfies a modest empiricism: only experience can help us decide the truth or falsity of factual statements, although it can decide only falsity.

Preferring theories

  • Popper separates the theoretician, who seeks true explanatory theories, from the man of action, who must choose something to act on. The theoretician cares about falsity too, since refuting a theory establishes the truth of its negation and sets a new problem.
  • A new theory must succeed where its predecessor succeeded and also where it failed. The theoretician tries to catch false theories by severe tests, designing “crucial experiments” between rivals. Even so, elimination can never establish a theory’s truth, because the number of possibly true theories stays infinite.
  • The best theory at any time is the most testable one, which is also the one with the greatest informative content and explanatory power. Ad hoc explanations, not testable independently of what they explain, “can be had for the asking”.
  • He offers an objective mark of a very bold theory: it explains its predecessor while correcting it, so that it actually contradicts it and contains it only as an approximation. Newton’s theory contradicts both Kepler’s and Galileo’s, and Einstein’s contradicts Newton’s (Chapter 5 works this out).
  • In practice the relation between theory and evidence is less clean, and test statements themselves can be criticised. That is why science needs methodological rules, which Popper calls the rules of critical discussion, all serving its aim of getting nearer to the truth. Nothing guarantees progress.

Corroboration: the merits of improbability

  • Popper’s preference has nothing to do with preferring the more probable hypothesis. Testability grows with informative content, and content grows as probability falls, so the better hypothesis is usually the less probable one. (He denies he ever proposed an “improbability criterion”, as John Harsanyi said in 1960, and notes that even Rudolf Carnap, the leading inductive logician, had come to accept that content equals improbability.)
  • He introduced the “degree of corroboration” to show that every probabilistic theory of induction is absurd. His definition: “a concise report evaluating the state (at a certain time t) of the critical discussion of a theory”, covering how it solves its problems, how testable it is, how severe its tests were and how it stood up to them.
  • It is comparative and a report of past performance only; it “says nothing whatever about future performance, or about the ‘reliability’ of a theory.” Readers who took his phrase about a theory proving “its fitness to survive” as a forecast are told that “it was not I who mixed the Darwinian metaphor”: every species that ever died out had survived until then.
  • A weak statement can borrow corroboration from a strong theory it follows from. “The sun rises in Rome once in every twenty-four hours” is poorly testable on its own, but well corroborated as a consequence of Newton’s theory and the earth’s rotation.
  • What separates him from the inductivists is emphasis on negative arguments (counter-examples, refutations, criticism) against their “positive instances”. Anything positive in science is positive only relative to negative methods.

Pragmatic preference

  • The man of action must choose, since inaction is also action. Popper’s two pragmatic questions get two answers. On which theory should we rely? On none, since none has been shown true. Which should we prefer as a basis for action? The best-tested one.
  • That choice is rational in the most obvious sense he knows: “I do not know of anything more ‘rational’ than a well-conducted critical discussion.” But it is not rational in the sense of giving good reasons to expect success. There are none, and that is exactly Hume’s result.
  • Even if our physics were true, the world as we know it could disintegrate in the next second; Popper notes he said so “before Hiroshima”. Most such disasters are beyond action and so not worth worrying about, though he refuses to count atomic war among them.
  • So “no theory of knowledge should attempt to explain why we are successful in our attempts to explain things.” Our own cosmology says the universe is almost empty, filled with chaotic radiation, dust and very hot stars; conditions where knowledge can arise are almost infinitely improbable and cosmically brief.

The psychological problem

  • Popper says he solved the psychological problem first. Watching animals and children, he saw an “immensely powerful need for regularity” that makes them find regularities even where there are none and cling to expectations dogmatically. Kant was right that the intellect imposes its laws on nature, but wrong not to notice how often it fails: the laws are psychologically prior to experience, not valid in advance of it.
  • The logical point is that repetition presupposes similarity, and similarity presupposes a point of view, which is to say a theory or expectation. So expectations cannot come from repetition. This, he says, is what made him give up psychology for the logic of discovery.
  • Trial and error elimination is what the psychology of learning looks like once the logic is transferred to it. Hence the book’s most famous comparison: “the main difference between Einstein and an amoeba (as described by Jennings) is that Einstein consciously seeks for error elimination. He tries to kill his theories.” The amoeba cannot criticise its expectations, because they are part of it.
  • “Only objective knowledge is criticizable”: subjective knowledge becomes objective when we say what we think, “and even more so when we write it down, or print it.”
  • Popper is not a “belief philosopher”; he cares about theories, not about whether anyone believes them. “This is why, like E. M. Forster, I do not believe in belief.” He grants a range of expectation, from the lively expectation of a dog about to go for a walk to the schoolboy who knows but does not really believe that he will one day be old.
  • Two restated psychological questions get two answers. Do we feel certain even of our best theories, on reflection? No: the sun may explode in the next half-hour; we ignore that only because we can do nothing about it. Are our strong practical beliefs the irrational product of repetition? No: they are partly inborn and partly shaped by trial and error, and acting on the results of science is not irrational, because no method is more rational than critical discussion.

Beyond induction and demarcation

  • Every “principle of induction” fails. If it is meant to justify our preference among theories it is unnecessary; if it is meant to justify reliance on regularities it is false, even paradoxical, because the science it would license tells us regularities are rare and short-lived. Critics complain he has not justified inductive inference. “Of course I have not.”
  • What must go is the search for justification; the search for truth stays. Truth is a “regulative idea”: “We test for truth, by eliminating falsehood.” Popper protests at an encyclopedia entry of 1967 that summed up his view as “Truth itself is just an illusion.”
  • The first formal statement of demarcation was too simple, because any theory can be protected from refutation, “immunized”, to use the German philosopher Hans Albert’s word, which Popper adopts in place of his own “conventionalist stratagem”. Hence the need for methodological rules against immunizing. Yet he also saw “the value of a dogmatic attitude”: someone has to defend a theory, or it will succumb before it has contributed anything.
  • The chain of consequences he lists runs through the rest of the book: all observation is theory-laden; all languages are “theory-impregnated”; language has an argumentative function; deductive logic is the organon of criticism, retransmitting falsity from conclusions back to premises; only formulated theories are objective, which leads to the “third world”.

Chapter 2 · 1970

Two Faces of Common Sense: An Argument for Commonsense Realism and Against the Commonsense Theory of Knowledge

The longest essay in the book. Common sense is right that there is a real world and wrong about how we come to know it; replace its theory of knowledge with an evolutionary, objective one, and Hume’s problem dissolves.

An apology for philosophy

  • Popper opens by saying philosophy now needs an apology. Most professional philosophers have lost touch with reality, and he quotes a joke he credits to the playwright Rolf Hochhuth: “The Marxists have merely interpreted Marxism in various ways; the point, however, is to change it.” (He dissociates himself “most emphatically” from Hochhuth’s attitude to Churchill.)
  • “The greatest scandal of philosophy”, he writes, is that while the world of nature perishes around us, philosophers go on debating whether that world exists. Scholasticism, to him, means arguing without a serious problem.
  • The anti-intellectualism of the National Socialists, now returning among disillusioned students, is only a little worse, and it is provoked by the intellectuals’ own treason: “If you feed them stones instead of bread, the young people will revolt, even if in so doing they mistake a baker for a stone-thrower.”
  • His excuse for philosophy: “We all have our philosophies, whether or not we are aware of this fact, and our philosophies are not worth very much. But the impact of our philosophies upon our actions and our lives is often devastating.” So they need improving by criticism.

Starting from common sense, insecurely

  • All science and philosophy must start from common sense, not because it is secure but because any part of it can be criticised later (the flat earth was part of it once). His slogan: “all science, and all philosophy, are enlightened common sense.” His first thesis: our starting point is common sense, and our great instrument of progress is criticism.
  • An objection says criticism needs some other theory taken dogmatically as its base. Popper answers that criticism needs only competing theories: we explore the consequences of each and look for weak points, which often shows the weakness of both.
  • Descartes wanted a secure starting point and found it in his own existence. Popper does not doubt his own existence either, but finds it much too narrow a platform, and not as indubitable as Descartes thought. His example is from Hugh Ruttledge’s Everest 1933: the Sherpa Kipa, who went too high, and whose “bewildered mind still held doggedly to the idea that he was dead”. (Popper adds that he had a similar experience, for a few seconds, when struck by lightning on the Sonnblick in the Austrian Alps.)
  • Locke, Berkeley, Hume, Russell and Moore all took observational experience as the secure base; Thomas Reid thought we perceive external reality directly. Popper replies that nothing in experience is direct or immediate. It is all decoding, learned so well that it feels immediate, as reading does to a practised reader or Morse code to a signaller. So “the quest for certainty, for a secure basis of knowledge, has to be abandoned”, and his problem becomes the growth of knowledge.

Realism, and the arguments for it

  • Common sense distinguishes appearance from reality, and recognises many sorts of reality: foodstuffs (which, Popper guesses, give us our sense of reality), stones and people, but also a toothache, a word, a highway code, a novel, a government decision, a valid proof, forces and regularities.
  • Realism can be neither proved nor refuted. Idealism, the view that the world is my dream, cannot be refuted either: whatever you do to convince me, including kicking me, I can say I dreamt it. But realism can be argued for, and the arguments are “overwhelmingly in its favour”.
  • First, realism is part of common sense, and every argument against it rests on the weakest part of common sense, the bucket theory. Second, almost every physical, chemical or biological theory implies realism (Popper himself calls realism metaphysical, being untestable). Third, language is descriptive and argumentative, and a description is always of some state of affairs; this argument is “as strong as reason itself”. Fourth, idealism implies that my mind created this beautiful world; but the beauty of Rembrandt’s self-portraits is not in my eye, nor that of Bach’s Passions in my ear. “Denying realism amounts to megalomania (the most widespread occupational disease of the professional philosopher).” Fifth, if realism is true, it explains why realism cannot be proved: our knowledge is a fallible adaptation to reality.
  • He closes with the two men he calls “the greatest of our time”, Einstein and Churchill. Einstein saw no “metaphysical danger” in accepting the objects of physics. Churchill, in My Early Life (1930), answered cousins who argued at university that nothing exists except what we think: astronomers predict by mathematics that a black spot will cross the sun on a certain day, we look and see it, and “we have taken what is called in military map-making ‘a cross bearing’.” The data could in principle be gathered by automatic calculating machines, and the sun is “as hot as Hell”, and if the metaphysicians doubt it, “they should go there and see.”
  • Popper calls this “the philosophically soundest and most ingenious argument against subjectivist epistemology that I know”, notes that Churchill is missing from every anthology of epistemology and from the Encyclopedia of Philosophy, and suggests that subjectivism may in future be ignored.

Truth, content and nearness to truth

  • “Our main concern in philosophy and in science should be the search for truth. Justification is not an aim; and brilliance and cleverness as such are boring.” Aiming at simplicity and lucidity is “a moral duty of all intellectuals: lack of clarity is a sin, and pretentiousness is a crime.”
  • He accepts the commonsense theory, refined by Tarski, that truth is correspondence with the facts. Using a metalanguage we can say that the German statement “Der Mond ist aus grünem Käse gemacht” corresponds to the facts if and only if the moon is made of green cheese. Truth is absolute but gives no criterion: Tarski proved that no general criterion of truth exists for a language rich enough to contain arithmetic. “We are seekers for truth but we are not its possessors.”
  • The content of a statement is everything that follows from it. Its truth content is its true consequences (tautologies apart); its falsity content, suitably defined, its false ones. A theory T₂ is nearer the truth, has more “verisimilitude”, than T₁ if more true statements follow from it without more false ones, or as many true and fewer false.
  • His example: to every question Newton’s theory answers, Einstein’s gives an answer at least as precise, and it answers questions Newton’s cannot. So Einstein’s has greater content, is potentially the better theory before any test, and challenges us to test it; without it no one would have measured star positions during an eclipse or the red shift of white dwarfs. It has more verisimilitude unless its falsity content is greater too, which is what testing tries to find out.
  • A 1974 footnote records that Pavel Tichý, John Harris and David Miller had shown falsity content grows with content, the result that sank his formal definition (see Appendix 2).
  • Verisimilitude is a better aim than truth alone: “all tables are tables” and 1 + 1 = 2 are true and no scientific achievement, and Newton’s theory, though probably false, is a far better approximation because of its immense true content. Wilhelm Busch’s rhyme, in Popper’s translation, says the same: “Twice two equals four: ’tis true, / But too empty and too trite. / What I look for is a clue / To some matters not so light.”
  • Two false statements can differ in truthlikeness. If it is 9.48 p.m., “It is now 9.45” seems nearer the truth than “It is now 9.40”; replace each by an interval (“between 9.45 and 9.48”) and the first really does entail more truth. (A note of 1978 adds, “But David Miller does not think so.”)
  • He stresses that all appraisals of theories are conjectural, and that he never hoped to put numbers on verisimilitude, except the limiting cases 0 and 1. Precision is not a value in itself: “we should never try to be more exact or precise than the problem before us requires.” His aim was to do for nearness to truth what Tarski did for truth: rehabilitate a commonsense notion that had fallen under suspicion. He compares the task to Neurath’s image of rebuilding a ship at sea.

What is wrong with the bucket

  • The commonsense theory of knowledge says: to learn about the world, open your eyes and ears; the senses are the sources of knowledge. Popper draws it as a bucket, filled through the senses (or through a funnel from above), where the material accumulates and is digested. Philosophers call it the tabula rasa, the blank slate. It survives in theories of teaching, in information theory and above all in behaviourism and the conditioned reflex.
  • On this theory, knowledge is made of thing-like items (ideas, impressions, sense data); the unprocessed “given” is certain; all error comes from our bad digestion of the given; expectations come from associations strengthened by repetition. This is, near enough, the empiricism of Locke, Berkeley and Hume.
  • Its central mistake is what John Dewey called the quest for certainty. Sense data “do not exist at all. They are the inventions of hopeful philosophers, who have managed to bequeath them to the psychologists.” As children we learn to decode a chaotic flood of signals by trial and error, on the basis of inborn dispositions, and become so good at it that the result feels given. A well-trained photographer rarely misjudges an exposure, but that is training, not a standard of truth.
  • Subjective instrumentalism refutes itself: physiology, which it accepts as a perfect instrument of prediction, predicts that our “data” are fallible. Russell had argued that physics shows naive realism false, because an observer of a stone is “really” observing the stone’s effects on himself; the argument impressed Einstein. Popper says it is as bad as saying that a reader of Russell is observing the effects of Russell on himself and so not reading Russell.
  • The theory is pre-Darwinian. Most dispositions are inborn (breathing, swallowing) or mature under the environment’s influence (learning a language). Popper goes further than most: “there is no such thing as association or conditioned reflex. All reflexes are unconditioned”; the supposedly conditioned ones are inborn reflexes modified by eliminating false starts.
  • The crucial distinction is between subjective knowledge, dispositions and expectations, which changes by eliminating its carrier, and objective knowledge, linguistically formulated expectations open to criticism, which changes by eliminating the conjecture while its carrier survives.

Sketch of an evolutionary epistemology

  • The term “evolutionary epistemology” is due, he says, to his friend the psychologist Donald T. Campbell; the idea goes back to J. M. Baldwin, C. Lloyd Morgan and H. S. Jennings at the end of the nineteenth century. Popper insists the leading ideas are logical, not factual, though the examples come from biology.
  • For Descartes and Berkeley truth was guaranteed by the origin of our ideas, ultimately by God, and in Locke, Berkeley and even Hume and Reid there are traces of the view that ignorance is sin. Popper reverses the order: a theory may feel immediately true because it is true and our equipment is adapted to it; immediacy is never a reason to believe it.
  • A central problem of evolutionary theory is that “survival of the fittest” comes close to a tautology, since the fit are identified as those that survive. What is empirical is that the environment has structure and changes, but not too fast. The method of trial and error elimination belongs to the logic of the situation.
  • Language made a difference of kind. Formulating a theory lets us criticise and eliminate it “without eliminating the race which carries them”; a conscious, systematic critical attitude then gives the method of science. “The amoeba dislikes erring while Einstein is intrigued by it.”
  • Knowledge never starts from nothing, only from background knowledge and problems, which arise when expectations clash with findings. If it were not absurd to estimate, Popper would say that “999 units out of 1,000 of the knowledge of an organism are inherited or inborn.” His “fundamental theorem”: all learning is the modification, possibly the rejection, of some knowledge or disposition already present, and in the last instance of inborn dispositions.
  • Even the senses are theory-impregnated: “there is no sense organ in which anticipatory theories are not genetically incorporated.” A cat’s eye is built to distinguish a few biologically important situations, and with them the theory that these are the situations that matter. What counts as input at all depends on the organism’s inborn programme.
  • So there are two kinds of knowledge: subjective, or better “organismic”, knowledge, consisting of dispositions; and objective knowledge, the logical content of theories, conjectures and guesses in journals, books and libraries (and perhaps in the genetic code). Popper names world 1 (physical), world 2 (conscious experience) and world 3 (the logical contents of books, libraries and computer memories), and claims that our full consciousness of self depends on world 3 theories about our body, time and memory, which animals lack.

Certainty

  • The deepest weakness of the commonsense theory is the demand that knowledge be belief with sufficient reasons for certain truth. By that standard only logic and finite arithmetic would count, and the whole of natural science, being conjectural, would not be knowledge at all; “conjectural knowledge” would be a contradiction in terms.
  • Certainty depends on the situation, not on the strength of a belief. Glancing at a reliable, ticking watch, Popper is certain enough that it is about eight o’clock. Given 20 pence in change at a bookshop, he is sure the coins are genuine, since inflation has made forging tenpenny pieces unprofitable. If a lot depended on it he would ask a bank teller, and if a man’s life depended on it he would try to get the Chief Cashier of the Bank of England to certify the coin.
  • The subjectivist theory that degrees of belief can be measured by betting odds (usually credited to Frank Ramsey, though Popper says it is in Kant) is “incredibly naive”. With his hands in his pockets he is sure he has five fingers on each hand; if his best friend’s life hung on it, he would take them out and look.
  • So “absolute certainty” is a limiting idea. Outside simple proofs, objective certainty does not exist. Science is less certain than ordinary life because it turns background assumptions into problems, yet its theories are far better tested than much that we trust our lives to, such as that the floor will not collapse. “Theories are true or false and not merely instruments. But they are of course instruments also.”

The method of science, and a verdict on it

  • “The method of science is the method of bold conjectures and ingenious and severe attempts to refute them.” A bold conjecture has more content than the theory it hopes to replace. Since truth content grows automatically with content, the empirical contest is really over falsity content. Popper borrows Churchill’s remark that wars “are never won but always lost”: we can never be sure a theory has won, only find where it loses.
  • We can never rationally justify a theory, but “if we are lucky” we can justify preferring one of a set of competitors, for the time being. Wesley Salmon objected that either this preference is purely logical, in which case no new predictions can come from it, or some step is “ampliative” and so inductive. Popper replies that the predictions come from the chosen hypothesis, which is synthetic; the reasons for choosing it can be logical without making the hypothesis true.
  • He calls science “one of the greatest creations of the human mind”, comparable to descriptive language or writing. Knowledge grew long before criticism, in the genetic code. But “only science … replaces the elimination of error in the violent struggle for life by non-violent rational criticism, and … allows us to replace killing (world 1) and intimidation (world 2) by the impersonal arguments of world 3.”

An afterthought on induction

  • Popper points out that he has described knowledge and scientific method without mentioning induction at all. “Induction is a muddle.”
  • He says he was the first to call the problem of induction “Hume’s problem”, in 1934, a name that then spread. Kant had used “Hume’s problem” for causation and then for the whole question of synthetic truths known a priori. Popper split Kant’s problem: on causation he disagreed with Hume and Kant alike; on the logic of induction he agreed with Hume completely; on its psychology he disagreed. His summary: induction is invalid because it leads either to an infinite regress or to apriorism.
  • Hume was a commonsense realist driven by his bucket theory into an idealism he could not live with, a “schizophrenia” Popper finds in the famous passage where Hume says that only carelessness and inattention cure scepticism, and that an hour later the reader will be persuaded there is an external world after all. Hume’s logical arguments are “the logical gems buried in the psychological mud of the bucket”.
  • The problem of induction is deeper than the problem of causation, because a conjectured law plus initial conditions supplies the “necessary link” between cause and effect that Hume looked for: the logical necessity of a deduction.
  • Kant saved Newton’s rationality by claiming the principle of causality was valid a priori, and by his “Copernican revolution”: the intellect imposes its laws on nature. The theory collapsed once Newton’s dynamics was seen to be a marvellous conjecture. But much of Kant survives: our laws are “genetically a priori though not a priori valid”, imposed on nature and often failing.
  • Hume’s paradox was that even our intellect is irrational, since we live by habit, which reason cannot defend. Russell thought this paradox caused the “schizophrenia of modern man”. Popper’s solution: we do not act on repetition but on the best-tested theories, for which we have good reasons, not reasons to think them true but reasons to think them the best available. To Hume’s question, do we act according to reason? “we can; and we do if we are reasonable.”
  • What looks like habit is really jumping to conclusions, like the “imprinting” Konrad Lorenz described, and clinging to them. Habit formed by repetition would be Lamarckian instruction; bold hypotheses tested against the world are Darwinian selection.
  • The textbook regularities are false: biologists have kept a chicken’s heart beating in a dish for more than half a century, a French village was nearly wiped out by its own bread, and the horse-drawn vehicles seen in London every day for centuries have vanished. We are commonsensically certain the sun will rise over London tomorrow; we do not know it.
  • This is not scepticism in the modern, pessimistic sense: “One will hardly describe as a sceptic a man who believes in the possibility of the unlimited growth of knowledge.” Of Hume’s four theses (induction is invalid; we do rely on a non-chaotic world; that reliance is irrational; human nature is irrational) Popper accepts the first two and rejects the last two, because realism is a conjecture that no one has criticised effectively.
  • Finally he dissects an old argument: a theory whose improbable predictions all come true must be almost certainly true. It cannot be right, because Newton’s and Einstein’s theories would each get a probability near 1, and since they contradict each other, the probability of “Newton or Einstein” would come out near 2. The success is due not to truth but to truthlikeness, and a high degree of corroboration can be read as a sign of verisimilitude “as it appears at the time t”.

Chapter 3 · 1967

Epistemology Without a Knowing Subject

The theory of knowledge has been studying the wrong thing. Scientific knowledge is not a state of anyone’s mind but a world of theories, problems and arguments, made by us and largely autonomous of us.

Three worlds, and the libraries

  • Popper opens by confessing that “although I am a very happy philosopher I have, after a lifetime of lecturing, no illusions about what I can convey in a lecture”, so he will not try to convince, only to challenge and provoke.
  • He distinguishes three worlds: physical objects and states; states of consciousness, or dispositions to act; and “the world of objective contents of thought, especially of scientific and poetic thoughts and of works of art.” The third has something in common with Plato’s Forms and Hegel’s Objective Spirit, more with Bolzano’s “propositions in themselves”, and most with Frege’s “objective content” of thought.
  • He means to provoke the “belief philosophers”, Descartes, Locke, Berkeley, Hume, Kant and Russell, who study subjective beliefs and their origins. The most important inmates of the third world are not beliefs but problems, theories and critical arguments, the “state of a discussion”, and the contents of journals, books and libraries.
  • Two thought experiments. In the first, all machines and tools are destroyed, together with all our know-how, but libraries and our ability to learn from them survive: after much suffering, the world gets going again. In the second, the libraries are destroyed too, and civilisation does not re-emerge “for many millennia”.
  • Three theses follow. Traditional epistemology, from Locke to Russell, which studies “I know” and “I believe”, is irrelevant to scientific knowledge (so is much modern epistemic logic, unless it disclaims any interest in science). What is relevant is the study of a largely autonomous third world of problems, conjectures and arguments. And studying that world throws much light on scientists’ minds, while the converse is not true.
  • Knowledge in this objective sense is “totally independent of anybody’s claim to know”: it is “knowledge without a knower: it is knowledge without a knowing subject.” Frege had already said it: “I understand by a thought not the subjective act of thinking but its objective content.”
  • Ordinary English blurs the two senses. “I know that Fermat’s last theorem has not been proved, but I believe it will be proved one day” is about a mind; “taking account of the present state of metamathematical knowledge, it seems possible that Fermat’s last theorem may be undecidable” and “I certify that this thesis is an original and significant contribution to knowledge” are about the third world.
  • Three supporting theses: the third world is a natural product of the human animal, “comparable to a spider’s web”; it is largely autonomous though we constantly act on it; and objective knowledge grows through our interaction with it, in close analogy with biological evolution.

A biological approach

  • Biologists study animal behaviour, but also the structures animals make: spiders’ webs, wasps’ nests, badgers’ burrows, beavers’ dams, paths through a forest. There are two kinds of problem: how the animal builds (production) and the structures themselves (their materials, geometry, adaptation and feedback on the builder).
  • Popper’s theses, restated: the distinction matters; the products matter more than the production; and “we can learn more about production behaviour by studying the products themselves than we can learn about the products by studying production behaviour.” That is an anti-behaviourist and anti-psychologistic claim.
  • The subjective approach looks more scientific because it is causal. But every science reasons from effects back to causes, so starting from the product is not teleological.

Objective and autonomous

  • A book, it is said, is nothing without a reader. But a wasps’ nest is a wasps’ nest after the wasps have left, and a bird’s nest is one even if no bird ever lived in it. A computer could print a book of logarithms to fifty decimal places that nobody ever consults; each figure is still objective knowledge.
  • “A man who reads a book with understanding is a rare creature.” What makes paper with marks into a book is its potential to be understood, or misunderstood; even readers from outer space, finding our libraries after we had gone, could decipher it.
  • Popper’s garden had a nesting-box. For years the birds ignored it; then blue tits inspected it and began to build, and gave up. It was an objective opportunity, a problem situation, whether or not any bird took it. So are ecological niches, and a bacteriologist’s culture dish.
  • Much of the third world is an unintended by-product, like a path trodden through the jungle by animals going to water. Language itself, like a bird’s nest, is an unintended product of actions aimed at other things, and once it exists it creates new needs and aims.
  • The standard example is arithmetic. “Pace Kronecker, I agree with Brouwer that the sequence of natural numbers is a human construction.” But the difference between odd and even is not our creation; it is an unintended consequence. So are the primes, and conjectures like Goldbach’s, which are hard facts we cannot influence. The autonomy is partial: new problems lead to new constructions, such as recursive functions, which create further unintended facts.
  • Here Popper introduces the schema P₁ → TT → EE → P₂: a problem, a tentative theory, error elimination by criticism or test, and a new problem that emerges “autonomously from the field of new relationships which we cannot help bringing into existence”. It explains how “we can lift ourselves by our own bootstraps”, and gives a rational sense to “emergence”.

Language, criticism and the third world

  • Following Karl Bühler, Popper distinguishes the functions of language. Animal and human languages share two lower functions, self-expression and signalling. Human language adds the descriptive function, with its regulative idea of truth, and, Popper’s own addition, the argumentative function (see Chapter 6’s figure).
  • Most philosophers overlook the higher functions because the lower ones are always present too, so any utterance can be “explained” as expression or communication.
  • Two points follow. Without an “exosomatic” descriptive language, one that grows outside the body like a tool, there is nothing to criticise; with one (and written language) a third world appears in which standards of rational criticism can develop. And we owe to these higher functions “our humanity, our reason. For our powers of reasoning are nothing but powers of critical argument.”
  • “We are workers who are adding to the growth of objective knowledge as masons work on a cathedral.” And its feedback on us is immense: “if anybody were to start where Adam started, he would not get further than Adam did.”
  • Belief philosophy cannot explain the decisive fact that scientists criticise and kill their theories. “Scientists try to eliminate their false theories, they try to let them die in their stead. The believer—whether animal or man—perishes with his false beliefs.”

Plato, Hegel, Bolzano, Frege, and the empiricists

  • “For all we know, Plato was the discoverer of the third world.” But his world was divine, unchanging and true, while Popper’s is man-made, changing and full of false theories and open problems. Plato also made its inmates concepts and essences, not theories, and offered them as ultimate explanations; from him descends the idea that the meaning of words matters as much as the truth of theories, which Popper calls Plato’s main error.
  • A table he uses more than once sets words, concepts, meaning and definitions on the left against statements, theories, truth and derivations on the right. The attempt to establish either meaning or truth by definition or derivation leads to an infinite regress; his thesis is that the left side is unimportant.
  • Hegel’s Objective Spirit changes, as Popper’s third world does, but in Hegel man is not creative: individuals are instruments of the Spirit of the Epoch. Hegel’s dialectic welcomes contradictions, where Popper’s schema works by eliminating them. And Hegel’s Spirit is conscious, a self; the third world is nothing of the kind.
  • Bolzano’s truths in themselves belong to the third world, though he was unclear about their relation to the rest; Frege drew the distinction clearly (and was, Popper says, the father of epistemic logic).
  • The British empiricists, he suggests, were driven by the question of religion against irreligion: could Christianity be justified as science was? That is why they treated knowledge as belief justified by the senses. Anyone who equates a proposition with the evidence for it ought to give up the law of excluded middle, since often neither p nor not-p is proved; nobody noticed this before Brouwer.

Homage to Brouwer

  • The Dutch mathematician L. E. J. Brouwer had died shortly before the congress, and Popper added a section to honour him. Brouwer took Kant’s view that arithmetic rests on a pure intuition of time. Popper objects that if non-Euclidean geometry destroyed Kant’s theory of space, special relativity destroyed his theory of time.
  • Kant needed intuition because he thought mathematics could not be merely discursive, yet Euclid’s geometry is obviously step-by-step argument. Brouwer resolved this ingeniously: mathematics is a mental construction in intuition, and language only communicates it afterwards. The theory solved problems about mathematical certainty, existence and proof in one stroke, and led to his rejection of the law of excluded middle and of indirect proof.
  • Popper praises two achievements: Brouwer saw that mathematics is made by man, and that mathematical objects must exist before we can talk about them. He criticises the rest. There are no authoritative sources of knowledge, intuition included; our intuition of time is shaped by language and theory (he cites Benjamin Lee Whorf on the Hopi, and optics, where there cannot be a state of the world at an instant).
  • Following Imre Lakatos, he argues that mathematics too grows by criticism of guesses and informal proofs, which needs language. Arend Heyting had admitted that “it has proved not to be intuitively clear what is intuitively clear in mathematics.” For proofs we want weak logical means; for criticism we want the strongest we have.

Subjectivism in logic, probability and physics

  • Epistemic logic formalises “a knows p” and “a believes p”. A scientist does neither. He tries to understand p, to think of alternatives to it and criticisms of it, proposes tests, tries to axiomatise or derive it, proposes new problems it raises and criticises his own latest solution. “We may doubt without criticizing, and criticize without doubting.”
  • Probability is still widely read subjectively, as a calculus of ignorance, by those who now call themselves Bayesians. Popper says he has fought this for thirty-three years; it springs from the idea that “I know that snow is white” has more dignity than “snow is white”.
  • Physics has been invaded since about 1926: first quantum mechanics, where opponents of the subjective view were “regarded as nitwits”; then statistical mechanics, after Leo Szilard proposed in 1929 that entropy is lack of knowledge; then information theory. He does not expect the tide to turn soon.

The logic and the biology of discovery

  • From the objective point of view, epistemology is the theory of the growth of knowledge: of problem-solving, and of the critical appraisal of competing conjectures. Before testing, theories are appraised by content and explanatory power, which go with testability; after testing, by how near to the truth they appear. A tautology is certain and has zero truth content.
  • “Science begins with problems.” Progress can be measured by the intellectual gap between the problem we started with and the new one left when the theory breaks down. The process “is not a repetitive or a cumulative process but one of error-elimination. It is Darwinian selection, rather than Lamarckian instruction.”
  • “Animals, and even plants, are problem-solvers.” Their tentative solutions are organs and behaviour, which correspond to theories, just as honeycombs and spiders’ webs correspond to tools. A new organ can turn a potential ecological niche into an actual one.
  • Sense organs incorporate theory-like expectations and are blind to what they do not expect. So classical epistemology, which takes sense data as given, “can only be described as pre-Darwinian”; there is no pure datum, just as there is no pure observational language.

Discovery, humanism and self-transcendence

  • The old view treated knowledge as the expression of mental states, “epistemological expressionism”, parallel to the expressionist theory of art. Popper grants that the third world is man-made but stresses that its impact on any of us, “even on the most original of creative thinkers, vastly exceeds the impact which any of us can make upon it.”
  • What matters most is the give-and-take between ourselves and our work, by which “we constantly transcend ourselves, our talents, our gifts.” This self-transcendence is “the most striking and important fact of all life and all evolution”. Theories, like children, become independent of their parents, and we may learn more from them than we put in.
  • Learning is imaginative criticism: inventing test situations and challenging our prejudices. “This is how we lift ourselves by our bootstraps out of the morass of our ignorance; how we throw a rope into the air and then swarm up it—if it gets any purchase, however precarious, on any little twig.”
  • There are three senses of “to learn” (to discover, to imitate, to make habitual), and all work by selection rather than instruction, though “Darwinism … simulates Lamarckism.” He ends with a conjecture: the genetic code may work by prohibiting certain chemical syntheses rather than by instructing (see what held up).
  • A bibliographical note of 1978 credits the Austrian philosopher Heinrich Gomperz, who drew the subjective–objective distinction in 1908, ten years before Frege’s “Der Gedanke”.

Chapter 4 · 1968

On the Theory of the Objective Mind

World 3 applied to understanding. To understand a text, an action or a historical figure, reconstruct the problem situation, a World 3 object, instead of trying to relive a mind.

Pluralism, and how the worlds interact

  • “Our main task as philosophers is, I think, to enrich our picture of the world by helping to produce imaginative and at the same time argumentative and critical theories.” Western philosophy has mostly been variations on body–mind dualism, with failed attempts at monism. Plato, the Stoics, Leibniz, Bolzano and Frege began a pluralism (Hegel, with his monistic tendencies, did not).
  • For Plato, the intelligible was as objective as the visible. The three worlds are related thus: 1 and 2 interact, and 2 and 3 interact, but 1 and 3 meet only through 2. The mind “sees” a number much as the eye sees a body.
  • That link is of the utmost importance, because World 3 transforms World 1: technologists apply consequences of theories that their inventors never imagined, in electrical power or atomic theory; economic theories decide whether a boat or an aeroplane is built. Popper suggests that psychology will one day have to be revolutionised by treating the mind as an organ for interacting with World 3.

The Stoics, and a man-made world that outgrows us

  • Plato, Popper thinks, did not see that propositions belong to the third world, true ones like “7 times 11 equals 77” and false ones like “7 times 11 equals 66”. The Stoics did. They saw that language belongs to all three worlds: as marks on papyrus or bronze (1), as an angry outburst (2), and as what is said, which may contradict what someone said yesterday (3). Their third world included problems, arguments, commands, prayers, treaties, poetry and narrative.
  • Philosophers split into two groups. Platonists accept an autonomous third world but make it divine and eternal. Locke, Mill, Dilthey and Collingwood see that language and what it expresses are man-made and so deny a third world; most students of the humanities are in this group.
  • Popper holds both halves: the third world is man-made and also, in a clear sense, superhuman. “It transcends its makers.” It is like honey, made by bees but unplanned.
  • “I believe (unlike Kronecker) that even the natural numbers are the work of men.” Yet there are infinitely many of them and infinitely many true and false equations, more than anyone will ever state, and problems about primes that we discover rather than make. Tarski, Mostowski and Robinson had shown that the true propositions of arithmetic cannot be axiomatised, so there will always be infinitely many unsolved problems. “There is no man who can master even a small corner of this world”, and there will always be room for original work.

Understanding is problem-solving

  • The central problem of the humanities, Popper suggests, is understanding World 3 objects. That departs from the dogma, accepted by almost all theorists of understanding (hermeneutics), that what we understand is mainly mental. An interpretation, as an object, is a theory: for example a historical explanation backed by arguments and documents.
  • Even the act of understanding is anchored in World 3, and almost everything worth saying about it concerns its relation to World 3 objects, with which we work “almost as if they were physical objects”, like a builder handling beams.
  • The activity follows the schema P₁ → TT → EE → P₂: a first tentative interpretation, criticised with the documents and against rival interpretations, which yields a new problem situation. Every problem arises against a background, which always includes a language full of theories (Popper cites Whorf again). A problem with its background is a “problem situation”; Lakatos’s “progressive” and “degenerating” problem shifts are World 3 relations too.
  • A trivial example. 777 times 111 equals 86,247. Popper admits he tried to hold the number in his head and confused it with 86,427. Understanding the equation means seeing the problem it answers, and checking it means a “paper and pencil operation”, a routine for eliminating errors, which shifts one hard multiplication into three easy ones and an addition. There are degrees of understanding, from merely grasping the words (one could equally “understand” a false equation) to understanding the problem and its background. Understanding a theory always turns out to be “a problem about a problem”.

Galileo’s theory of the tides

  • “The main aim of all historical understanding is the hypothetical reconstruction of a historical problem-situation.” Popper’s example is Galileo’s theory of the tides, which denied the moon any effect and for which Galileo has been attacked as dogmatic.
  • Galileo argued that the combination of the earth’s spin and its orbit makes a point on its surface move faster at midnight (orbital speed plus rotational speed) than at midday (orbital speed minus rotational speed); the periodic speeding up and slowing down would slosh the seas like water in a basin. Popper notes that the theory is plausible but wrong: apart from the constant centripetal acceleration there is no periodic acceleration.
  • Galileo’s real problem was not the tides but whether the earth moves. He hoped a successful theory of the tides would decide for Copernicus, whose “incredible daring and simplicity” (the earth and planets as moons of the sun) had long attracted him. He had seen a model of the solar system in Jupiter’s moons and had observed the phases of Venus predicted by the Copernican theory.
  • As a physicist he wanted a mechanical explanation, and he tried to build physics on just two conservation laws, inertia and the conservation of rotary motion. That is why he kept circular orbits despite knowing Kepler’s work, and he was right to push his narrow basis to the limit; Kepler’s ellipses were equally bold oversimplifications, luckily soon explained by Newton.
  • He rejected the moon’s influence because he opposed astrology, in which planets were gods (he reproves Kepler’s astrological “puerilities”), and because his conservation principle ruled out forces between heavenly bodies. Newton’s attraction was later thought occult by Berkeley and even by Newton himself. Psychological explanations such as ambition or jealousy become superfluous; Dilthey’s talk of a “mysterious circular movement” is a misreading.
  • Lessons: the reconstruction matters most for unsuccessful theories; it is itself a conjecture; understanding is a metaproblem on a higher level than Galileo’s own; and “the history of science should be treated not as a history of theories, but as a history of problem situations”, in which failed attempts may be as important as successes.
  • Popper calls the method “situational analysis”: an idealised reconstruction of the situation that makes an action “rationally understandable”, an application of the rationality principle. It can distinguish the situation as the agent saw it from the situation as it was, and so explain failures. Kepler saw his problem as a Pythagorean harmony of the world; Schrödinger did not see that his problem was statistical.
  • It extends to art. Popper cites E. H. Gombrich on artists as problem-solvers, and the story of Haydn, hearing the first chorus of his Creation, breaking into tears and saying, “I have not written this.”

The value of problems

  • To understand a theory we must understand its problem; but how do we understand a problem? Popper’s answer: “in order to understand any such ‘dead’ problem we must, at least once in our life, have seriously wrestled with some live problem.”
  • We learn to understand a problem by trying to solve it and failing, since understanding a problem means finding out where the difficulty lies, by seeing why the obvious solutions do not work. “When we have failed a hundred times, we may even become experts with respect to this particular problem.”
  • This, he thinks, is the key to the transfer of learning between fields. Those trained only to solve problems inside a given framework, the practitioners of what Thomas Kuhn calls “normal science”, will not transfer well; those who have wrestled with ill-formulated problems will. There are no subject matters, only problems.
  • Beethoven’s notebooks for the last movement of the Ninth Symphony show its introduction telling the story of a problem: how to break into words.

The humanities, and Collingwood

  • Dilthey and Collingwood held that the humanities differ from the natural sciences because their task is to understand, in the way we understand people. Popper accepts that understanding is the aim of the humanities but denies it is not the aim of science too. Einstein wrote to Born: “You believe in the dice-playing God, and I in the perfect rule of law within a world of some objective reality which I try to catch in a wildly speculative way.”
  • “Science, after all, is a branch of literature; and working on science is a human activity like building a cathedral.” The most important field of history may be the history of human knowledge, and only someone who understands science can understand its history, and vice versa. “Labouring the difference between science and the humanities has long been a fashion, and has become a bore.”
  • Some historians, he says, are guilty of “scientism”, copying a method science does not use: collecting documents and drawing conclusions. “A ticket collector collects documents, but he rarely collects historical evidence.” And avoiding error is a poor ideal: “it is from our boldest theories, including those which are erroneous, that we learn most.”
  • Collingwood’s historian, reading an emperor’s edict in the Theodosian Code, must envisage the situation as the emperor did and re-enact his reasoning. Popper agrees about the importance of the situation and disagrees about re-enactment. What matters is the analysis of the situation, a conjecture argued for with evidence; re-enactment is at most an intuitive check.
  • Often it is impossible. The act may be one of unbearable cruelty, supreme heroism or despicable cowardice, or an achievement beyond the historian’s powers: we cannot expect a historian to combine the gifts of Caesar, Cicero, Catullus and Theodosius, and “no historian of art can be a Rembrandt.” In trivial cases it is superfluous.
  • A long footnote on Bacon shows interpretation at work: Ranke mistranslated Bacon’s Latin caste (“modestly”) as keusch (“chaste”) and attributed the passage to the wrong preface.

Chapter 5 · 1957

The Aim of Science

The aim of science is satisfactory explanation, and that one aim drives science towards ever more testable and deeper theories, which explain their predecessors by correcting them.

What a satisfactory explanation is

  • Speaking of “the aim” of science sounds naive, since scientists have different aims. But science looks like a rational activity, and a rational activity must have an aim. Popper’s suggestion: “it is the aim of science to find satisfactory explanations, of whatever strikes us as being in need of explanation.”
  • The thing to be explained, the explicandum, is usually known. The explanation, the explicans, has to be discovered, so a scientific explanation is “the explanation of the known by the unknown.” (If the explicandum might be imaginary, like flying saucers, what needs explaining may be the reports.)
  • The explicans must entail the explicandum, must not be known to be false, and must be testable independently of it. The alternative is circularity. “Why is the sea so rough today?” “Because Neptune is very angry.” “What is your evidence that Neptune is angry?” “Don’t you see how rough the sea is?” Dissatisfaction with such explanations is “among the first fruits of the critical or rational approach”.
  • Independent testability alone is not enough, since one can bolt irrelevant testable claims onto an explanation (“these plums are juicy”, “all ravens are black”). Only universal laws plus initial conditions do the job, and only if the laws are testable. So the aim of explanation pushes towards theories of richer content, greater universality and greater precision; and since every explanation can itself be explained, the task renews itself.

Against ultimate explanation

  • “Essentialism” holds that science seeks ultimate explanations in terms of essences, beyond which no question arises. Descartes explained physics by the essence of body, extension; some Newtonians, following Roger Cotes, made inertia and attraction the essence of matter. Newton himself disagreed: in the Principia’s concluding scholium he says he has not found the cause of gravity itself and does not invent hypotheses.
  • The usual critics of essentialism were instrumentalists, for whom theories are only tools of prediction. Popper rejects both and calls his “third view” a “modified essentialism”, a phrase a reviewer applied to it. Every explanation can be further explained; “what is” questions about the essence of things must be abandoned; and inherent essences of individual things explain nothing, since they cannot say why different things behave alike.
  • Plato explained likeness by a common original Form, and we still explain similarity by common origin. Universal laws, which are neither in the things nor outside the world, are conjectural descriptions of the structural properties of the world. We can probe ever deeper into it, and every refutation of such a theory teaches us the unexpected and shows our theories are real claims about the world, “for they can clash with something we never made.”
  • “Depth” resists logical analysis (mathematical theorems are all equivalent given the axioms and still differ in depth). It seems to need rich content plus a certain coherence or “organicity”. Depth, coherence and even beauty guide intuition; content and tests decide a theory’s fate.

Newton contradicts Galileo and Kepler

  • Newton is often said to have induced his dynamics from Galileo’s and Kepler’s laws, or even deduced it. He could not have, because his theory contradicts both, and “neither a deductive nor an inductive inference can ever proceed from consistent premises to a conclusion that formally contradicts the premises.” Popper calls this “a very strong argument against induction”.
  • From Newton’s theory the old laws follow only approximately, and only by adding false assumptions. And “only after we are in possession of Newton’s theory can we find out whether, and in what sense, the older theories can be said to be approximations to it.” The step was made by ingenuity, not logic.
  • That is Popper’s sufficient condition for depth: a new theory of higher universality that explains an older one by correcting it. Following Bohr, the demand that the new theory contain the old as an approximation can be called the “principle of correspondence”. It is not a necessary condition: Maxwell’s theory did not correct Fresnel’s in this way, yet Einstein listed the problems it dissolved.
  • The chapter ends on realism. Methodology need not assume that the true structure of the world can be discovered or even expressed; if our science is roughly right, conditions almost everywhere make such discoveries almost impossible. That science has nonetheless been “miraculously successful” cannot be explained without proving too much, but it can encourage us.

Chapter 6 · 1965

Of Clouds and Clocks: An Approach to the Problem of Rationality and the Freedom of Man

If the world were perfect clockwork, or a clockwork with dice added, human creativity and freedom would be illusions. Popper argues for a world of clouds controlled by clouds, in which meanings steer bodies by plastic, not cast-iron, control.

Compton, clouds and clocks

  • The lecture honours the physicist Arthur Holly Compton, whom Popper never met: he arrived in Berkeley in 1962 hoping to, and Compton died first. Popper had known of him since 1925, when an experiment by Compton and Simon refuted the short-lived quantum theory of Bohr, Kramers and Slater, clearing the way for the “new quantum theory” of Born, Heisenberg, Schrödinger and Dirac. During the war he discovered Compton the philosopher, in The Freedom of Man (1935).
  • Imagine an arrangement with a disorderly cloud on the left and a precise pendulum clock on the right. The changing seasons go towards the clocks; animals nearer the clouds than plants; a young puppy further left than an old dog; motor cars by reliability, a Cadillac far to the right and a Rolls-Royce near the best clocks; the solar system furthest right.
  • His paradigm cloud is a swarm of gnats: each flies irregularly, yet the swarm holds together because gnats that stray turn back to the densest part. A philosophical gnat might call it the most egalitarian, free and democratic society imaginable; the author of The Open Society denies it is open, since it has no freedom of association or pluralism. It is also a whole that is exactly the sum of its parts. A picnicking family roaming the woods near its car is cloudier still.
  • For about 250 years the Newtonian revolution persuaded almost everyone of a “staggering proposition”: “All clouds are clocks—even the most cloudy of clouds.” That is physical determinism; our arrangement reflected only our ignorance. Newton’s theory, “the first really successful scientific theory in human history”, explained the planets, falling apples, projectiles, pendulum clocks and the tides, and determinism became the ruling faith of enlightened men. (Newton himself was an exception: he thought the solar system imperfect and likely to perish, and was accused of impiety for it.)

All clocks are clouds

  • Among the few dissenters was Charles Sanders Peirce, “one of the greatest philosophers of all time”. In 1892 he pointed out that even if Newton was right there was no reason to think any clock perfect: the most refined measurements of mass and length “fall behind the accuracy of bank accounts”, and determinations of physical constants “are about on a par with an upholsterer’s measurements of carpets and curtains.” He conjectured that the world is ruled by laws of chance as well as strict laws, so that to some degree all clocks are clouds.
  • Then the tables turned. Indeterminism, equated with obscurantism until 1927, became the fashion, and Planck, Schrödinger and Einstein, who had led the way to quantum theory, were treated as “old fogies”. Popper once heard a brilliant young physicist call Einstein “antediluvian”.
  • His own position: Peirce was right, and the view fits classical physics, relativity and quantum theory, each more clearly. He is an indeterminist, like Peirce, Compton and most physicists, and thinks Einstein wrong to cling to determinism (“I discussed this matter with him, and … did not find him adamant”). But Einstein’s criticism of the Copenhagen interpretation, like de Broglie’s, Schrödinger’s, Bohm’s, Vigier’s and Landé’s, was brushed aside too lightly. Fashions and bandwagons in science “should be resisted rather than encouraged”, a jab at Kuhn.
  • Compton welcomed Heisenberg’s indeterminism of 1927, invited Heisenberg to lecture in Chicago in 1929, and saw in it an answer to what Popper calls “the nightmare of the physical determinist”. Compton put it: “If … the atoms of our bodies follow physical laws as immutable as the motions of the planets, why try?”

Why determinism is a nightmare, and chance no cure

  • Hume’s “doctrine of necessity” (like causes, like effects), the determinism of most philosophers and psychologists, is too vague to worry anyone. The serious problem is physical determinism, which claims infinitely precise predictability from the present state of the world, as Laplace said, and treats the world as physically closed.
  • If it were true, a physicist who was completely deaf and had never heard music could write all of Mozart’s and Beethoven’s works by predicting where their bodies would put black marks on paper, and even the works they would have written had they eaten lamb instead of chicken, or drunk tea instead of coffee. Popper calls this absurd.
  • Worse, determinism undermines itself: if it is true, we hold it because of our physical state, not because of arguments, so “physical determinism is a theory which, if it is true, is not arguable.” A physicist who knew no French could predict what a French determinist and his opponent would say.
  • The doctrine that man is a machine (de La Mettrie, 1751) now has perhaps more defenders than ever, as the thesis that man is a computer. Popper calls Turing’s challenge, to name a behaviour a computer could not match, “an intellectual trap”: to specify a behaviour is to write the specification for building a machine that does it. Einstein used to say “My pencil is more intelligent than I”, which does not make him indistinguishable from his pencil.
  • “Indeterminism is not enough.” The determinist Moritz Schlick said that freedom and responsibility stop where chance begins, and Popper agrees that a lecture produced by chance, a random string of English words, would be even less satisfactory than one produced by physics. Compton’s own model, a quantum jump amplified to fire or stop an explosion, is a model of a snap decision, like tossing a penny. What we need is something intermediate between perfect chance and perfect determinism, between perfect clouds and perfect clocks.

Compton’s problem and plastic control

  • Compton describes accepting an invitation to lecture at Yale on 10 November at 5 p.m., then travelling to the Rocky Mountains and to sunny Italy. As a physical event, his turning up in chilly New Haven was fantastically improbable, yet the audience was right to expect him: “it was my purpose [which] determined that I should be there.”
  • Popper splits this into two problems. Compton’s problem: how can the abstract content of a letter or a promise, which stays the same under translation, steer a man’s body from Italy back to Connecticut? That is the influence of the universe of meanings (promises, aims, rules of grammar, logic, chess or counterpoint) on behaviour, and Popper calls it “more important than the classical body-mind problem”. Descartes’s problem: how can states of mind move our limbs?
  • Compton’s “postulate of freedom” constrains any answer: freedom must be explained not as chance but as “a subtle interplay between something almost random or haphazard, and something like a restrictive or selective control”, a control that is plastic, not cast-iron. Compton was free to take an American, a French or an Italian boat, or to postpone if something more important came up.
  • The “master-switch” models fail: Descartes’s pineal gland, where mind acts on body at a point, and the quantum theorists’ single amplified quantum jump. Popper calls them “tiny baby theories”, after the unmarried mother who pleaded that it was only a very tiny one: “I still do not love the baby.” Real decisions mature through deliberation, which works by trial and error elimination.
  • Human language adds two higher functions to the two animal ones: description, governed by the ideal of truth, and argument, governed by validity. Argument is the latest and, through science, “perhaps the most powerful tool for biological adaptation which has ever emerged in the course of organic evolution.” Samuel Butler, author of Erewhon (1872), whom Popper ranks among the greatest English philosophers, saw that humans evolve outside their bodies: instead of better eyes we grow spectacles and telescopes, instead of better memories paper, printing and libraries, and lately computers.
  • The higher functions control the lower plastically. At a scientific conference a good joke may win in the short run; a valid argument wins in the long run. The solution of Compton’s problem: the higher levels of language evolved under pressure for better control, and in doing so grew abstract contents; “part of the function of contents and meanings is to control.” The control runs both ways: our theories control us, and we can criticise and reject them, so when we submit to them we do so freely.

A new theory of evolution, in twelve theses

  • Popper confesses he used to be contemptuous of evolutionary philosophies. Twenty-two years earlier he had agreed with Canon Charles Raven that the Darwinian controversy was “a storm in a Victorian teacup”, and mocked “the vapours still emerging from the cup”. “But now I have to confess that this cup of tea has become, after all, my cup of tea; and with it I have to eat humble pie.”
  • Herbert Spencer’s “survival of the fittest” is close to a tautology, since fitness is judged by survival. Popper restates Neo-Darwinism as a growing hierarchy of plastic controls: mutations are trial-and-error gambits and natural selection is one way of eliminating errors.
  • The theses, condensed: all organisms are constantly problem-solving, and so are whole lines of descent; the problems are objective, reconstructable in hindsight and need not be conscious; solutions come by trial and error; errors are eliminated either by killing the form or by evolving controls that suppress it; each organism “telescopes” the controls of its line and is its spearhead, probing new niches.
  • The schema is not a cycle: P₁ → TS → EE → P₂, with many tentative solutions in parallel. Neo-Darwinism recognises one problem (survival) and one form of error elimination (death); Popper’s version has endless specific problems. Reproduction solved one problem and created another, offspring that threaten to suffocate their parents and each other; multicellular life, a “common economy”, may have been one answer. This gives a rational account of “creative” or “emergent” evolution.
  • The twelfth thesis: evolution can produce error-eliminating controls, warning organs like the eye and feedback mechanisms, that remove errors without killing the organism, “and it makes it possible, ultimately, for our hypotheses to die in our stead.”
  • An organism is “a system of clouds controlled by clouds”: standing still, our muscles contract and relax almost at random and are corrected at once, the way an autopilot holds a plane on course.
  • We speak of problems in hindsight. Kepler thought his problem was the harmony of the world, and solved the mathematics of two-body orbits; Schrödinger thought his waves were waves of electric charge, and was shocked by Born’s statistical reading for the rest of his life. So “from the amoeba to Einstein is just one step.” The difference is Einstein’s critical attitude to his own ideas, which let him reject hundreds of hypotheses quickly; the physicist John Wheeler put it: “Our whole problem is to make the mistakes as fast as possible.” The critical method “consists in letting our hypotheses die in our stead: it is a case of exosomatic evolution.”

The soap bubble, and the body–mind problem

  • Do plastic controls exist in inorganic nature? Popper’s answer, after much trouble, is a soap bubble (or a child’s balloon). The air inside and the film are both clouds and control each other: without air the film collapses into a drop; without the film the air diffuses. Unlike a clock or a computer, the bubble does not suppress molecular effects but uses them; struck by a heat ray it warms, expands and floats.
  • Consciousness probably began as something like irritation at a problem, and became important when it began to anticipate possible movements and their outcomes. It is one control system among many, and not the highest: it is itself controlled by the exosomatic systems of books, theories and law it helped to produce, as we are controlled by a legal system we made.
  • So mental states control some physical movements, plastically, as anyone who plays the piano or violin knows. Popper adopts a dualism of interacting states, not substances, and then a pluralism, because marked bits of paper can hold states of a discussion beyond the grasp of the minds that made them: “abstract rules and abstract ideas … may move mountains.”
  • Aims can outgrow survival. Some become autonomous and even opposed to it: to excel in courage, climb Mount Everest, discover a new continent, be first on the Moon, or discover a new truth. Artists work by trial and error too, and an accident, a smudge or a wrong note, can change the problem.
  • A note added in 1974 concedes that he has given evolutionary reasons for interaction, not an account of how it happens, which may be insoluble: we do not know “how” electric charges repel each other either.

Chapter 7 · 1961, addendum 1971

Evolution and the Tree of Knowledge

Knowledge grows by the natural selection of hypotheses, but pure knowledge grows the opposite way from the tree of life, towards unity. And a conjecture: in evolution, behaviour leads and anatomy follows.

Problems, observation and the natural selection of hypotheses

  • The Herbert Spencer Lecture at Oxford let Popper talk about method in biology. He will not try to convince anyone of his own conjecture, “simply because I am not convinced myself.”
  • On the theory of knowledge he disagrees “with almost everybody, except perhaps Charles Darwin and Albert Einstein”. Theory, at least rudimentary expectation, always comes before observation, and observations and experiments serve to show theories false. We start not from observations but from problems, and “the growth of knowledge proceeds from old problems to new problems, by means of conjectures and refutations.”
  • He then asks the audience to observe, “here and now”, expecting some to ask what they are supposed to observe; if they do, the experiment has worked. Darwin knew it: “How odd it is that anyone should not see that all observation must be for or against some view.” “Observe!” is not a clear command, and nor is “observe this spider!”; “observe whether this spider climbs up, or down, as I expect it will!” is.
  • We understand a problem only by producing inadequate solutions and criticising them, until we see why the obvious answers fail. Knowledge at any moment consists of the hypotheses that have so far survived; scientific criticism “often makes our theories perish in our stead, eliminating our mistaken beliefs before such beliefs lead to our own elimination.” He insists this is not a metaphor.

The tree of knowledge

  • The tree of life grows from a common stem of single-celled ancestors into ever more specialised branches, and the tree of tools, from a stone and a stick, does the same. So does applied knowledge. But pure knowledge grows the other way. As Spencer noticed, it tends towards integration into unified theories, as when Newton joined Galileo’s terrestrial mechanics to Kepler’s celestial motions.
  • With time running upwards, the tree of knowledge springs “from countless roots which grow up into the air rather than down, and which ultimately, high up, tend to unite into one common stem.” The reason is our aim of explaining, together with a language that lets us argue about the truth of descriptions.
  • Practical problems start theoretical ones: “What can be done to combat poverty?” leads to “Why are people poor?”, and so to the theory of wages and prices; fighting smallpox leads to immunology and the theory of antibody formation. The problems multiply and differentiate, but the explanatory theory integrates.
  • This refutes the fashionable view that knowledge is only a tool for survival. The fittest hypothesis is the one that best solves its problem and resists criticism; for a theoretical problem, criticism is guided by the idea of truth, not survival. Two theses sum up: we are fallible but can learn from our mistakes; we cannot justify theories, but can criticise them and tentatively adopt the ones that best withstand criticism and explain most.

Method in biology

  • Two deliberately discouraging theses: there is no royal road to success in science, and no scientific result can be justified. Then “a secret of success”: be as clear as you can about your problem and watch it change; formulate and criticise the theories you hold, including the ones you hold unconsciously; construct alternatives even to theories that seem inescapable, since “whenever a theory appears to you as the only possible one, take this as a sign that you have neither understood the theory nor the problem which it was intended to solve”; treat experiments as attempts to overthrow a theory; aim to refute your own theories rather than leave it to others; but defend a theory well against criticism, and criticise it only in its strongest form.
  • Learning is “evocative rather than instructive”, a distinction he takes from Peter Medawar’s Reith Lectures: the environment challenges us, it does not instruct us, and evocation can simulate instruction.
  • Darwin is wrongly compared to Newton: there are no Darwinian laws of evolution. (Spencer tried to write some, of “differentiation” and “integration”, and they are nearly empty; Darwin found them of little interest.) But Darwin showed that it is “in principle possible to reduce teleology to causation”: natural selection can simulate design, so biologists are free to use purpose-talk. The words “in principle” matter, because no Darwinian has yet given an actual causal explanation of the adaptive evolution of any single organ.
  • Lamarck’s inheritance of use and disuse has been explained, or simulated, by natural selection, in what is now called the Baldwin effect (J. M. Baldwin, C. H. Waddington, G. G. Simpson, Schrödinger). Just as Einstein’s gravity simulates Newton’s, “even if Lamarckism is false … it should be respected by Darwinists as a first approximation to Darwinism.”
  • The real difficulty is Samuel Butler’s question, “Luck or Cunning?”: how can a goal-directed organ like the eye come from a huge number of independent accidental mutations, each with survival value? Bergson’s élan vital merely names the difficulty.
  • Popper also complains that defining fitness statistically, by numbers of surviving genes, has eclipsed Darwin’s central idea, since a species can prosper simply by breeding faster. He proposes measuring adaptation by subtracting birth rate from population growth. And he defends theorists: “there is nothing wrong with armchairs”, which have supported Kepler, Newton, Maxwell, Einstein, Bohr and Schrödinger; he himself is “a mere armchair philosopher”.

A conjecture: genetic dualism

  • Popper’s hypothesis: in many organisms we can distinguish a behaviour-controlling part, like the central nervous system, from an executive part, sense organs and limbs, and their mutations are largely independent. Dispositions such as seeking food, avoiding danger or learning skills by imitation can mutate without changing any organ. He calls this “genetic dualism”; if possessing an organ and using it are genetically one thing, “genetic monism” holds and his hypothesis is false.
  • His model is a fighter plane with an automatic pilot, whose “aim-structure” (attack a weaker enemy, support a friend, flee a stronger one) and “skill-structure” (stabilising, steering, aiming) together form its “central propensity structure”, or “mind”.
  • His main result: once a new aim or skill has evolved in the controlling part, previously useless changes to the executive organs become useful if they serve it, so the evolution of the organs becomes “goal-directed”. The woodpecker’s beak and tongue developed after it changed its tastes and feeding habits; had the beak come first, “it would not have known what to do with its new organs.” The giraffe’s feeding habits must have changed before its neck.
  • This lets Darwinism simulate Lamarckism, vitalism and animism as first approximations, and explains the eye as many small steps in one direction, directed by a tendency or wish to use it. He was led to the idea by a puzzle: the four-winged mutant of the two-winged fruit fly, Drosophila. Why did it not crash? He offers possible answers, including that the mutation is an atavism and the skill survived from four-winged ancestors.

Addendum: the hopeful behavioural monster

  • Written ten years later, in 1971. An expert had discouraged him from publishing the lecture. He now thinks “genetic dualism” should have been “genetic pluralism”: separate genetic bases for aims or preferences, for skills, and for anatomy.
  • The geneticist Richard Goldschmidt (1878–1958) argued in The Material Basis of Evolution (1940) that small variations cannot do the work, and proposed occasional large mutations, “hopeful monsters”, most of which die. Reading Norman Macbeth’s Darwin Retried (1971) prompted Popper to revive the idea in a new form: behavioural monsters, creatures whose novelty lies mainly in how they behave.
  • Monstrous behaviour is far less likely to be lethal than monstrous anatomy, and it can come from a genetic change, an injury or a new environment. Once successful, it selects new niches, which then put selection pressure on anatomy in a definite direction: an “interest in seeing” can make light-sensitive spots on the skin valuable, and lead the evolution of the eye. Even youthful playfulness may help survival in a changing world. This Darwinian theory “simulates” not only Lamarckism but Bergson’s vitalism too.

Chapter 8 · 1966

A Realist View of Logic, Physics, and History

Realism applied three times: to reduction and emergence, to the observer in quantum physics, and to logic, which is best seen as an instrument of criticism resting on the idea of truth.

We have not made our world, but we remake it

  • Modern philosophers say man is a stranger and afraid in a world he never made. So are animals and plants, Popper replies, and they have remade their corner of it. The plants transformed the chemistry of the whole atmosphere; marine animals built coral reefs, islands and mountain ranges of limestone. Man, apart from spreading desert by felling forests and building a few pyramids, began to compete with the corals only in the last century or so. “Still more recently he began to undo the work of the plants by slightly, though significantly, raising the carbon dioxide content of the atmosphere.”
  • Our distinctive products are myths, ideas and scientific theories, “exosomatic artifacts”, organs that grow outside our skins. Knowledge in this objective sense is like honey: made, stored and eaten by bees, including drones who made none, and each worker must eat some to make more. We too must consume other people’s theories (digest, criticise, demolish them) to produce our own.
  • He restates the tetradic schema P₁ → TT → EE → P₂: problems, once solved, “tend to beget problem-children”, often deeper than their parents, and the best gauge of progress is the distance in depth and unexpectedness between P₁ and P₂. If P₂ turns out to be P₁ in disguise, that can be a decisive objection to the theory. Applied to evolution, error elimination can yield more than Darwin’s “survive or perish”: new problems.

Reduction and emergence

  • What distinguishes a scientific theory from a story is not that one is true, but that they belong to different critical traditions; the scientific one is realist, aiming at theories that correspond to the facts.
  • The reduction of chemistry to physics, “apparently now well on the way”, is the paradigm of a good reduction: all of chemistry deduced from physical principles, a real gain in understanding. Reducing biology to physics would be far more spectacular. We might even learn to create primitive life artificially, but control is not reduction, just as chemists made compounds long before understanding them; there may be no theoretical reduction, just as mechanics and electrodynamics do not reduce to each other. Then life would be an emergent property of matter.
  • “As a rationalist I wish and hope to understand the world and … I wish and hope for a reduction.” But those who assume reduction must be possible destroy their own triumph: if it comes, they will only have been proved right.
  • Herbert Feigl and W. V. Quine, expected in the audience that morning, argued that mental states can be replaced by bodily ones; Quine wrote, “The bodily states exist anyway; why add the others?” Popper calls this bad, “ad hoc” reduction by language, a “degenerating problem shift” in Lakatos’s phrase: by the same method one could have abolished chemistry by saying each chemical state is some physical state. His “pluralistic postulate”: “We must beware of solving, or dissolving, factual problems linguistically.” Behaviourists, he adds, unconsciously keep Berkeley’s idealism in the form “to be is to be observed”.

Emergence in history

  • Popper is hopeful about reconstructing how life emerged but sceptical about reduction, because evolution is as unpredictable as history. His indeterminism is more radical than Heisenberg’s: like Peirce’s and Alfred Landé’s, it includes classical physics.
  • The growth of knowledge must be unpredictable in principle: “he who could so predict today by scientific means our discoveries of tomorrow could make them today”, and unpredictability in principle has always been the mark of emergence.
  • Theories cannot be reduced to the thoughts of those who hold them, because thoughts stand in causal relations and theories in logical ones. Nietzsche was influenced by Schopenhauer (causal); the Buddha’s ideas agree with Schopenhauer’s and contradict Nietzsche’s (logical). An author may not understand his own theory: Schrödinger, arguably, did not fully understand his equation until Born interpreted it, and Kepler seems to have disliked his own area law. Understanding a theory is an infinite task, like understanding a person.
  • Ideas have “a kind of life”: they can be misunderstood, rejected and forgotten, and come back. Unlike Plato’s eternal concepts and Hegel’s self-changing ones, the ideas that matter most are statements. Only after recognising the plurality of what exists can we usefully apply Ockham’s razor; inverting Quine, only if Plato’s beard is tough and tangled enough is it worth shaving.

The observer in physics

  • Subjectivism has entered physics twice: in Boltzmann’s theory that the direction of time is subjective, and in Heisenberg’s reading of his formulas as limits on the observer’s interference with what he observes. (Einstein brought an observer into his thought experiments and then steadily exorcised him.)
  • Popper derives Heisenberg’s relation between energy and time from Planck’s quantum postulate of 1900, without the new quantum mechanics of 1925–26, and the position–momentum relation from Duane’s principle in the same way (figure below). Since Planck’s theory is statistical, the formulas are naturally read as statistical “scatter relations”, as Popper proposed in 1934: they say nothing about how precisely we can measure.
  • Indeed, testing a scatter relation requires measuring momentum far more precisely than the scatter, which laboratories do every day. Heisenberg called attaching meaning to such measurements “a matter of personal belief”; Popper says they are the tests of the formula. Quantum mechanics is statistical because its problems, such as spectral intensities, are statistical. The observer’s role is what it was in Newton or Maxwell: “the observer is, essentially, the man who tests the theory … we are not so much observers as thinkers.”

Realism in logic

  • Logic is not a game, nor a matter of choice. It is the theory of deduction, which transmits truth from premises to conclusion and retransmits falsity from conclusion back to the premises. The first use serves proofs; the second serves criticism. Anyone who answers a criticism with “your logic may be all right for you, but I prefer a different logic” undermines critical discussion.
  • In the empirical sciences logic is used mainly to criticise, so we should use the strongest logic we have: “we must use all the guns we have. Every shot is important.” Retreating to a weaker logic, intuitionist or three-valued as Hans Reichenbach proposed for quantum theory, is a sign “that something is rotten in the state of Denmark”, meaning the Copenhagen interpretation.
  • In proofs the reverse holds: proving an old theorem with weaker means is a real mathematical discovery. “Intuitionist logic” is a misnomer for a weakened classical logic devised by Brouwer and formalised by Heyting; Popper admires the logic but not the philosophy, since intuitions “as a rule … do not stand up to criticism. So I am not an intuitionist.”
  • There are three theories of truth: correspondence with the facts; coherence with our beliefs or with statements already accepted (which would make knowledge “utterly conservative”); and pragmatic usefulness (“useful for whom?”, since a lie can be useful to a criminal in court). Popper refuses to ask “What is truth?”, because “What is?” questions are pseudo-questions and “we should altogether avoid, like the plague, discussing the meaning of words.” If challenged about what a word means, say, “I don’t know, and I am not interested in meanings; and if you wish, I will gladly accept your terminology.”
  • The real question is whether a statement can correspond to a fact. Schlick said correspondence is a one-to-one relation, but vague statements can be true and unambiguous ones false (a murderer saying “I have not killed him”). Wittgenstein said a proposition pictures reality, as a groove on a gramophone record pictures a sound. Popper answers with the story of Livingstone and an African king: the king’s one-word reply is translated as a ten-minute tale of woe, and his ten-minute answer to a second question is translated as “No.” (“No doubt this story is invented. But it is well invented.”) And Wittgenstein himself declared his propositions nonsensical in the end.
  • Tarski rehabilitated correspondence by a simple observation: to speak of a statement’s correspondence to a fact we need a metalanguage that can both name statements and describe facts. In English we can say that the German statement “Das Gras ist grün” corresponds to the facts if and only if grass is green (see Chapter 9). A judge who tells a witness to speak the truth means correspondence, not usefulness or consistency.
  • Tarski also proved there can be no general criterion of truth for rich languages, so truth is a regulative idea. It is easier to find a theory false than true, and most of our best theories, being idealisations, are strictly false; hence nearness to truth. “As a realist I look upon logic as the organon of criticism (rather than of proof).”

Chapter 9 · 1971, addendum 1955

Philosophical Comments on Tarski’s Theory of Truth

A tribute to the man Popper calls his one real teacher in philosophy: Tarski made it respectable to say that a statement corresponds to the facts, and even let us define reality. Popper adds his own measure of nearness to truth.

A bench in the Volksgarten

  • “Our main concern in science and in philosophy is, or ought to be, the search for truth, by way of bold conjectures and the critical search for what is false in our various competing theories.” That was his view in July 1934 when he first met Tarski, at a conference in Prague organised by the Vienna Circle, carrying the page proofs of Logik der Forschung.
  • Yet he was uneasy about truth. Not the liar paradox so much as two worries: what the correspondence of a statement to a fact could be, and the view that a legitimate notion needs a criterion for applying it. His book even had a section suggesting that the word could be avoided in favour of deducibility.
  • Early in 1935 he met Tarski again in Vienna, at Karl Menger’s colloquium, where he also met Gödel, Thoralf Skolem and Abraham Wald. Tarski explained his theory in about twenty minutes “on a bench (an unforgotten bench) in the Volksgarten in Vienna” and showed him the proofs of the German translation of his great paper. “I looked upon him as the one man whom I could truly regard as my teacher in philosophy. I have never learned so much from anybody else.”
  • What delighted him was that truth could be defined in unobjectionable logical terms, applied to every unambiguous statement, and yet had no general criterion. That lack is no scandal: many theories are undecidable, and theoremhood is still perfectly clear. So the Wittgensteinian thesis that “a concept is vacuous if there is no criterion for its application” is refuted by logic, and an ancient quarrel between the Stoics and the Sceptics is settled by a theorem.
  • Popper was always a commonsense realist: “If there was not a real world … then I would not be interested in philosophy.” He never found out Tarski’s own view of realism; Tarski seemed impressed both by Tadeusz Kotarbiński’s “reism” and by Viennese positivism, and stressed that his concept of truth was neutral. Popper claims a realist reading anyway, while dissociating Tarski from it.

What Tarski found

  • Any theory of correspondence must be stated in a metalanguage with three kinds of expression: names of the statements of the object language; translations of them, to describe the facts; and predicates relating the two, such as “corresponds to the facts”. Tarski called this a semantical metalanguage. His greatness, Popper says, lay in finding these minimum requirements and in seeing that the third kind goes essentially beyond the object language, which cannot, without paradox, contain its own semantics.
  • Popper found it easier to teach the theory by speaking of correspondence instead of truth, and by using false examples: the German “Der Mond besteht aus grünem Käse” corresponds to the facts if and only if the moon consists of green cheese.
  • Tarski defined truth via “satisfaction”, a semantic idea, yet claimed to use no semantic concepts. The puzzle dissolves: the semantics of one language can be part of the syntax of a richer one. Still, Popper thinks definitions matter little; what matters is the rehabilitation of correspondence.
  • If truth is correspondence to the facts, reality can be defined as that to which true statements correspond: the moon’s being made of green cheese is a real fact if and only if the statement describing it is true. Popper does not mean truth is more basic than reality, which would have “an idealistic flavour”; he means the concept of reality is no more vacuous than that of truth.

Truth content, falsity content, verisimilitude

  • Combining the theory of truth with Tarski’s calculus of deductive systems (completed, Popper recalls, in Paris in 1935) gives simple theorems: the set of all true statements is a complete deductive system, and for rich languages it cannot be axiomatised. The truth content of a statement is the set of true statements following from it.
  • Falsity content cannot simply be the set of its false consequences, because that set is not a deductive system (every false statement entails true ones). Popper defines it as a relative content and then uses a content measure, 1 minus the logical probability.
  • His proposed measure of verisimilitude is the difference between the measures of truth content and falsity content, divided by a normalising factor, so that it runs from −1 to +1. A tautology scores 0: saying nothing is safe but makes no approach to “the whole truth”. That is why verisimilitude is a better aim than truth: we may rightly prefer theories we think false to true but empty ones.
  • He claims no criterion for applying it, any more than for truth. But scientists, Einstein among them, want to say that Einstein’s theory is probably false yet nearer the truth than Newton’s, and to say this “with a good conscience seems to me a major desideratum”. His definitions did not survive (see Appendix 2).
  • An addendum reprints a 1955 note from Mind showing how Tarski’s definition of satisfaction can use finite sequences of things, of sufficient length, instead of infinite ones, which Popper thought more natural for empirical theories; it lets one define a refuting instance of a law as a finite sequence of things that do not satisfy it.

Appendix 1 · 1948

The Bucket and the Searchlight: Two Theories of Knowledge

The oldest piece in the book and the most readable. Observations are made, not had; a question comes first and lights up what is worth seeing; science is the critical tradition applied to our myths.

The bucket

  • Popper gave this lecture in German at the European Forum in Alpbach in August 1948; it was published in 1949 and appears in English for the first time here. The phrases “bucket theory of the mind” and “searchlight theory” date, he says, from his New Zealand years, and he read a paper called “The Bucket Theory of the Mind” at the LSE staff club in 1946.
  • The bucket theory holds that before we can know anything we must have perceptions, so knowledge is either accumulated perceptions (naive empiricism) or perceptions sorted and classified (Bacon, and more radically Kant). The Greek atomists pictured atoms flying off objects into our senses, where knowledge assembles itself like a jigsaw.
  • Bacon called perceptions “grapes, ripe and in season”, to be gathered patiently and pressed into the pure wine of knowledge, and strict empiricists want us to interfere as little as possible, since our additions are the source of error. Kant replies that perceptions are never pure; the mind processes them, into “fermented wine”, perhaps.

Observations are made, not had

  • In science what matters is observation, not perception, and observation is active: “We do not ‘have’ an observation … but we ‘make’ an observation.” A navigator even “works” one. Every observation is preceded by an interest, a question or a problem, something theoretical, and so every observation is selective.
  • Biology helps. Every organism has an inborn, limited set of dispositions to react, its inner state, and learning is a change in those dispositions, not a store of memory traces. An expectation is “a disposition to react, or … a preparation for a reaction, which is adapted to [or which anticipates] a state of the environment yet to come about.” We often notice our expectations only when they fail, as when we meet an unexpected step in our path.
  • At every moment we live in a “horizon of expectations”, conscious or not, which gives our observations their meaning. An observation that clashes with it can act “like a bombshell”, forcing us to rebuild the horizon at a higher level; a successful rebuilding is what we call an explanation.
  • Which comes first, hypothesis or observation, is like the hen and the egg, and both are answerable. The bucket theory says observation, as the single-celled egg came before the hen. The searchlight theory says the hypothesis, “even though an observation that refutes a certain hypothesis may stimulate a new (and therefore a temporally later) hypothesis.” We learn from our hypotheses where to look.
  • Science is the continuation of the pre-scientific repair of our horizons. It never starts from scratch: today’s science rests on yesterday’s, the oldest science on myths, and so back to the expectations of a newborn child or a single cell. There is “only one step from the amoeba to Einstein.”

The Greek invention

  • Babylonians, Greeks and New Zealand Maoris all told cosmological myths about the origins of things, kept by priests or medicine men and changed only slowly. What was new in Greece, around the turn of the sixth and fifth centuries BC, was not the replacement of myth but a new attitude to it: criticism became part of the school’s tradition, “a tradition of a higher order”. Observation was called in only as a witness in that critical debate.
  • It can hardly be chance that Anaximander, Thales’s pupil, consciously diverged from his master, and Anaximenes from Anaximander; Thales must have challenged his pupils to criticise him. It happened, as far as Popper knows, only once; the Pythagoreans were a school of the old, preserving kind.
  • Xenophanes shows the critical attitude at full strength: if cattle, horses or lions could draw, “then the horses would draw their gods / Like horses; and cattle like cattle”. The task of science, explanation, and many of its ideas were taken over from myth-making; the critical tradition is what is new.

What an explanation is

  • Explanation is not “reduction of the unknown to the known”. All the kinds of explanation science has accepted share one form: a deduction of the thing explained from premises consisting of universal laws and initial conditions. What changed over history was the silent abandonment of demands that the premises be intuitive or self-evident, in favour of the demand that they be independently testable.
  • His example, “somewhat gruesome”, is a dead rat. The explicandum: this rat has died recently. The hypothesis: rat poison. The explicans needs a law (“if a rat eats at least eight grains of rat poison it will die within five minutes”) and an initial condition (“this rat ate at least eighteen grains of rat poison, more than five minutes ago”). In a world where rat poison made you lively and happy for a week, the initial condition alone would explain nothing.
  • If a sceptic asks how we know it ate poison, “How can you doubt it, seeing that it is dead?” is circular; “Analyse the contents of its stomach” is an independent test. Cause and effect mean something only relative to a tested law, a point Popper says Hume overlooked.
  • The same schema covers prediction, technical application and testing (figure below), and distinguishes the theorist, who holds a law fixed while varying the rest, from the historian, who holds a description of a particular event fixed and uses laws, usually trivial ones, to test it. Only “unexpected” predictions corroborate: passing an exam says something only if the exam was severe. (A Viennese examiner used to muse that a student who answered “eighteen” to “how much is 5 plus 7?” got a pass, but one who answered “green” perhaps should have been failed.)
  • A satisfactory theory must say more than the instances that prompted it, or its explanations are circular. That principle “stands in direct contradiction to all positivistic and naively empiricist” tendencies: it demands bold hypotheses that open new fields of observation, not careful generalisations, “the idols of all naive empiricists” since Bacon. Testability grows with universality and precision, which explains the push to measurement, though measurement came late and depends on theory. And unlike art and music, “there really is, in science, such a thing as progress.”
  • Newton’s theory contradicts Kepler’s and Galileo’s (as in Chapter 5) and so could not be induced from them; it lit the way to where they fail. From Descartes to Maxwell physicists wanted mechanical models of push and pressure; Newton’s attraction was the first defeat of that programme, and Maxwell’s abandoned ether model the last. Since then we accept abstract theories if they are independently testable.

Getting in touch with reality

  • “There is no road, royal or otherwise, which leads of necessity from a ‘given’ set of specific facts to any universal law.” Laws are conjectures within a larger system, never tested in isolation, and no theory is sacrosanct; the “truly magnificent” corroborations of nineteenth-century mechanics made people forget that, and the physics of the twentieth century taught them again.
  • Disappointed expectations are like a blind man walking into an obstacle: “It is through the falsification of our suppositions that we actually get in touch with ‘reality’.” A falsified theory can always be saved by extra hypotheses, like epicycles, but that is not how science progresses.
  • Laws can be written as prohibitions: there is no closed system in which energy changes in one part without compensating changes elsewhere (the first law of thermodynamics); there is no machine that is 100 per cent efficient (the second). To the engineer these are advice; to the scientist, a challenge to find what they forbid.
  • The lecture ends by calling science “a magnificent adventure of the human spirit”, whose aim is not certainty, an idea descended from magic, but “better and better theories [or to invent more and more powerful searchlights]”.

Appendix 2 · 1978

Supplementary Remarks (1978)

Written for the revised edition, answering critics under five headings. On the most serious one, nearness to truth, Popper concedes that his definition was faulty.

  • Induction. Anthony O’Hear (1975) argued that science cannot do without inductive assumptions. Popper replies that we never infer predictions from observation alone, only from observations plus universal theories, and the theories are conjectured, often before any evidence exists. Asked why the best-tested theory is a rational basis for action, he agrees that having no better method does not make a method rational, but says worse ones plainly exist, such as clinging to the first guess. He is “not a justificationist”, and would be “surprised but happy” to hear of a method whose success is certain. Higher-level “metaconjectures”, for instance that no method will predict the weather on Sundays five weeks ahead, may soothe inductivist intuitions.
  • Truth. Susan Haack (1976) argued that Tarski did not offer a correspondence theory. Popper quotes Tarski himself, on grasping “the so-called classical conception of truth (‘true—corresponding with reality’)”, and adds that he first used Tarski in support of realism in 1956 without ascribing that view to Tarski.
  • Comparing contents. He had proposed comparing theories by the questions each can answer. David Miller, Adolf Grünbaum and John Watkins produced counter-examples, and Popper admits that his wording (“all” questions) was wrong: a successor theory that corrects its predecessor cannot answer every question the predecessor did. Some such questions “do not arise” in the successor; Watkins’s example, that Kepler predicts planetary positions without needing their masses, is the most interesting. His fix is to relativise the comparison to scientifically relevant problems, which need not be arbitrary.
  • Verisimilitude. “My theory of verisimilitude has been severely criticized, and I must at once admit that my proposed ‘definition of verisimilitude’ is faulty.” His corrected intuition: one statement is nearer the truth than another if its (relativised) truth content is greater and some of the other’s false consequences, preferably all the refuted ones, are replaced by their negations. He notes Miller’s alternative, distance from the set of truths, and stays optimistic, agreeing with Pavel Tichý. “I am deeply sorry for having made some very serious mistakes in connection with the definition of verisimilitude; but I do think that we should not conclude from the failure of my attempts to solve the problem that the problem cannot be solved.”
  • Crucial experiments. Miller had shown something that looked like Russell’s paradox for physics: if one false theory is closer to the true values of two parameters, the theories can be restated in two equivalent parameters on which the ranking is reversed (figure below). Frege, faced with Russell’s paradox, said arithmetic was tottering; one is tempted to say Miller shows physics tottering, but physics plainly works. Popper has no general answer, only that the choice of parameters in each case is guided by the problems at hand and need not be arbitrary.

Context

Why this is also a political theory

Popper never separated his theory of knowledge from the politics of The Open Society and Its Enemies (1945). In both fields, he thought, the traditional question asks about authority when it should ask about correction. The book shows the link in passing: Chapter 3’s “no authoritative sources of knowledge”, the swarm of gnats in Chapter 6 that is free and equal but not an open society because it has no freedom of association, Hegel’s individuals reduced to “instruments” of the Spirit of the Epoch, and B. F. Skinner’s Walden Two, “a charming and benevolent but utterly naive Utopian dream of omnipotence”, whose totalitarian cousins Popper calls politically dangerous.

The wrong question

Who is the authority?

Knowledge
What are the sources of knowledge: reason, the senses, intuition, tradition?
Politics
Who should rule: the wisest, the best, the majority?

The right question

How do we correct errors?

Knowledge
How can we find out whether a belief is wrong? Its pedigree is irrelevant.
Politics
How can bad rulers be removed without violence, and limited while in office?

Chapter 2’s line about science states the connection most plainly: science “allows us to replace killing (world 1) and intimidation (world 2) by the impersonal arguments of world 3.”

Context

What held up

This is a book of philosophy, not forecasts, but it makes a few datable claims about how science and the world would go. Here is how they look from 2026. The verdicts are our judgement; the outcomes are well documented.

Held

Man is raising the carbon dioxide in the air Ch. 8 · 1966

Said in passing, “slightly, though significantly”. The Mauna Loa annual mean was about 321 parts per million in 1966 and about 427 in 2025.

Held

Name a behaviour and you have written a specification for a machine Ch. 6 · 1965

Popper’s answer to Turing’s challenge. Machines have since matched one named benchmark after another: chess in 1997, Go in 2016, fluent conversation in the 2020s. As he predicted, the argument has moved from what machines can do to whether that makes them like us.

Held

Fermat’s last theorem “will be proved one day” Ch. 3 · 1967

Offered only as an example of a subjective belief, beside the more cautious suggestion that it might be undecidable. Andrew Wiles proved it in 1994.

Partly

Chemistry is being reduced to physics Ch. 8 · 1966

Quantum chemistry now computes many molecular properties from physical principles, as he expected. Working chemistry still relies on its own concepts, and whether the reduction is complete is still argued.

Partly

Behaviour leads, anatomy follows Ch. 7 · 1961 and 1971

The Baldwin effect, “behavioural drive” and niche construction theory have given behaviour-first evolution a respectable place, though not the leading role Popper proposed. It remains a live debate, not a consensus.

Partly

The tide will turn against subjectivist quantum mechanics Ch. 3 · 1967

He expected it to take “many more years … if it ever does turn”. Observer-free interpretations, such as many-worlds, Bohmian mechanics and objective-collapse theories, are now taken seriously, but none has displaced the others, and experiments have confirmed the quantum predictions Einstein hoped would fail.

Not yet

We may create primitive life artificially Ch. 8 · 1966

He thought it “not at all improbable”. Scientists have built synthetic genomes and put them into existing cells, but nothing living has yet been assembled from non-living chemistry, and the origin of life is still unsolved.

Missed

The genetic code works by prohibition, not instruction Ch. 3 · 1967

Labelled “a conjecture but … an attractive one”. Protein synthesis turned out to be template-directed, closer to instruction; repression plays a large part in gene regulation, but the code itself is not a set of prohibitions.

Missed

Verisimilitude can be defined in purely logical terms Ch. 2, 9 · 1970–71

Refuted by Tichý, Miller and Harris in 1974, and conceded by Popper himself in Appendix 2. Later accounts based on likeness, by Graham Oddie (1986) and Ilkka Niiniluoto (1987), took another route.

What was built on it

  • Evolutionary epistemology. Popper credits the name to Donald T. Campbell, whose “blind variation and selective retention” carried the argument of Chapters 2 and 7 into psychology and the study of creativity.
  • The Self and Its Brain. The three worlds went on to structure Popper’s book with the neuroscientist John Eccles (1977), on the body–mind problem that Chapter 6 treats as a problem of plastic control.
  • Theories that correct their data. The bibliographical note to Chapter 5 records that Paul Feyerabend took its idea, that a new theory can correct the “observational” laws it explains, as the starting point of his paper “Explanation, Reduction and Empiricism” (1962).
  • George Soros studied under Popper at the LSE and has long described his approach to markets as applied Popper: state why a price is wrong, say in advance what would prove you wrong, and close the position when it arrives. His Open Society Foundations take their name from Popper’s other book.
  • Where the word got worse. “Unfalsifiable” became a debating weapon for throwing claims out of court. That inverts Popper twice: he denied that unfalsifiable ideas are meaningless, and he argued at length for realism, which he called irrefutable.
  • On an ordinary Monday. Chapter 7’s “secret of success” works outside science: write the problem down and watch it change; list the theories you are holding, including the unconscious ones; invent an alternative even to the theory that seems inescapable; look for the observation that would sink your favourite; and defend it properly before you let it go.

Context

Where it has been argued with

You cannot falsify one hypothesis on its own

The Duhem–Quine problem: a test always confronts a theory together with assumptions about instruments and conditions, and logic does not say which to blame. Popper grants the premise here, calling test statements criticisable and laws “never tested in isolation” (Appendix 1), and answers with methodological rules against ad hoc rescue. Rules are not deductions, and critics still see this as the deepest crack.

Scientists do not behave this way

Thomas Kuhn’s The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962) described normal science tolerating anomalies until a replacement exists. Popper’s replies in this book are sharp but brief: normal science is poor training, and fashions should be resisted. Imre Lakatos’s research programmes, whose “progressive” and “degenerating problem shifts” Popper borrows in Chapters 4 and 8, were an attempt to keep his standards while allowing for the history.

Why act on corroboration if it says nothing about the future?

Wesley Salmon pressed this against the first edition, and Popper answers him in Chapter 2; Anthony O’Hear pressed it again in 1975, and Popper answers in Appendix 2. Either corroboration carries forward-looking weight, and something like induction is back, or it does not, and the preference for the best-tested theory looks unmotivated. This is the objection Popper answered least convincingly.

Verisimilitude failed

Pavel Tichý, David Miller and John Harris showed in 1974 that on Popper’s definitions no false theory can be nearer the truth than another. Popper conceded in 1978. Graham Oddie’s Likeness to Truth (1986) and Ilkka Niiniluoto’s Truthlikeness (1987) built alternatives on similarity to the truth; none commands general agreement, which matters because “nearer to the truth” carries much of the weight in Popper’s account of progress.

Is there really knowledge without a knower?

Susan Haack’s “Epistemology With a Knowing Subject” (1979) argued that no viable epistemology can drop the believer, and that fallibilism itself needs an account of what the subject is entitled to believe. Others find World 3 either trivial (it just means what books say) or mysterious (it needs a metaphysics Popper never supplies).

Is Tarski’s theory a correspondence theory?

Haack (1976) said Tarski never presented it as one, and that realism cannot be read out of it. Popper answered with Tarski’s own words about the “classical conception of truth”, while agreeing that Tarski stressed neutrality and denied metaphysical conclusions. The question is still disputed among philosophers of logic.

Terms of art

Glossary

Bucket theory
Popper’s name for the commonsense theory of knowledge: the mind is an empty container filled through the senses (the tabula rasa).
Searchlight theory
His alternative: expectations and questions come first and decide what we observe.
Horizon of expectations
The sum of an organism’s conscious and unconscious expectations, which gives observations their meaning.
Hume’s problem
Popper’s name, from 1934, for the logical problem of induction: can we reason from observed to unobserved instances?
Principle of transference
His conjecture that what is true in logic is true in psychology and in scientific method.
Demarcation
The line between empirical science and the rest: a theory is empirical if some possible observations would contradict it.
Content
The set of all a statement’s logical consequences; it grows as the statement’s probability falls.
Truth content, falsity content
The true, and the false, consequences of a statement (the second defined as a relative content).
Corroboration
A report on how well a theory has stood up to severe tests so far; about the past, not the future.
Verisimilitude
Nearness to the truth, or truthlikeness. Popper’s formal definition failed; the idea remains in use.
Ad hoc
Of an explanation: not testable independently of what it was invented to explain.
Immunizing stratagem
Any move that protects a theory from refutation (Hans Albert’s term, adopted by Popper).
World 1, 2, 3
Physical things; mental states; the objective contents of thought, such as theories, problems and arguments.
Autonomy of World 3
Its capacity to produce consequences and problems nobody intended, such as the primes from counting.
Exosomatic
Outside the body: tools, writing, libraries, and theories, which evolve like organs but outside us.
P₁ → TT → EE → P₂
Problem, tentative theory, error elimination, new problem: Popper’s schema for the growth of knowledge.
Problem situation
A problem together with its background of accepted theories and framework.
Situational analysis
Explaining an action by an idealised reconstruction of the problem situation the agent faced.
Modified essentialism
Popper’s “third view”: explanation by ever deeper structural laws, never by ultimate essences.
Principle of correspondence
Bohr’s term for the demand that a new theory contain the old one as an approximation.
Clouds and clocks
The spectrum from disorderly to orderly systems; Popper holds that all clocks are clouds to some degree.
Plastic control
Control that restricts without determining, as aims and meanings steer behaviour; opposed to cast-iron control.
Compton’s problem
How abstract meanings, such as a promise to lecture, can steer physical behaviour.
Genetic dualism
Popper’s conjecture that the genes for behaviour (aims and skills) and for anatomy mutate independently.
Behavioural monster
A creature whose novelty lies in its behaviour; Popper’s variant of Goldschmidt’s “hopeful monster”.
Metalanguage
A language in which we can name the statements of another language and describe the facts they speak of.
Regulative idea
An ideal that guides inquiry without providing a criterion, as truth does.

A study summary of Karl Popper, Objective Knowledge: An Evolutionary Approach (Clarendon Press, 1972; revised edition 1979), checked against the text of the revised edition (eighth impression, 1994) as scanned on archive.org. Paraphrased throughout. Words in quotation marks are Popper’s own, or those he quotes (Russell, Hume, Strawson, Ryle, Churchill, Einstein, Peirce, Compton, Frege, Heyting, Quine, Wheeler, Darwin, Xenophanes in Popper’s translation, Busch in Popper’s translation, Tarski); each was checked against the book.

This page replaces an earlier, shorter summary. Corrections made in the redo: the opening sentence is now quoted in full (“Of course, I may be mistaken; but …”); the chapters date from 1948 to 1971 (Chapter 5 from 1957, Appendix 1 from 1948), not 1965–71; the revised edition has two appendices, not one; the classroom instruction to “observe” is in Chapter 7, not in the bucket-and-searchlight appendix; and claims the book does not make have been removed. The 1919 story and the Adler anecdote are from Conjectures and Refutations, and are marked as such.

The bucket-and-searchlight drawing, the target of truth, Galileo’s tides, the two trees, the three-worlds and bridge diagrams, and the clouds-to-clocks arrangement are our drawings of images or arguments in the book. The Newton, schema, language, autopilot, Heisenberg, metalanguage and explanation tables are condensed from the text. The Miller table uses Popper’s own numbers, whose arithmetic we checked. The “what held up” verdicts are our judgement; the carbon dioxide figures are Mauna Loa annual means.
