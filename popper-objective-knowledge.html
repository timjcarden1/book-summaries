An Evolutionary Approach

Objective
Knowledge Nine essays arguing that knowledge is not a state of mind, that induction does not exist, and that we grow by killing our own guesses.

Popper opens chapter one with a sentence almost no philosopher would risk: I think I have solved a major philosophical problem: the problem of induction. The solution is not a better justification for generalising from experience. It is the claim that we never generalise from experience at all — we guess, and then we try to destroy the guess. Everything else in the book follows from taking that seriously: a theory of mind, a theory of evolution, a theory of what science is for, and an account of knowledge as something that exists outside anybody's head.

Context

Where These Ideas Came From

A cabinetmaker's apprentice from Vienna who spent seventy years attacking certainty.

Objective Knowledge is a late book — Popper was seventy when it appeared, and the papers it collects were written between 1965 and 1971. Nearly everything in it is a development of positions he had held since his twenties, and those positions were formed in a specific place at a specific moment: Vienna between the wars, where two things were simultaneously true. Einstein had just overturned Newton, and half the intellectual class had adopted doctrines that seemed to explain everything and could be refuted by nothing.

Popper was born in 1902 to a Viennese family of Jewish descent that had converted to Lutheranism — a background that made him, in the 1930s, exactly the wrong sort of person to remain in Austria. His father was a lawyer with a fourteen-thousand-volume library. Popper left school early, spent time as a manual worker, and apprenticed himself to a cabinetmaker, an experience he described as more instructive than most of his formal education; the master craftsman he worked under, Adalbert Pösch, appears in his autobiography as a man who claimed to know everything and could not be told anything. He qualified as a schoolteacher, took a doctorate in the psychology of thought in 1928, and taught in Viennese secondary schools while writing the book that made his name.

The formative year was 1919, when he was seventeen. He had been briefly attracted to Marxism, and broke with it after an incident in which unarmed young socialists — some of them his friends — were shot by police during a demonstration the party had encouraged. What appalled him was not only the deaths but the ease with which the theory absorbed them: the episode was immediately explained as a necessary stage, confirming the doctrine rather than testing it. In the same year, Eddington's eclipse expedition tested Einstein's prediction that starlight would bend by a specific amount near the sun. Popper noticed that the two cases had opposite structures, and spent the rest of his life on the difference.

The official opposition

Popper is routinely described as a member of the Vienna Circle, and he was not. The Circle — Moritz Schlick, Rudolf Carnap, Otto Neurath and others — were logical positivists: they held that a statement is meaningful only if it can be verified by experience, which made metaphysics not false but literally nonsense. Popper knew them, published in their book series, and disagreed with them about nearly everything of consequence. Neurath called him die offizielle Opposition, and the label stuck because it was accurate.

The disagreement matters for reading this book, because Popper's targets are often positivist rather than commonsensical. Where the positivists sought a criterion of meaning, Popper sought a criterion of scientific status — and insisted that unscientific does not mean meaningless. Metaphysical ideas, on his account, are frequently the seedbed of scientific ones: atomism was metaphysics for two thousand years before it was physics. Where the positivists wanted to ground science in observation, Popper argued that observation is theory-laden and cannot ground anything. And where they hoped an inductive logic could assign probabilities to theories, he thought the whole programme was chasing something that does not exist.

The famous criterion

What Makes a Theory Scientific

Not that it explains a lot. That it forbids a lot.

This is the idea Popper is best known for and the one most often misstated, including by people who invoke it approvingly. It is not a theory of meaning, not a theory of truth, and not a claim that unfalsifiable statements are worthless. It is a proposal for where to draw one particular line: a theory belongs to empirical science if there is some conceivable observation that would count decisively against it, and its author can say in advance what that observation is.

The contrast that produced it is the one from 1919. Popper had been struck that Marx's theory of history, Freud's psychoanalysis and Adler's individual psychology all shared a feature their adherents regarded as their greatest strength: whatever happened, the theory accounted for it. A man pushes a child into a river and a man risks his life to save a child from a river; Freudian theory explains the first as repression and the second as sublimation, and Adlerian theory explains the first as an attempt to prove himself and the second as an attempt to prove himself. Neither could name a piece of human behaviour that would embarrass it. Popper's anecdote about Adler is exact: when he reported a case that seemed not to fit, Adler analysed it in terms of inferiority feelings without having seen the child, and when asked how he could be so certain, said "because of my thousandfold experience" — to which Popper replied that with this new case it was presumably thousand-and-one-fold.

Einstein's theory was in a different position entirely, and the difference was not that it was better supported. It was that Einstein had specified in advance a result that would sink it. General relativity predicted that light passing the sun would be deflected by 1.75 arcseconds — twice the Newtonian figure. Had Eddington's plates shown the Newtonian value, or no deflection, the theory would have been finished, and Einstein said as much. A theory that takes that risk is doing something a theory that explains everything is not doing.

And nothing underneath

If theories are tested against observations, what are the observations tested against? Popper's answer is the most vertiginous thing in his epistemology and the one that most clearly separates him from the empiricists he is often lumped with. Observation statements — he calls them basic statements — are themselves theory-laden, fallible, and accepted by a kind of convention among investigators. They are not certain. They can be, and are, overturned. There is no floor.

"Science does not rest upon solid bedrock. The bold structure of its theories rises, as it were, above a swamp. It is like a building erected on piles… we simply stop when we are satisfied that the piles are firm enough to carry the structure, at least for the time being."The Logic of Scientific Discovery, §30 — the image recurs throughout Objective Knowledge

The consequence is that Popper's system has no foundations anywhere: not in observation, not in reason, not in probability. What holds it up is not a base but a practice — the continual attempt to knock it over. This is why he calls his position critical rationalism rather than rationalism, and why he thinks the demand for justification, in epistemology as in politics, is the root error rather than the goal.

Chapter 1

The Problem of Induction, and Its Dissolution

Hume was right, and it does not matter.

Hume's problem: no number of observed instances can justify a universal law, because the inference from "all observed A are B" to "all A are B" is not valid, and any attempt to justify it by experience assumes the very principle in question. Hume accepted the logic and then explained our confidence psychologically — as habit and custom. Popper accepts Hume's logic and rejects the psychology. We do not form expectations by repetition; we bring expectations to experience, and experience's only job is to knock them down.

The problem, split into answerable pieces

Popper's actual method in chapter 1 is to refuse the question as usually posed and break it into separate ones — some of which have a clean answer. The distinction between the third question and the first is the one most readers miss, and it is where the whole dispute with his critics lives.

Chapters 2 & Appendix

The Engine, and the Mind That Runs It

One four-step loop, applied from the amoeba to general relativity.

If knowledge does not come from experience, where does it come from? Popper's answer is that all knowledge — biological, perceptual, scientific — is produced by the same cycle, and that the cycle starts not with observation but with a problem. This four-term schema is the most portable thing in the book, and the piece that later becomes the backbone of David Deutsch's work.

Chapters 3 & 4

Epistemology Without a Knowing Subject

The claim the book is named for, and the one philosophers still argue about.

Almost all epistemology since Descartes has asked about the states of a knowing mind: what do I believe, and am I justified? Popper says this is the wrong object of study. The knowledge that matters — the content of a theory, the logical consequences it has whether or not anyone has noticed them, the problem it leaves unsolved — is not a mental state and does not depend on there being a mind that holds it.

The practical consequence If knowledge is a World 3 object, then the right question about a theory is never "how confident is its author?" or "what are their credentials?" but "what does it say, what does it forbid, and where does it break?" Popper thinks the entire tradition of asking after the sources of knowledge — reason, sensation, authority, revelation — is a hangover from asking who should rule. The better question is structural: how do we arrange things so that errors get found?
Chapters 2, 5 & 9

Realism, and What Truth Is For

Theories are guesses about how things actually are — not summaries, not tools.

A recurring worry about Popper is that if we can never justify a theory, and corroboration confers no probability of truth, then talk of truth has quietly dropped out and we are left with an elaborate game. Popper's answer occupies chapters 2, 5 and 9, and it is unambiguous: theories are attempts to describe a real world that exists independently of us, they are true or false regardless of what anyone believes, and the fact that we cannot certify which is which does not make the distinction idle.

He positions this between two rivals he thinks are both wrong. The first is essentialism — the doctrine that science aims at ultimate explanations, at descriptions of the essences or natures of things that need no further explanation and admit of none. Popper's objection is not that essences are unreal but that the doctrine is a stop sign: it invites us to declare an explanation final, and any explanation declared final is one nobody will try to get behind. Newton's gravitation was treated this way for two centuries; Einstein got behind it.

The second rival is instrumentalism, the view — held in various forms by Berkeley, Mach, Duhem and, in Popper's reading, by Bohr — that theories are not descriptions of anything but calculating devices for generating predictions from observations, and so are neither true nor false, only more or less useful. Popper's objection here is sharper and more interesting: an instrument cannot be refuted. A hammer that fails at a task is not shown to be false; it is simply the wrong tool, and you reach for another. If theories are instruments, then the anomalies that drive science forward are not discoveries about the world but merely notes on the limits of applicability, and the entire critical enterprise loses its point.

Tarski, and why Popper stopped being embarrassed about truth

Popper is candid that until the mid-1930s he had been uneasy about the notion of truth, because the correspondence theory — a statement is true if it corresponds to the facts — seemed either empty or paradoxical. What rehabilitated it for him was Alfred Tarski's work of 1933, which showed how to define truth for a formalised language without contradiction, by talking about the object language in a richer metalanguage. The famous schema — "snow is white" is true if and only if snow is white — looks trivial, and its triviality is the point: it shows that correspondence can be stated precisely and consistently, which is all Popper needed.

What this buys him is a distinction he uses constantly and which the rest of his system would collapse without: the difference between truth and certainty. Truth is objective and absolute — a statement is true or it is not, and no amount of belief affects this. Certainty is a psychological state, is always attainable and is worth nothing. Popper's whole epistemology is an attempt to keep the first while abandoning the second, and his complaint against most of the tradition is that it conflated them and then, finding certainty unavailable, concluded that truth must be relative too.

Commonsense realism, yes — the commonsense theory of knowledge, no This is the argument of chapter 2, and the title "Two Faces of Common Sense" names the split. Common sense is right that there is a real world of chairs and stars, existing independently of observers, and Popper defends this vigorously against idealism and subjectivism. Common sense is wrong about how we come to know it — it holds the bucket theory, that knowledge is what has flowed in through the senses. Popper's position is that you should keep the first face and discard the second, and that almost all of modern philosophy has done the reverse: grown sophisticated about knowledge while losing its nerve about the world.
Chapters 5 & 9

What Science Is Actually For

Not prediction, not saving the phenomena — explanation.

Popper's answer is deliberately plain: the aim of science is to find satisfactory explanations of whatever strikes us as being in need of explanation. Prediction is a by-product and a testing device, not the goal. This puts him against the instrumentalists, for whom a theory is a calculating tool that is neither true nor false, and against the positivists, for whom explanation is suspect metaphysics.

Verisimilitude — the idea, and the formalism that failed Popper wanted to say that a false theory can still be closer to the truth than another false theory, and proposed measuring it as truth content minus falsity content. In 1974 — two years after this book — David Miller and Pavel Tichý independently showed the definition collapses: on it, no false theory can ever be closer to the truth than another. Popper accepted the refutation. The intuition survives and is used constantly; the formal account does not. Read chapter 2's confident passages on verisimilitude with that in mind.
Chapters 6 & 7

Clouds, Clocks, and the Tree of Knowledge

Where the subtitle — "an evolutionary approach" — earns its place.

Popper's most original move is to treat the growth of knowledge as continuous with the growth of life. Both proceed by variation and selection; both are ways of solving problems set by an environment. The difference is one that matters enormously, and he states it in a sentence that is the emotional centre of the book: we can let our theories die in our stead.

Chapter 4

Understanding, and How to Read Anything

The most useful chapter in the book, and the least discussed.

Chapter 4 asks what it means to understand a text, an action, a work of art or a historical event. The traditional answer in the German tradition Popper grew up in — Dilthey's, and later Collingwood's — is empathy: you understand a past agent by re-enacting their mental states, putting yourself in their position, recovering what it felt like to be them. Popper thinks this is exactly wrong, and that it makes the humanities look unrigorous for no good reason.

His alternative follows straight from the three worlds. Understanding is not a World 2 operation on someone else's World 2 states, which we have no access to anyway. It is a World 3 operation: you reconstruct the problem situation — the problem the author or agent was facing, the background of assumptions they were working within, the options that were available, and what would have counted for them as a solution. Having done that, you can assess whether the move they made was adequate to the problem, which is something you can argue about with evidence and be shown to have got wrong. Empathy offers nothing to argue about.

Popper calls the method situational analysis or situational logic, and he offers it as the characteristic method of the social sciences and of history. The explanation of an action is a reconstruction of the situation as the agent saw it, plus the assumption that the agent acted appropriately to it. That assumption — he calls it the rationality principle — is almost certainly false in many individual cases, and Popper says so; he treats it as a deliberately simplifying element, so that when an explanation fails it is the reconstruction of the situation, not human rationality, that we should suspect first.

The parallel

Why This Is Also a Political Theory

The same mistake, in two disciplines — and the same repair.

Popper never separated his epistemology from his politics, and Objective Knowledge keeps gesturing at a connection worked out at length in The Open Society and Its Enemies. The link is not an analogy or a metaphor. It is that the two fields have made the same structural error, which is to ask a question about authority when they should be asking a question about correction.

In epistemology the bad question is: what are the sources of our knowledge? Reason, the senses, intuition, revelation, authority — the tradition has fought over which of these guarantees truth, and Popper's answer is that none does, that knowledge has no pure source, and that the pedigree of an idea is irrelevant to its worth. A theory that arrived in a dream is not thereby worse than one derived from careful observation; both must face the same tests. The right question is not where a belief came from but how we can find out whether it is wrong.

In politics the bad question is: who should rule? The best, the wisest, the majority, the workers, the philosopher-kings — Plato posed it in this form and, on Popper's account, poisoned political theory for two thousand years, because every answer to it licenses the concentration of unaccountable power in whoever qualifies. The right question, once you accept that rulers will sometimes be incompetent or malicious, is institutional: how do we arrange things so that bad rulers can be removed, and can do limited damage while they are there? On this view democracy is not fundamentally about the wisdom of the people or the legitimacy of majorities. It is defined by a single property — that a government can be got rid of without violence.

Downstream

Popper → Deutsch

What The Beginning of Infinity takes, what it sharpens, and where it departs.

Deutsch is explicit that his epistemology is Popper's, and reading them in order is the single best argument for starting here. Nearly every foundational move in The Fabric of Reality and The Beginning of Infinity — the rejection of induction and empiricism, fallibilism, knowledge as conjecture plus criticism, problems as the engine — is in this book first. What Deutsch adds is a sharper criterion and a much longer reach.

Against

The Standing Objections

Popper has been argued with for sixty years. Here is where the pressure is.
01

You can never falsify a single hypothesis — the Duhem–Quine problem

A test never confronts one theory; it confronts the theory plus assumptions about instruments, background conditions and auxiliary hypotheses. When the result comes out wrong, logic alone does not say which member of the bundle to blame. Popper knew this and answered with methodological rules — do not save a theory by ad hoc rescue — but rules are not deductions, and this is the deepest crack in the edifice.

02

Scientists do not behave this way — Kuhn and Lakatos

Kuhn observed that anomalies are usually tolerated, not treated as refutations, and that theories are abandoned only when a replacement exists. Lakatos tried to rescue Popper by shifting the unit from the single theory to the research programme, with a protected hard core. Popper's reply is that he is describing what makes an activity rational, not what working scientists happen to do.

03

If corroboration says nothing about the future, why act on it?

Popper insists that a well-tested theory is not thereby more likely to be true. But we do board the aircraft. Critics — Salmon most sharply — argue that either corroboration carries some forward-looking weight, in which case something like induction is back, or it does not, in which case Popper has no account of rational action. This is the objection Popper answered least convincingly.

04

The formal theory of verisimilitude is dead

Refuted in 1974 by Miller and Tichý, and Popper conceded. Later repairs exist and none commands agreement. Since "closer to the truth" is doing real load-bearing work whenever Popper explains why we should prefer a better-corroborated theory, this is not a footnote.

05

World 3 is either trivial or mysterious

The complaint from both directions: if World 3 just means "what books and arguments say", it is an unremarkable way of speaking; if it means more, it needs a metaphysics Popper never supplies. He was uninterested in the objection, holding that the autonomy argument — invented sequences producing unbidden consequences — settles it.

06

And a note on the prose

This is a collection of lectures and papers, not a book written straight through, and it repeats itself considerably. Chapters 8 and 9 are technical and can be skipped on a first pass. Popper is also a pugnacious writer who spends real space on grievances against contemporaries.

  1. There is no such thing as induction, and nothing is lost.We never reason from instances to laws. We guess laws and then hunt for the instance that kills them. The asymmetry between confirmation and refutation is doing all the logical work.
  2. Observation is always selective."Observe!" is not an instruction anyone can follow. A theory, an interest or a problem must come first to say what counts as data — the searchlight, not the bucket.
  3. Ask what a theory forbids, not who holds it.The question about sources — reason, senses, authority — is a leftover from asking who should rule. Replace it with an institutional question: how do we arrange things so errors are found fast?
  4. The best theories are the least probable.Content, testability and explanatory depth all rise together, and logical probability falls as they do. Safety and informativeness pull in opposite directions.
  5. Progress is measured in better problems.P₂ is never P₁. A field that has stopped generating new and harder problems is not consolidating; it has stopped.
  6. Knowledge exists outside heads, and that is what makes criticism possible.Once a guess is written down it becomes an object others can attack. This, for Popper, is what language is for and why it changed everything.
  7. Let the theory die instead of you.The one-sentence summary of the whole book, and the reason Popper thought his epistemology was also a moral and political position.
