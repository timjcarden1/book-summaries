The Causes of Portuguese Backwardness

As Causas do Atraso Português

Portugal was not behind in 1700. Brazilian gold made it so.

Nuno Palma · Dom Quixote · 2023

The whole book in one breath

Palma, an economic historian at Manchester, spent a decade rebuilding Portuguese GDP, wages, prices, population and public finances from the archives, year by year, back to 1527. This book is what those numbers say, and they are uncomfortable in every direction. Portugal was not behind in 1700: income per head doubled between 1527 and about 1750. It fell behind in the eighteenth century, and the cause was Brazilian gold, which gave the Crown money without needing the Cortes, wrecked manufacturing and led to the destruction of the school system. Almost everything the country usually blames (empire, Catholicism, the Inquisition, the 1755 earthquake, Napoleon, the Estado Novo) happened at the wrong time or points the wrong way. And the same mechanism, he argues, is running again today under a new name: EU funds.

The argument

Why is Portugal rich by world standards and poor by European ones?

The question in the book’s first line is the whole book. The historical answer is that the backwardness is about three centuries deep and has a datable origin. The contemporary answer is that the mechanism which produced it around 1720 is running again now.

Palma opens with Antero de Quental’s famous 1871 lecture at the Casino Lisbonense, which blamed Portuguese decline on Catholicism and the Counter-Reformation in the sixteenth century. Palma’s opening move is to say that Quental was wrong about both the date and the cause. The rest of the book is the demonstration.

Method

The method is the argument

With Jaime Reis, Palma reconstructed real GDP per head annually from 1527 to 1850 from the account books of convents, hospitals, charities, palaces, farms, the University of Coimbra, cathedrals and town councils. Prices, wages and rents were converted out of a chaos of local units (an almude of wine meant 16.8 litres in Lisbon and 25.4 in Porto) and deflated with a Portuguese consumption basket: bread, meat, olive oil, chickens, eggs, wine, soap, linen, candles and firewood.

He is blunt about the limits. Levels before the nineteenth century carry at least ten per cent of error, so small differences between countries mean nothing. What the series identifies reliably is direction and timing. That distinction does most of the work in the book: it lets him say with confidence that income fell sharply from the 1770s without claiming to know exactly how rich Portugal was in 1750. Much of Portugal’s received history, it turns out, is about the wrong decade.

What to take from it

Eight lessons

Method

Timing is the whole argument

Most explanations of Portuguese decline fail on chronology alone. Build the annual series first, then see which causes sit on the right side of the turning point.

Ch. 6

Windfalls end the need to negotiate

The Crown stopped summoning the Cortes because it no longer needed their money. That is the resource curse’s real bite.

Ch. 6

Destroying is instant, rebuilding takes centuries

Twenty thousand pupils in 1759, and not twenty thousand again until the 1930s, in a country three times larger.

Throughout

De jure is not de facto

Compulsory schooling was decreed in 1835 and stayed a dead letter. The national failure is unenforced intentions, not bad ones.

Ch. 8–9

Reform that fights the culture loses

The First Republic built schools and the children didn’t come. The dictatorship aligned schooling with Catholic families, and they sent their children.

Ch. 9

Levels and trends are different questions

Portugal in 1974 had Western Europe’s worst education and had been improving faster than ever. Confusing the two misassigns credit and blame.

Ch. 10

The debated causes are symptoms

Taxes, productivity, courts and bad politicians are real and endogenous. The question is why a country that can name its problems doesn’t demand solutions.

Throughout

Every regime blames its predecessor

Pombal blamed the Jesuits, the liberals absolutism, the republicans the monarchy, the Estado Novo the Republic, and today’s regime the Estado Novo.

Part I

Portugal since the late Middle Ages

The shorter half, and the one Palma calls objective: population, institutions and the economy, traced in detail to the early nineteenth century and then to the present. Its job is to fix the facts Part II must explain, and to remove several favourite explanations before the argument begins.

  • Portugal has kept its borders and language since the thirteenth century, remarkable in Europe, though the country has no natural geographic unity. Whatever else it lacked, it never had an identity problem.
  • Hearth counts from 1527–32, 1706–12 and a 1758 parish survey, joined by parish registers, give an annual series. The flattening in the early eighteenth century is emigration to the gold fields of Minas Gerais, three to ten thousand people a year.
  • An influential literature claims southern European women married younger, worked less and were paid half of men’s wages, which would explain southern backwardness culturally. Palma’s work with Reis and Lisbeth Rodrigues finds it false for Portugal. Women married late, often stayed single, worked after marriage, and earned about two-thirds of men’s wages, the northern European ratio.
  • Fertility fell from about three children per woman in 1960 to 1.4 by 1994 and has stayed there.

Chapter 2

Political Institutions

The Cortes constrained the Crown, by several measures better than the English Commons, until the seventeenth century.

  • The standard story (North and Thomas, Tilly, and above all Acemoglu and Robinson in Why Nations Fail) holds that Iberian crowns were more despotic than England’s from the Middle Ages. Palma and António Castro Henriques went and counted.
  • In the Portuguese Cortes the decisive voice belonged to the Povos, the elected procurators of the towns, who alone were taxed and so alone voted on taxes. Porto’s oldest surviving electoral roll, from 1497, lists cobblers, a notary and a knight.
  • Between 1385 and 1600 the Cortes rejected 7.3% of royal tax requests. The English Parliament rejected none in the same period. Then the positions swap. The last Iberian reductions came in 1625 (Castile) and 1674 (Portugal), and the Cortes last met in 1697–98, then not again for 122 years.
  • England’s constitutional revolution is a seventeenth-century event. The Iberian collapse is two separate events, Castile first and Portugal three-quarters of a century later, with two causes that Chapter 6 supplies.

Chapter 3

Economy and Development

An annual income series that puts the catastrophe in the wrong century for almost every conventional explanation.

  • Average income per head roughly doubled between 1527 and the mid-eighteenth century, so the idea of decline ever since the Discoveries is simply wrong.
  • The catastrophe comes from the 1770s and wipes out three centuries of gains. The 1755 earthquake is two decades too early to cause it and the French invasions of 1807 three decades too late.
  • In 1750 Portugal compares well with Sweden, Germany, France and Spain. What distinguishes it is never once managing rapid sustained growth in the two centuries that followed.
  • Spain’s decline starts in the sixteenth century, and Portugal’s only in the eighteenth: two episodes of the same disease, silver from Potosí and gold from Minas Gerais.
  • Between 1950 and 2000 income per head multiplied by seven, which Palma calls the most important event in Portuguese history measured by human welfare. Then growth stalled again.

Part II

The argument

Seven chapters that test each candidate cause against the timeline, find the real one, and follow its consequences through every regime to the present.

Chapter 4

Expansion and Empire

A sea empire of trading posts, useful to the Crown’s budget and marginal to the country.

  • Portugal never had a land empire and couldn’t have: Asia around 1600 had over 350 million people against Europe’s 75 million. What it had was a network of trading posts and forts resting on two technologies where Europe led, gun-armed warships and European-designed fortresses. Presence depended on local tolerance, and in Macau and Japan it ended when rulers decided.
  • Most seventeenth-century losses were shifts in Asian politics, not Dutch victories. Where the Dutch won, better use of force and diplomacy explains it. In Brazil the heavily capitalised Dutch West India Company was beaten.
  • Crown revenue is not national wealth. Imperial receipts mattered to the treasury in some decades, but the state was a small part of the economy (see the table below).
  • Portugal did pass Brazil a worse institutional design than England passed the United States, Palma concedes, because by the eighteenth century gold had already wrecked Portugal’s own institutions.

Chapter 5

Culture and Religion

Culture responds to politics more than the reverse, and the data show nothing to explain before the 1700s.

  • The case against Weber’s Protestant-ethic thesis: in mixed Prussia, Catholics and Protestants show no gap in savings or literacy once the Polish minority is set aside. England’s growth starts a century after Henry VIII. Catholic Belgium industrialised second, and France cut fertility first.
  • The Inquisition is sized honestly, not defended. It was a court that required evidence, acquitted many and executed fewer than two thousand people over three centuries. It policed heresy, “blood purity” and morals rather than science. Early-modern intolerance was general, so it can’t explain why some countries grew and others didn’t.
  • What actually shut Portuguese science down was political. The Jesuit University of Évora, which taught science, was closed in 1759 and not reopened for two centuries. The man who ordered it was Pombal, and the Inquisitor-General who condemned the Jesuit Malagrida was Pombal’s own brother.
  • The Newtonian and Royal Society fellow Bento de Moura Portugal died in Pombal’s prison in 1766. These catastrophes, Palma writes, had a political origin, not a religious one.

  • The road not taken. After peace with Spain in 1668, Portugal was growing and industrialising. Income rose from about 900 dollars to over 1,200 by the 1690s, and a series of laws from 1672 to 1698 pursued import substitution under the Count of Ericeira. By 1680 Portuguese textiles were exported to Castile.
  • Gold. Large finds in Minas Gerais in 1694 were public by 1697. By 1705 some fifty thousand prospectors and their slaves lived in the gold towns. Hume in 1742 and Adam Smith in 1776 both noticed that gold and silver had impoverished Spain and Portugal.
  • Two channels (see the figure below). The economic one is “Dutch disease”: non-tradable prices rose about 30%, farmers turned grain land to pasture, and the trade deficit tracked the gold arrivals, from 2.4% of GDP in 1720 to 6.1% in 1756. The political one mattered more: a windfall with no strings attached removed the one constraint that had forced the Crown to bargain.
  • Pombal was a symptom before a cause. Royal power was already absolute when the 1755 earthquake made him the state’s central figure. Palma still judges him without softening: perhaps the worst politician ever to govern Portugal.
  • 1759, the single worst decision. About twenty thousand pupils were in pre-university education, nearly all taught free by the Jesuits in some twenty colleges across Portugal. The Jesuits were expelled, 1,100 of about 1,500 exiled and 80 dying in prison, and nothing replaced them. Portugal would not have twenty thousand such pupils again until the 1930s, with nearly three times the population.

Chapter 7

The Constitutional Monarchy

By mid-century, the most backward country in Western Europe, a position it has held ever since.

  • Between 1820 and 1851 there were five coups, two civil wars and four state bankruptcies. The liberal victory in 1834 came through British intervention, and Palma notes that the British had lent money to the liberals.
  • “Liberal” is only a legal label. In economic policy the regime kept some of Europe’s highest tariffs from 1837 through the first age of globalisation.
  • In 1834 about 450 male religious houses were dissolved and their property auctioned in ways that favoured insiders. They were the last literate institutions spread across the whole country. Compulsory primary schooling was decreed in 1835 and stayed a dead letter: nothing had been learned from Pombal.
  • Fontes Pereira de Melo’s railways and roads, financed by deficits and British credit, disappointed because physical capital came without human capital. Literacy in textile mills ran below 20%.
  • The Brazilian revolution of 1889 cut emigrant remittances by over 80%, and Portugal defaulted in 1891. Politically Palma rates the regime moderately positive: parliaments met, from 1878 the vote reached 72% of adult men including illiterates, and censorship was low. Economically it was a century lost.

Chapter 8

The First Republic, 1910–1926

It made literacy its declared priority, then restricted the vote to the literate.

  • Palma characterises the regime as sectarian and radical, and less democratic than the monarchy it replaced. Elections were neither free nor fair, a political police intimidated opponents, and in 1921 the “ghost lorry” killed the outgoing prime minister and two heroes of 1910.
  • It had forty ministers of instruction in thirteen years. Plans for compulsory schooling, teacher training and new universities mostly stayed on paper.
  • Crucifixes were banned from classrooms, religion was removed from schooling and classes were made mixed. In a rural, Catholic country where children were real labour, parents did not give that labour up to have their children taught republican secularism. Adult illiteracy fell only from about 70% in 1911 to 62% in 1926.
  • Entry into the First World War in 1916 was a deliberate choice to defend the African empire, a decade before the Estado Novo. And by 1923–24 the last republican governments had already stabilised the public finances, correcting a favourite Salazarist myth.

  • Palma builds a fence before the argument. Political prisons, censorship and sham elections are unacceptable, no economic success justifies them, and he has no sympathy for the regime. Then he asks what actually happened.
  • With Salazar the danger is the reverse of Pombal’s: the rhetoric praised poverty and doubted the value of reading, while the regime’s actual record on schooling was strong.
  • More than nine thousand conscript records from 1924–1950 show every social group gaining literacy, the poorest included. The mechanism wasn’t spending. Schools were aligned with Catholic, conservative families (single-sex classes, crucifixes back, a single national reader), and the families sent their children. Palma and Reis attribute about 70% of the fall in illiteracy to that cultural alignment, 20% to better living conditions and only 10% to school building.
  • Food consumption rose steadily. Between 1948/9 and 1974, milk rose from 11.9 to 57.3 litres a head, chicken from 1.3 to 11.9 kg and beef from 3.8 to 14.3 kg.
  • Growth came from opening to Europe, through the Marshall Plan, EFTA in 1960 and GATT, not from Africa. Colonial exports were about 3% of GDP, and the empire was a growing net charge on the budget from 1961.
  • Palma argues the regime was authoritarian, nationalist and anti-parliamentary but lacked the defining features of fascism, and that the label now does political work: blaming a regime gone for half a century absolves what has been done since.

  • Convergence with richer Europe ran from about 1950, stalled after the 1974 revolution, resumed in the 1990s and stopped around 2000. Since then most Central and Eastern European members, far poorer in the 1990s, have overtaken Portugal.
  • Low productivity, weak human capital, slow courts and high taxes are all real and all endogenous: things to explain, not explanations. The real puzzle is the absence of demand for reform.
  • The economic channel repeats the eighteenth century: money flows into non-tradables and public works, and firms receiving funds gain sales but not productivity. The political channel is decisive: outside money lets governments hand out unearned rents and breaks the link between bad policy and recession that voters need to feel.
  • Why did Eastern Europe take the same money and converge? Palma argues communism inoculated it against romance about the state. Portugal’s post-1974 settlement did the opposite, identifying capitalism with the old regime and socialism with democracy.
  • He ends pessimistic by his own admission. The solutions are known, and the patient keeps voting against the medicine. If trends continue, Portugal could be the poorest country in the EU within a decade.

Context

Where a careful reader should push back

The 1690s counterfactual carries heavy weight

The claim that Portugal was on an industrialising path rests on one decade, a handful of state-sponsored factories and one good set of numbers. Whether it would have survived the loss of Brazil is asserted more than shown.

Culture is dismissed, then used

Chapter 5 says culture follows politics and can’t be a fundamental cause. Chapter 9’s key finding is that schooling worked because it aligned with the culture. That the culture is downstream of politics is a claim about direction, not a demonstration.

The two resource curses aren’t symmetric

Gold ran at 4–7% of GDP into a state with no parliament. EU funds run at 2–3% into an audited democracy. The political-accountability argument is strong; the Dutch-disease analogy is weaker.

The Estado Novo chapter will be misread

Palma condemns the dictatorship in plain words, but the carefully quantified good news inevitably reads as more favourable than the condemnation, and he knows it will be quoted that way.

Nobody is to blame

He treats party behaviour as a product of circumstance and declines to blame any current party. That is consistent, untestable, and leaves no domestic actor with a reason to try.
