As Causas do Atraso Português

The Causes of
Portuguese
Backwardness Why Portugal is rich by world standards and poor by European ones — a quantitative economic history that dates the fall precisely, and blames almost nobody the country expects.

Palma is an economic historian at Manchester who spent a decade rebuilding Portuguese GDP, wages, prices, population and fiscal capacity from the archives, year by year, back to 1527. This book is what those numbers say, and what they say is uncomfortable in every direction: Portugal was not behind in 1700. It became behind in the eighteenth century, and the cause was Brazilian gold. Everything the country normally blames — empire, Catholicism, the Inquisition, the 1755 earthquake, Napoleon, the Estado Novo — either happened at the wrong time or points the wrong way.

The Book, and the Shape of Its Argument

What Palma is claiming, why it is unusual, and how the ten chapters fit together.

The question in the first line of the preamble is the whole book: why is Portugal today a rich country by world standards but a poor one in a European context? Palma's answer has two halves. The historical half says the backwardness is roughly three centuries deep and has a datable origin. The contemporary half says the mechanism that produced it in 1720 is running again now, under a different name — EU structural funds.

The book opens where every Portuguese discussion of decline has opened since 1871: with Antero de Quental at the Casino Lisbonense, arguing that Portugal fell in the sixteenth century because it stayed Catholic and submitted to the Counter-Reformation. Palma's opening move is to say that Quental — whose lecture Eduardo Lourenço called the founding act of modern Portuguese culture, and who shot himself on a garden bench in Ponta Delgada twenty years later — was wrong about both the date and the cause. The rest of the book is the demonstration.

The four things this book is arguing against
Myth 1

Empire made Portugal rich

Intercontinental trade was a marginal share of a farming economy in the 16th and 17th centuries. It mattered to the Crown's budget in some decades. It never made the country rich, and in the eighteenth century it made it poor.

Myth 2

Catholicism held it back

Literacy, numeracy, family structure and income show no Catholic–Protestant gap before the eighteenth century. Belgium industrialised second. Ireland is now among the richest countries in Europe.

Myth 3

Absolutism was ancient

The Cortes rejected 7.3% of royal tax requests between 1385 and 1600; the English Parliament rejected none. Real Portuguese absolutism is an eighteenth-century phenomenon, and it has a cause.

Myth 4

The Estado Novo caused the gap

Portugal was already the poorest and most illiterate country in Western Europe when the regime inherited it. Under it, income per head multiplied by five and child illiteracy went from 58% to 3%.

"Everyone who has governed in Portuguese affairs by discourse has erred and been lost; and by this route were lost, even among us, those whom men held to be of the greatest judgement."Padre António Vieira · epigraph to the book

A note on method, because the method is the argument

Palma's authority here is not interpretive but archival and statistical. With Jaime Reis he reconstructed Portuguese real GDP per head annually from 1527 to 1850 out of account books from convents, monasteries, hospitals, misericórdias, palaces, farms, the University of Coimbra, the cathedrals of Lisbon and Porto, and municipal chambers — prices, wages and rents, converted into a modern metric system out of a chaos of local units where an almude meant 16.8 litres in Lisbon and 25.4 in Porto. The consumption basket used to deflate it is Portuguese rather than the standard Strasbourg one: 182kg of bread, 26kg of meat, 5.2 litres of olive oil, five chickens, 52 eggs, 28 litres of wine, soap, linen, candles and firewood, with wine substituted for beer and olive oil for butter.

He is blunt about what that buys him and what it does not. Levels before the nineteenth century carry at least ten per cent of error, so small differences between countries mean nothing; what the series identifies reliably is direction and timing. That distinction does almost all the work in the book. It is why he can say with confidence that Portuguese income fell sharply from the 1770s without claiming to know exactly how rich Portugal was in 1750 — and why a great deal of received Portuguese history turns out to be about the wrong decade.

The ten chapters at a glance
I · 1PopulationOne million people in 1500, ten million now. Portugal sits west of the Hajnal line on every measure — late female marriage, high celibacy, female labour force participation, no sex-selective neglect. Family structure cannot explain the gap. I · 2Political institutionsThe Cortes as a real constraint on the Crown, more effective than the English Commons until the mid-seventeenth century — and then, in 1698, silent for 122 years. I · 3Economy and developmentThe series: income doubles from 1527 to a peak around 1750, then collapses to its early-sixteenth-century level by 1850, and is not recovered until the twentieth century. II · 4Expansion and empireA thalassocracy of forts and factories, not a land empire. Fiscally useful in some decades, economically marginal in most, and a genuine contribution to the Scientific Revolution. II · 5Culture and religionWeber tested and rejected. The Inquisition as an instrument of politics — Pombal's own brother ran it — rather than a cause of scientific backwardness. II · 6The golden curseThe core chapter. Dutch disease plus the disappearance of fiscal bargaining, and the single worst decision in Portuguese history: the expulsion of the Jesuits in 1759. II · 7A new, liberal country?Neither new nor liberal. Five coups, two civil wars and four bankruptcies before 1851; tariffs among Europe's highest; compulsory schooling decreed in 1835 and never delivered. II · 8The First RepublicSixteen years, forty ministers of education, and a franchise deliberately narrowed to keep the rural illiterate majority away from the ballot box. Its schools stood empty. II · 9The Estado NovoThe chapter that will cost him friends: a dictatorship, condemned as such, that nonetheless ended child illiteracy and presided over the fastest growth in Portuguese history. II · 10The contemporary periodDiverging since the late 1990s. EU funds as the new Brazilian gold: they anaesthetise the economy, break political accountability, and should — he says plainly — stop.
