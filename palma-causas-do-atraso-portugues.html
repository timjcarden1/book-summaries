The Causes of Portuguese Backwardness

As Causas do Atraso Português

Portugal was not behind in 1700. Brazilian gold made it so.

Nuno Palma · Dom Quixote · 2023

The whole book in one breath

Palma, an economic historian at Manchester, spent a decade rebuilding Portuguese GDP, wages, prices, population and public finances from the archives, year by year, back to 1527. This book is what those numbers say, and they are uncomfortable in every direction. Portugal was not behind in 1700: income per head doubled between 1527 and about 1750. It fell behind in the eighteenth century, and the cause was Brazilian gold, which gave the Crown money without needing the Cortes, wrecked manufacturing and led to the destruction of the school system. Almost everything the country usually blames (empire, Catholicism, the Inquisition, the 1755 earthquake, Napoleon, the Estado Novo) happened at the wrong time or points the wrong way. And the same mechanism, he argues, is running again today under a new name: EU funds.

Preamble and introduction

Why is Portugal rich by world standards and poor by European ones?

That question, in the first line of the preamble, is the whole book. Palma gives it two answers. The historical answer is that the backwardness is about three centuries deep and has a date: it begins in the eighteenth century, not the sixteenth. The contemporary answer is that the mechanism which produced it after the 1690s, money arriving from outside with nothing asked in return, is running again now under a different name, European Union funds.

Where every Portuguese argument about decline starts

  • The book opens where the national debate has opened since 1871: with Antero de Quental’s lecture on the causes of the decline of the peninsular peoples, given at the Casino Lisbonense. Quental blamed Catholicism and the Counter-Reformation, absolutism, and the conquests, and dated the fall to the sixteenth century. Eduardo Lourenço later called the lecture the founding act of modern Portuguese culture. Quental shot himself on a garden bench in Ponta Delgada twenty years later.
  • Palma’s opening move is to say that Quental was wrong about both the date and the cause, and that most of what the country has repeated since inherits the mistake. The rest of the book is the demonstration.
  • The book’s epigraph is from Padre António Vieira, the seventeenth-century Jesuit: those who have governed Portuguese affairs by discourse, by talk rather than evidence, have erred and been lost, even the ones people took for the wisest. It is a warning about method as much as about politics.
  • He is explicit about how the book is built. Part I, he says, is the objective half, a record of population, institutions and income; Part II is the interpretation, and Chapter 10, on the present, is the most subjective chapter in the book.
  • He separates proximate from fundamental causes throughout. Low productivity, little schooling, weak courts and bad government are real, but they are things to be explained. The fundamental causes are the ones that explain them, and they have to sit on the right side of the turning point in time.
  • The target is not only Quental. The international literature on why north-western Europe got rich (Douglass North, Charles Tilly, and above all Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson) treats Spain and Portugal as absolutist from the Middle Ages, which Palma thinks is the Portuguese liberals’ own myth exported abroad.

Everyone who has governed Portuguese affairs by talk has erred and been lost.Padre António Vieira, the book’s epigraph (paraphrased)

Method

The method is the argument

Palma’s authority here is archival and statistical, not interpretive. Most of the book rests on about twenty-five of his own papers, many written with the economic historian Jaime Reis, and on 1,032 notes. The central object is an annual series of real GDP per head for 1527–1850, built with Reis from prices, wages, rents and population, and joined to the standard series for later years.

How the series was built

  • Sources. Institutions that lasted for centuries, kept continuous audited accounts and bought and sold in the market: town councils, hospitals, misericórdias (charitable brotherhoods), prisons, the University of Coimbra, orphanages, convents and monasteries, the cathedrals of Lisbon and Porto, palaces and farms. The data cover four regions, represented by Porto, Coimbra, Lisbon and Évora.
  • Units. Everything had to be converted out of a chaos of local measures into metric units. An almude of wine was 16.8 litres in Lisbon, 25.4 in Porto, 16.7 in Coimbra and 17.4 in Évora; charcoal in Lisbon was sold in five different units.
  • Wages. A weighted wage of skilled men (masons, excluding master masons) and unskilled helpers, in agriculture or building, where no payment in kind blurred the figure.
  • The basket. Prices are deflated with a Portuguese consumption basket rather than the standard northern one, with wine for beer and olive oil for butter (see the figure).
  • Error. Levels before the nineteenth century carry at least 10% of error, so small gaps between countries mean nothing. What the series identifies reliably is direction and timing.
  • That distinction does most of the work in the book. It lets him say with confidence that income fell sharply from the 1770s without claiming to know exactly how rich Portugal was in 1750, and it is why so much received Portuguese history turns out to be about the wrong decade.
  • The unit throughout is the “1990 international dollar” (Geary–Khamis purchasing-power dollars), the standard unit of historical national accounts, in which about 400 a head a year is bare subsistence.

What to take from it

Eight lessons

Method

Timing is the whole argument

Most explanations of Portuguese decline fail on chronology alone. Build the annual series first, then see which causes sit on the right side of the turning point.

Ch. 2, 6

Windfalls end the need to negotiate

The Crown stopped summoning the Cortes because it no longer needed their money. That, more than inflation or the exchange rate, is the resource curse’s real bite.

Ch. 6

Destroying is instant, rebuilding takes centuries

About twenty thousand pupils in 1759, and not twenty thousand again until the 1930s, in a country nearly three times larger.

Throughout

De jure is not de facto

Compulsory schooling was decreed in 1835 and stayed a dead letter. The recurring failure is unenforced intentions, not bad ones.

Ch. 8–9

Reform that fights the culture loses

The First Republic built schools and the children didn’t come. The dictatorship aligned schooling with Catholic families, and the same families sent the same children.

Ch. 9

Levels and trends are different questions

Portugal in 1974 had Western Europe’s worst schooling and had been improving faster than ever. Confusing the two misassigns credit and blame.

Ch. 10

The debated causes are symptoms

Taxes, productivity, courts and bad politicians are real and endogenous. The question is why a country that can name its problems does not demand solutions.

Throughout

Every regime blames its predecessor

Pombal blamed the Jesuits, the liberals absolutism, the republicans the monarchy, the Estado Novo the Republic, and today’s regime the Estado Novo.

Part I

Portugal since the late Middle Ages

The shorter half, and the one Palma calls objective: population, institutions and the economy, each traced in detail from the late Middle Ages to the early nineteenth century and then forward to the present in summary. Its job is to fix the facts Part II must explain, and to remove several favourite explanations before the argument begins.

An old country without a natural shape

  • Portugal has kept its language and its borders in continuity since the Middle Ages, remarkable in Europe and almost unique in the world. The borders are a medieval political construction, fixed by the Treaty of Alcañices at the end of the thirteenth century and unchanged since, apart from the loss of Olivença in 1801.
  • This is all the more striking because, as the geographer Orlando Ribeiro showed, the country has no natural geographical unity: the south differs sharply from the north, and the coast from the interior. Whatever else Portugal lacked, Palma notes in passing, it never had an identity problem. (Chapter 7 will show that this was, oddly, a cost: nobody needed schools to make people Portuguese.)

Counting the people

  • The count starts with the numeramento of 1527–32, a national count of hearths (fogos), each an inhabited house living as one household. At four to five people a hearth this gives slightly over a million.
  • A second hearth count in 1706–12 gives 2.35 million; a parish survey in 1758, three years after the Lisbon earthquake, nearly 2.5 million; two rough censuses around 1800 almost three million. The first modern census, in 1864, counts 3.83 million.
  • Between these snapshots, parish registers of baptisms and burials allow an annual series to be interpolated, which is what the GDP-per-head series in Chapter 3 divides by.
  • Quental, incidentally, claimed Portugal had three million people under João II (r. 1481–95) and only one million by 1640, a collapse that fitted his thesis about the sixteenth century and never happened.
  • Growth flattens in the first half of the eighteenth century because of emigration to the gold fields of Minas Gerais in Brazil, between three and ten thousand people a year. The flat line after 1981 is a country that has stopped reproducing: fertility fell from about three children per woman in 1960 to 1.4 by 1994 and has stayed there.
  • Today about one Portuguese-born person in five lives outside Portugal.

West of the Hajnal line, on every measure

  • In 1965 the demographer John Hajnal drew a line from St Petersburg to Trieste. West of it prevailed the “European Marriage Pattern”: women marrying late, many never marrying, and (later work added) consensual marriage, property rights for women and more women in paid work.
  • The argument Palma is testing is a second split, north–south inside Western Europe, advanced by an influential Dutch literature: women in Italy, Spain and Portugal supposedly married younger, worked less, were paid a customary half of men’s wages whatever the market said, and therefore invested less in their children’s schooling. If true, it would neatly explain southern backwardness in cultural terms.
  • His work with Jaime Reis and Lisbeth Rodrigues finds it false for Portugal. Women’s age at first marriage was generally above 25 nationwide, and more than 10% never married.
  • Married women worked, and were self-employed at the same rate as in the Netherlands. Their wages ran at about two-thirds of men’s, the northern European ratio, not one-half. Inheritance rules were comparable.
  • There was no neglect of girls of the kind seen in China, India and the Middle East. The sex ratio of surviving children, if anything, ran the other way: in Porto between 1680 and 1780 most abandoned foundlings were boys, and in the mid-nineteenth century infant mortality was generally higher among boys.
  • Even in 1974, before the revolution, some 43% of Portuguese women of working age worked outside the home, close to the European average and well above Spain, Italy and Greece.
  • Where a real gap does open (women more discriminated against in the south), it opens in the twentieth century, after the economic divergence had already happened, and is more plausibly its consequence than its cause.

What the chapter removes from the table

  • Family structure, fertility norms and the treatment of women were, until at least the late nineteenth century, indistinguishable in Portugal from those of the countries that grew rich.
  • Since parental investment in children is the standard cultural route from family structure to schooling, this closes off the most popular non-political explanation of the gap before the argument has properly started.

The story being tested

  • This is the chapter that most directly attacks the international literature. The standard story (Douglass North and Robert Thomas, Charles Tilly, Thomas Ertman, John Maddicott, and above all Acemoglu and Robinson in Why Nations Fail) holds that the Iberian crowns were more despotic than England’s from the late Middle Ages, so that England’s later advantage had medieval roots.
  • In the underlying paper the targets are named: Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson (2005) class Portugal and Spain around 1500 as absolutist monarchies; Francis Fukuyama (2011) calls sixteenth-century Spain absolutist because it was not formally accountable to a parliament; some authors trace the divergence back to Magna Carta, or to the 1260s.
  • Palma and the historian António Castro Henriques went and counted: how often parliaments met and why, how often they refused or cut taxes, how often rulers debased the coinage, and what interest rates the Crown paid compared with private borrowers.
  • Their finding is that there is no evidence Portuguese or Spanish political institutions were worse until the English Civil War. England’s institutional divergence starts in the mid-seventeenth century, which is also when English income per head starts to grow persistently.

Who sat in the Cortes, and how they were chosen

  • The Cortes assembled the three estates, but the decisive voice was neither the clergy nor the nobility. It was the Povos, the procurators of the towns, who alone paid the taxes and therefore alone voted on them. They were elected.
  • In Loulé in 1385 the town council summoned the good men of the town “and the greater part of the people”, the poor included, to elect procurators to the Cortes of Coimbra, the assembly that then chose João I as king.
  • The oldest surviving electoral roll, Porto in 1497, lists 34 named voters (three cobblers, a notary and a knight among them), and the clerk adds that many others of the people voted whose names he did not know.
  • Lisbon in 1580 had an electorate of about a hundred, including 48 representatives of the craft guilds. Porto’s seventeenth-century Cortes elections averaged 128 voters and were routinely won by a handful of votes.
  • The English contrast is not flattering. Until 1628 elections in Newcastle, Bristol and Gloucester were controlled outright by closed local oligarchies. The Earl of Pembroke intervened decisively in nearly ninety elections for the parliaments of 1614 and 1628 and chose 98 members himself. The Speaker of the Commons was picked by the king and paid by the Crown.
  • Non-resident “carpet-bagger” members were common in England, forbidden in Castile until the seventeenth century, and very rare in Portugal.
  • Towns overseas had the right to sit in the Iberian Cortes: Goa from 1645, Salvador da Bahia from 1653, São Luís do Maranhão from 1676. The absence of that right in the English system was, famously, one cause of American independence.

The test that settles it: who could say no

  • Frequent parliaments can simply mean frequent taxation; during the Hundred Years War, English consent to taxes was little more than a formality. The better test is whether an assembly refused or cut what the ruler asked for.
  • Between 1385 and 1600 the Portuguese Cortes refused 7.3% of the extraordinary taxes the Crown requested, Castile’s 2.4%, and the English Parliament none. Between 1601 and 1800 the English Parliament refused 7.7% and the Iberian assemblies none, because they had stopped meeting (see the figure).
  • When a request was cut rather than refused, the median Portuguese cut was about half of what was asked. Castile cut on 13 occasions, Portugal on 5 and England on 4. Most Iberian cuts came in the fifteenth century and most English ones in the seventeenth, the direction of travel in both cases.
  • The dates of the last Iberian reductions, Castile’s in the 1620s and Portugal’s in 1674, are not random. After them, never again.

When the assemblies met

  • The English constitutional revolution is a mid-seventeenth-century event. The Iberian collapse is not simultaneous with it and is not one event: Castile goes first, between the 1620s and the 1660s, and Portugal follows three-quarters of a century later. Two separate causes, not a shared Iberian temperament; Chapter 6 supplies both.
  • The Restoration of 1640, when Portugal broke from the Spanish Habsburgs, revived the Cortes rather than weakening them. They were summoned within days of 1 December, sat in fourteen separate years between 1641 and 1698, deposed Afonso VI in 1667–68, forced a reduction of the décima tax in 1674, and aspired to audit royal spending.
  • Fiscal debasements of the coinage, absent in Portugal since the first half of the fifteenth century, resumed only after 1640, and then with the explicit consent of the Cortes, because the country was in a life-or-death war with Spain.
  • Then, abruptly and permanently, they stopped being called. After 1697–98 the Cortes did not meet again for 122 years, until the liberal revolution of 1820 (the traditional three-estate Cortes only in 1828).

The inversion, in four other markets

  • Coinage. Henry VIII’s Great Debasement of 1542–55 cut the silver in an English penny by 83.1% and raised prices by 123%. In Castile the Cortes of 1442 declared that no change in the coinage could be made without their assent. Portuguese coinage stayed roughly stable in value from 1500 to 1800.
  • Forced loans and money levied without parliamentary consent were more frequent in England than in Castile or Portugal until the late sixteenth century, and the pattern flips thereafter.
  • Borrowing. In the early sixteenth century both Iberian crowns could borrow at long, even perpetual, maturities at low rates, which England could not do until the second half of the seventeenth century. England defaulted in 1340 and 1472, while Spain and Portugal never defaulted between 1300 and 1550; Portugal’s only early modern default came in 1560.
  • Iberian crowns first paid lower rates than private borrowers and by the eighteenth century higher ones, because, as the Count of Castanheira observed in 1541, a merchant can have a debtor jailed but cannot do the same to a king. England went the other way.
  • Orphans’ property. England’s Court of Wards was described by its own historians as “fiscal feudalism”, exploiting the estates of the minors it was supposed to protect. Its Portuguese counterpart, the Juízo dos Órfãos, behaved considerably more even-handedly.

History written by the winners

  • That is Palma’s phrase for the Anglo-Dutch literature that projects eighteenth-century Iberian absolutism back to Magna Carta. It had a domestic accomplice: the nineteenth-century Portuguese liberals, the historian Alexandre Herculano above all, who described an Old Regime despotism reaching back to the sixteenth century because they needed a tyranny to have overthrown.
  • Both versions are still taught. Neither, on Palma’s count, survives the sources. What the sources do show is a real change around 1700, and the question Part II has to answer is what caused it.

The shape of the series

  • Average income per person roughly doubles between 1527 and the middle of the eighteenth century. The idea that Portugal has been in decline ever since the Discoveries, urged by the historian João Lúcio de Azevedo and by Quental alike, is simply wrong.
  • The path was not smooth. The underlying paper finds five decades of growth from the late 1520s to the late 1570s, decline until the 1630s, then steady growth to the mid-eighteenth century (with a twenty-year pause around 1690–1710), accelerating after 1710 and peaking about 1750.
  • The catastrophe comes from the 1770s and wipes out three centuries of gains. By 1850 income per head was back where it had been in the early 1530s, and the 1750 level was not regained until the twentieth century.
  • Part of the arithmetic is population: after 1750 people kept multiplying (about 0.34% a year) while output did not, so income per head fell even before output did.
  • The chronology alone disposes of two favourite culprits. The 1755 Lisbon earthquake is two decades too early to be the cause of the fall, and the French invasions of 1807 three decades too late.

Against the neighbours

  • In 1750 Portugal compares well with Sweden, Germany, France and Spain, countries it now takes for granted are richer. Older scholarship half-saw this: the economic historians Robert Thomas and Deirdre McCloskey called Portugal and Spain the “giants” of the sixteenth century, and Paul Bairoch placed Portugal among Europe’s five richest countries as late as 1800 (on the new series that is half a century too late), with Lisbon among its four largest cities.
  • What distinguishes Portugal is not being poor in 1750 but never once managing rapid sustained growth in the two centuries that followed, while nearly every other Western European country did.
  • England is flat until the mid-seventeenth century and then never stops, which is exactly when, on Chapter 2’s evidence, its parliament changed.
  • Spain’s decline starts in the sixteenth century and Portugal’s only in the eighteenth. That century of difference is why Palma insists these are two episodes of the same disease with different start dates: silver from Potosí for one, gold from Minas Gerais for the other.
  • Around 1850 Portuguese income per head was below that of other peripheral countries such as Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Finland and Spain.

The twentieth century, in proportion

  • Palma is careful to put the twentieth century in proportion in both directions. Portugal in 1950, at $2,086 a head, was poorer than Mozambique in 2010 and clearly poorer than Cape Verde in 2008.
  • Between 1950 and 2000 income per person multiplied by seven. Anyone who hears that for the first time and is not astonished, he writes, has not understood what it means.
  • That half-century was an economic miracle, and in his judgement the most important event in Portuguese history measured by its effect on human welfare. It began under the dictatorship; Chapter 9 asks why.
  • Then, in the twenty-first century, the economy went mediocre again, the subject of Chapter 10.

Part II

The argument

Seven chapters that test each candidate cause against the timeline, find the real one, and follow its consequences through every regime to the present: empire, religion, the golden curse, the constitutional monarchy, the First Republic, the Estado Novo and the contemporary period.

Chapter 4

Expansion and Empire

A sea empire, by necessity

  • Palma describes the conversation he has with compatriots abroad about the vast lands Portugal supposedly conquered, and answers it with one line: only one problem, none of that happened.
  • His claim is not that the empire was small in extent but that it could not have enriched Portugal, and that European empires are better understood as a consequence of an earlier institutional and technological advantage than as its cause.
  • Portugal never had a land empire in Asia and could not have had one. Asia around 1600 held over 350 million people against Europe’s 75 million; conquest was arithmetically out of the question.
  • What it had was a thalassocracy, a sea empire resting on the two military technologies where Europe was genuinely ahead: warships carrying effective artillery, and fortresses built to European designs.
  • The feitorias were trading posts with warehouses, chapels and offices, usually negotiated with local rulers. Presence depended on local tolerance. At Macau the Chinese could threaten to close the Porta do Cerco, the land gate, and starve the city, and did so more than once. In Japan the Portuguese stayed until the shogun decided otherwise, and then left.
  • By 1900, after four centuries, only about twenty thousand Europeans lived in the whole empire.

What was lost, and to whom

  • The seventeenth-century losses were mostly not Dutch victories at all but shifts in Asian politics: Syriam in Burma in 1612–13, Hormuz in 1622 (to Persia, with English help), Hooghly in Bengal in 1632, expulsion from Japan in 1638–39, the ports of Canara in 1654.
  • Where the Dutch did win, in the Moluccas and at São Jorge da Mina on the Gold Coast, Palma credits better use of force and diplomacy rather than any superior commercial organisation.
  • And in Brazil the Dutch West India Company, more heavily capitalised than the famous East India Company (VOC), was decisively beaten.

Why Europe and not China

  • The comparison he draws is scale. Zheng He’s treasure ships of 1405–33 were up to 70 metres long, with rudder posts over ten metres, sailing in fleets of hundreds of vessels carrying many thousands of men. Vasco da Gama’s São Gabriel of 1497–99 had a hull of about 20 metres; the caravel Bérrio was smaller still.
  • By the time the Portuguese reached China a century later, the Chinese no longer knew how to build the larger ship. The voyages had been cancelled as too expensive, a technological regression that was possible, Palma notes, precisely because China was a single state.
  • The point is not Portuguese prowess. European fragmentation forced continuous competitive investment, where Chinese unity allowed a single decision to stop.
  • The empire’s one genuinely large contribution to world development, in his account, is intellectual: the empirical habit that came out of navigation, summed up in Duarte Pacheco Pereira’s maxim that experience is the mother of all things. It fed the Scientific Revolution. It did not make Portugal rich.

Revenue to the Crown is not wealth to the country

  • This is the distinction Palma says generations of historians collapsed. The empire mattered to the royal budget in some decades, and the archives allow it to be measured, starting with the oldest surviving estado da fazenda, a statement of the royal finances, from 1526.
  • But the state was a very small part of the economy, so both things can be true at once: imperial receipts significant for the treasury, imperial trade marginal for the country. The economic presence of empire in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries can be described, he writes, without much exaggeration, as a few ships a year arriving at the Terreiro do Paço, Lisbon’s waterfront square.
  • Charles Tilly argued that dependence on imperial revenue let the Portuguese Crown do without representative institutions. That fails twice over: the empire was never so large a share of revenue, and the representative institutions demonstrably existed (Chapter 2).
  • Until the early nineteenth century Portugal was, for its population, a fiscally strong and centralised state. It became fiscally weak only afterwards, collecting about 3.5% of GDP in the 1850s.

Slavery, and the Dutch counterfactual

  • Palma’s argument here runs the same way and is deliberately unsentimental. Since the empire did not make Portugal rich, it is also necessarily false that Portuguese wealth was produced by slavery: there was no such wealth to explain.
  • His work with Guilherme Lambais finds unskilled living standards in Bahia comparable to Europe’s in the late sixteenth century and falling through the seventeenth and eighteenth as slave arrivals rose.
  • With Hélder Carvalhal he finds unskilled living standards in Luanda and Benguela falling across the nineteenth and much of the twentieth century, because coerced labour distorted the labour market.
  • Slavery enriched some colonial elites and held back the development of everywhere it touched. Between the mid-nineteenth and mid-twentieth centuries, average incomes in Portugal were not much above those in Brazil or its own colonies.
  • He is equally unimpressed by the idea that Dutch colonisation would have served Brazil better. Suriname is today less developed than Brazil, and Dutch rule in Java stayed extractive well into the nineteenth century: at their peak in the 1850s the batig slot transfers from Java were nearly 4% of Dutch GDP and over half of Dutch government revenue, a far heavier extraction than anything Portugal managed.
  • What he does concede is that Portugal passed on to Brazil a worse institutional design than England left in North America, and he explains why in one sentence: by the eighteenth century, when the transfer happened, Portugal’s own institutions had been wrecked by gold.

Chapter 5

Culture and Religion

The Protestant ethic, tested

  • Max Weber’s The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1905) is the canonical cultural explanation, and Quental’s was a Portuguese version of it. Palma assembles the comparative case against it quickly.
  • Detailed local data for late-nineteenth-century Prussia, a genuinely mixed population, show no differences between Catholics and Protestants in savings, incomes or literacy once the discriminated Polish minority is taken out. The only real gap is ethnic, and Weber was himself a German nationalist hostile to Polish interests.
  • England’s sustained growth begins more than a century after Henry VIII’s death, and only after its seventeenth-century institutional changes (Chapter 2), not after the Reformation.
  • The second country to industrialise was Belgium, Catholic and long ruled by the Spanish Habsburgs. Catholic France raised incomes by a different route, becoming the first country in the world to go through a fertility transition, decades before 1789. Catholic Ireland is now among Europe’s richest countries.
  • Books were printed continuously in Portugal from the end of the fifteenth century, whatever the international datasets that record Portugal as zero may imply.

The Inquisition, sized honestly

  • This is the passage most likely to be quoted against him, and the one he argues most carefully. He does not defend the institution; he asks what it can and cannot explain.
  • The Portuguese and Spanish Inquisitions were courts. They required evidence, acquitted many, and executed a small share of the accused; the executions were carried out by the civil authorities. The Portuguese tribunal sentenced over 45,000 cases between about 1536 and the 1760s and executed under 2,000 people in some 230 years.
  • Their preoccupations were heresy, “purity of blood” and morals (sodomy, bigamy, belief) rather than science. They regulated rather than banned the disciplines we have since abandoned: judicial astrology was forbidden and natural astrology allowed, so one could forecast for navigation, medicine and farming but not predict the death of kings or the success of a marriage.
  • Where Catholic authorities censored books, Protestant ones burned them.
  • Intolerance was general across early modern Europe. England expelled its Jews in 1290 and readmitted them under Cromwell for a payment; France expelled them repeatedly between 1182 and 1394 and confiscated their property. It therefore cannot tell the countries that grew rich from those that did not.
  • The Dutch Republic, usually held up as the tolerant exception, prosecuted homosexuality more ferociously than almost anywhere in Europe. After the courts decided in the early 1730s that “this vice must be exterminated”, dozens were executed, young men among them, hundreds fled, and around two hundred were killed before the Napoleonic Code decriminalised same-sex relations in 1811. In England, Alan Turing was chemically castrated in 1952.
  • Many Portuguese Jews chose the Levant over the Netherlands, and the Levant did not become a dynamic economy either.

What actually shut Portuguese science down

  • The chapter’s hinge is one example. The University of Évora, run by the Jesuits, taught science as well as theology; its azulejo tile panels from the 1740s illustrating the vacuum and magnetism can still be visited.
  • It was closed in 1759 with the expulsion of the Society of Jesus and did not reopen for more than two hundred years. Its lecture rooms became stables.
  • The man who ordered it was not a churchman but the Marquis of Pombal, and the Inquisitor-General who condemned the Jesuit Gabriel Malagrida to death in 1761 was Pombal’s own brother, Paulo António de Carvalho e Mendonça, installed after Pombal jailed his predecessor. What looks like religious persecution was the political power using the Inquisition’s resources and prerogatives for its own ends.
  • The second example is Bento de Moura Portugal (1702–66): an early Newtonian, Fellow of the Royal Society, inventor of an improved steam engine. He was denounced anonymously in January 1759, imprisoned in the Junqueira fort in July 1760 on charges of criticising the government and sympathising with Malagrida and the Távora family, and died in his cell in 1766. The historian of science Rómulo de Carvalho counted him among Pombal’s victims.
  • These catastrophes, Palma writes, had no cultural or religious origin whatever. They had a political one.

Culture reacts to politics

  • His standing rebuttal to cultural determinism is that the supposedly fixed traits keep changing. The writer J. Rentes de Carvalho wrote in the 1970s that the Portuguese were constitutionally timid and submissive and that the gap between rich and poor countries would only widen.
  • The half-century since has contained the largest and fastest exit from poverty in history, in China from the late 1970s and India from the 1990s, and Portugal itself developed with tremendous force in the twentieth century while still a deeply Catholic country.
  • So culture cannot be a fundamental cause in this book. It is real, and it matters, but it is downstream of political decisions. Chapters 8 and 9 will put that claim to a harder test.

The road not taken, 1668–1690

  • The force of the chapter depends on Portugal having had somewhere else to go. In the last three decades of the seventeenth century, after the Treaty of Lisbon ended the war with Spain in 1668, Portugal was growing, industrialising and politically improving at the same time.
  • Income per head rose from about $900 in 1640–60 to over $1,200 by the early 1690s. The share of the workforce in agriculture fell from 64% to 59% between 1650 and 1700, while the rural non-agricultural share rose from 24% to 29%.
  • By 1680 Portuguese industry could cover domestic and colonial demand for several goods and was exporting textiles to Castile.
  • This was policy, not luck. A sequence of laws between 1672 and 1698, the Leis Pragmáticas, pursued import substitution and closer integration with the colonies, drawing on the diplomat Duarte Ribeiro de Macedo and on French Colbertism.
  • The driving figure was D. Luís de Meneses, third Count of Ericeira, Vedor da Fazenda, roughly a modern minister of economy and finance. Foreign technicians were brought in and state–private partnerships set up; spinning stayed in rural households while the factories did the carding, dyeing and finishing.
  • Palma’s counterfactual is deliberately modest: not that Portugal would have had an industrial revolution, but that these factories and several more would have been built, that the gains from clustering would have survived the eventual loss of Brazil, and that the country would have entered the nineteenth century with an industrial structure instead of without one.
  • The sites (Covilhã, the Minho, Porto) are the same regions that did finally industrialise, two centuries later.

1694: gold

  • Europeans had searched for gold in the Brazilian interior since 1531, when an expedition left São Vicente and never came back. Around 1694 large alluvial deposits were found in what is now Minas Gerais by adventurers hunting for native slaves and silver.
  • The prospectors tried to keep it quiet; by 1697 the news was out. By 1705 perhaps fifty thousand prospectors and their slaves lived in the gold towns, fed by emigration from Portugal of three to ten thousand people a year.
  • Output was about 40,000 kg in each of the first two decades of the eighteenth century, 100,000 kg in the 1720s and over 140,000 kg in the 1730s, peaking at about 14 tonnes a year around 1750. By 1730, on the published paper’s figures, Brazil supplied over 70% of the world’s gold output.
  • More than 80% came back to Portugal, mostly private rather than the Crown’s: the royal fifth (quinto) was a tax on production, but most gold travelled as remittances of the private owners of the mines. It arrived already minted. Three annual fleets carried an average of about 3 billion réis each, shared among some 2,300 private individuals.
  • People saw the danger at once. The colonial administrator D. João de Lencastre warned in 1701 that Brazilian gold would end up benefiting England and Holland rather than Portugal, exactly as American silver had done for Spain. An Italian Jesuit in Brazil, Antonil, said much the same a decade later. Hume noticed the pattern in 1742 and Adam Smith in 1776. Nobody acted on it.

The two channels

  • A windfall impoverishes a country by two routes, and each makes the other worse (see the figure). The economic route is what economists call Dutch disease. The political route is the more consequential: a windfall with no strings attached removes the one constraint that had reliably forced the Crown to negotiate.
  • Palma’s image for the second: if a drunk driver hits a pedestrian, nobody says the problem was that he did not brake. The modern control case he keeps returning to is Venezuela, whose politics and economy are hard to imagine being worse without oil.
  • The published paper stresses that the political curse is conditional: what institutions a country has when the money arrives decides how much harm it does. Portugal’s Cortes had power only because the Crown needed their taxes, which is exactly what the gold removed.

Dutch disease, in the prices

  • The price evidence is direct. Over the eighteenth century the ratio of non-tradable to tradable prices rose about 30% in every region studied, tracking the gold cycle. Meat and eggs, which could not be traded far before refrigeration, rose relative to wheat, olive oil and textiles.
  • In the southern Alentejo, rising meat prices drove large-scale conversion of low-yielding cereal land to pasture from about 1710, and the same happened in the Algarve; by 1722 Algarve livestock could supply other provinces as well as its own. This was a rational response to relative prices.
  • It left the country importing 5.5–7% of its grain against England’s 3%. Imported cereals supplied 55% of Lisbon’s consumption in 1729 and 72% by 1778.
  • The trade deficit rose from 2 billion réis in 1720 (2.4% of GDP) to over 4 billion by 1750 (3.9%) and nearly 6 billion in 1756 (6.1%), just after the earthquake, and tracked the gold arrivals throughout.
  • Imports from England rose from £355,000 a year in 1699–1702 to £1.3 million in 1756–60, while Portuguese exports to England fell from a peak of £387,000 in 1720 to £257,000. Woollens were never less than 70% of English goods sent to Portugal before 1760, much of them re-exported to Brazil.
  • Britain sent 18% of its manufactured exports to Portugal in 1741–45, and in 1736–40 Portugal bought about half of the English textile industry’s exports. The gold left Lisbon for Plymouth on regular packet boats, whose diplomatic immunity let them ignore bans on exporting bullion.
  • A visitor in 1760 wrote that the Portuguese were very rich in gold and jewels but that none of their wealth came from anything produced in Portugal, and was astonished that even their shoes came from England or France.

Methuen, demoted

  • Since the German economist Friedrich List in the nineteenth century, the Methuen Treaty of December 1703 (English woollens admitted to Portugal, and Portuguese wine taxed in England a third less than French) has carried the blame for Portuguese de-industrialisation.
  • Palma inverts the causation. The treaty was itself a consequence of the gold that was already arriving and was known to be coming for a long time. It was signed over the objection of D. Luís da Cunha, Portugal’s ambassador in London and a partisan of Ericeira’s manufacturing policy, who was removed from the negotiation and spent decades arguing that it was bankrupting factories that were “the remedy of innumerable peoples”.
  • Methuen was a mechanism. It was not the cause.

Growth that was not development

  • The share of people working outside agriculture rose to almost half by about 1760, a pre-twentieth-century record, and it is a trap. The first half of the rise looks like development and is not: it is urban services, a consumption boom paid for in gold.
  • Lisbon became one of the four largest cities in Europe, alongside Naples, Paris and London, as a city of consumption dependent on imports. Urbanisation rose from about 12% in 1650–1700 to 17.3% in 1750, then fell to 16.2% by 1800.
  • In 1769 there were fifteen factories in the whole country, and only four of them made textiles, the growth sector of the British Industrial Revolution.
  • By 1841 the non-agricultural share was back to 32%, the level of 1500, and it did not move again until the 1950s. Portugal missed the Industrial Revolution outright.

Where the money went

  • Private owners spent it on imported consumer goods, on churches and convents, and on land.
  • The Crown was no better. João V (r. 1706–50) built the vast palace-convent at Mafra, spent heavily on a numerous clergy, and sent a spectacular embassy to Pope Clement XI whose three gilded triumphal coaches can still be seen at the Coach Museum in Belém. There was little interest in promoting domestic manufacturing.
  • Credit markets developed miserably: a legal ceiling of 5% on perpetual loans from 1698, further distortion by Pombal in 1757, no land registry (so the same land could be mortgaged to exhaustion), and in the 1770s a ban on the Misericórdia of Lisbon, in practice a bank, lending to private borrowers, so that its money went to the state instead.
  • The Misericórdia of Porto and others kept lending only to private borrowers, which tells you what lending to the Crown was worth.

Pombal, a symptom before a cause

  • By the time the 1755 earthquake let Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo (1699–1782, Count of Oeiras in 1759, Marquis of Pombal in 1770) become the central figure of the state, gold had been distorting the economy for half a century and the Cortes had not met for more than fifty years.
  • Royal power was already absolute before José I came to the throne in 1750. Pombal could do what he did because nothing was left to stop him, and his power ended the moment the king died, in 1777.
  • The earthquake itself, Palma insists, should not be exaggerated: the Lisbon inhabitants who died or left were about 12% of the city, its population recovered within 25 years, and GDP barely moved. Its real consequence was political: it made Pombal.
  • What he did with the power Palma judges without any softening. The Távora family, who opposed him, were falsely incriminated in an attempt on the king’s life, tortured and executed, their estates seized. In 1757 he put down the Innkeepers’ Revolt in Porto against one of his monopolies, with hundreds arrested and dozens executed, and made the city pay for the troops.
  • He held shares in the Grão-Pará company in his second wife’s name while his brother governed the region it monopolised; he set up the Real Mesa Censória, the first salaried royal censors, and made households declare their books.
  • Merchants took to writing in vinegar and lemon juice. People close to him lived in fear, and long after his fall one of his own daughters was still advising her mother to burn her letters.
  • Nothing changed after 1777. The abuses continued under the police intendant Pina Manique, and a foreign observer described the government of Maria I as perhaps the most despotic in Europe, where the law was an empty word unless put into force by special order of the sovereign.

The single worst decision: 1759

  • In the mid-eighteenth century Portugal had two universities and a network of pre-university schools across the whole country, and its human capital was only slightly behind the most advanced parts of Europe. Its numeracy was close to the best in Europe in the first half of the century.
  • About twenty thousand pupils were in pre-university education, essentially all taught by the Society of Jesus, and taught free, as was standard in Jesuit colleges. The Colégio de Santo Antão in Lisbon had 2,500–3,000 pupils; even small colleges had a few hundred.
  • Santo Antão’s Aula da Esfera ran without interruption from 1590 to 1759, taught in Portuguese, open to laymen and free, covering logarithms, the telescope and the Mercator projection, with distinguished foreign teachers.
  • A decree of 28 June 1759 abolished the Jesuit schools in the king’s name, so that their memory should be erased “as if they had never existed in my Kingdoms”. The plans for what should replace them were vague and never carried out.
  • Of about 1,500 Jesuits in Portugal, more than 1,100 were deported to the Papal States, 222 imprisoned, and 80 died in prison.
  • Palma is careful about the claim. The Jesuits could not by themselves have delivered mass education; there were not enough of them. What matters is that they were the base from which a later expansion, by the state if necessary, could have been built, because it takes human capital to make human capital, and in a country of illiterates the missing input is teachers.
  • The Director-General of Studies appointed to replace them, D. Tomás de Almeida, could not recruit staff. He reported in 1763 that people could not afford the masters’ salaries and were not sending their children, “so that many talents are lost that would be useful to the Fatherland”.
  • Two years later he found teachers in several of the few remaining schools still using Jesuit grammars; the teachers were suspended and the books burned in public. By 1773 fewer than a third of the existing primary schools had a teacher.
  • A law of 1772 laying the foundations of the new school system said in its preamble that most of the population did not need to be literate, being reserved for “the rustic and humble service of the State”.
  • Portugal became a country without schools. It would not have twenty thousand pupils in pre-university education again until the 1930s, with a population nearly three times larger. By 1800 the share of adults who could sign their name was well behind the rest of Western Europe.

The verdict

  • “It does not seem excessive,” Palma writes, “to say that Pombal was the worst politician ever to govern Portugal.”
  • He is not generally in favour of pulling down statues, he adds, but finds it curious that Pombal’s stands on the country’s best-known roundabout, a monument to the triumph of propaganda over truth two and a half centuries on, put there by a later regime (the First Republic) whose own gap between declared educational intentions and delivered results would be just as wide.
  • The country entered the nineteenth century with its industrial base gone, an archaic political system, and not even enough schooling to educate the minimum elite from which a later expansion could grow, precisely as most of Western Europe was preparing for its industrial revolutions. That, in one sentence, is the book’s thesis about where Portuguese backwardness comes from.
  • The published paper behind the chapter puts a number on it. A synthetic-control counterfactual, a weighted blend of other European economies matched to Portugal before 1694, suggests that by 1800 Portuguese GDP per head was about 40% lower than it would have been without Brazilian gold.

Three wrong names

  • Palma’s problem with the period is first about words. “Constitutional Monarchy” implies that constitutional rules had not operated before, which Chapter 2 showed to be false.
  • “Absolute Monarchy” for the other side implies a limitless power that the Miguelists, who called themselves realistas, royalists, would not have recognised.
  • And “Liberal Monarchy” is simply wrong: in economic policy the regime was never liberal at all. Jaime Reis demolished that label in a series of papers in the 1980s.
  • Far from free trade, Portugal kept some of the highest tariffs in Europe from the schedule of 1837 onward, straight through the first age of globalisation. Part of the reason was fiscal (the state depended on customs revenue and could not touch the schedule), and part was that public talk of “liberalism” blamed the Anglo-Portuguese trade treaties for the country’s backwardness, so free trade had no advocates.
  • The liberal label survives only in a legal sense: nominal equality before the law, nominal abolition of privilege. In practice, as an English visitor observed, the rich oppressor could count on legal support while the poor victim of his tyranny drank a bitter cup and fell silent.

Thirty-one years of disorder, 1820–1851

  • Five successful coups, two civil wars and four sovereign bankruptcies. Constant electoral fraud (stamped lists, stolen ballot boxes, falsified minutes), and a governing practice called exclusivismo: whoever won purged the administration of the losers. In the absence of a coup, elections were decided by whoever organised them.
  • The final liberal victory in 1834 came through British military intervention, and Palma is blunt about why: the British had lent money to the liberals, and if the Miguelists won the loans would have been repudiated.
  • It is not unreasonable, he writes, to see the triumph of the Liberal Regime as imposed by foreign powers concerned that creditors of their own nationality should be repaid.

The dissolution of the monasteries, and what it cost

  • In 1832 Mouzinho da Silveira abolished ecclesiastical tithes. In 1834 Joaquim António de Aguiar, a freemason remembered ever after as Mata-Frades, the friar-killer, signed the law of 30 May extinguishing all the convents, monasteries, colleges and houses of the male religious orders, roughly 450 of them, and putting their property up for auction.
  • The female orders were forbidden to take novices and so condemned by attrition. Many of today’s state hospitals and public buildings are that expropriation.
  • The auctions were known as devorismo, the devouring, and with reason. Treasury bonds were accepted at face value however deeply they were discounted in the market, and the most valuable properties were auctioned in Lisbon rather than in the district towns: a fairly precise description of a mechanism for transferring assets to people with information and cash.
  • What Palma stresses is the collateral damage. Whatever else the monasteries were, they were the last literate institution present across the whole territory, and so the only cheap base left for a national school system.
  • Primary schooling was made compulsory by law in 1835, again in 1844, and remained a dead letter for the rest of the century. Nothing had been learned from Pombal.
  • Under the Constitutional Monarchy, schooling was paid for out of municipal budgets, which tells you where it sat among the state’s priorities.

The price of being an old nation

  • Jaime Reis’s explanation, in a paper Palma calls highly innovative for its time, is that Portugal paid a price for being an old nation-state.
  • Elsewhere in Europe (Italy, Germany, and to a large degree Spain and France) mass schooling was the instrument for building a national identity out of regional and linguistic difference.
  • Portugal had had one language and a settled identity for centuries, so nobody needed the school to make Portuguese, and nobody built it.

Fontismo: capital without people

  • The Regeneração of 1851 finally delivered stability, and Fontes Pereira de Melo an ambitious programme of railways, macadamised roads, bridges and telegraph lines, financed by deficits and largely by British credit.
  • The returns fell well short of expectations, and Palma’s diagnosis is that they were bound to: there was no matching investment in people to go with the physical capital.
  • In the mid-nineteenth century all of Portugal’s “factories” together were worth less than two ships loaded with port wine.
  • The Industrial Enquiry of 1881 has the directors of the Tomar cotton mill saying it themselves: one of their greatest difficulties was the lack of qualification of the staff; with little general education and almost no technical instruction, it was hard to find not only good workers but competent foremen. Literacy in the textile sector ran below 20%, often at 10%.
  • The latifundia were not the problem. Another of Reis’s demolitions, which Palma repeats: the great Alentejo estates were not inefficient. Steam threshing was adopted there when it paid, barely before 1900 and dominant by the early 1930s, which is what profit-maximising landowners should have done given costs and the politics of cereal protection. No appeal to backward mentalities is needed.

The end: financial, then humiliating

  • Remittances from emigrants in Brazil had become a material share of national income. Brazil’s republican revolution of November 1889 collapsed its exchange rate and cut the value of those remittances by more than 80%.
  • That was enough. Portugal left the gold standard and defaulted in 1891, and in 1898 Britain and Germany went as far as agreeing how they would divide the Portuguese colonies if the debts went unpaid. The novelist Eça de Queirós wrote to a friend: Portugal acabou, Portugal is finished.
  • The year before the default came the British Ultimatum of 1890 over the land between Angola and Mozambique, which Portugal could not conceivably have resisted and which became the greatest propaganda gift the republicans ever received.
  • A Portuguesa was written in 1890 as one of several patriotic songs against the Ultimatum. The republicans took it, and it is now the national anthem.

The verdict

  • Politically, Palma marks the regime moderately positive, and the reasoning is the mirror image of his verdict on the Estado Novo.
  • Parliaments met continuously. After the 1878 extension the franchise reached 72% of men over 21, one of the highest proportions in Europe, and unlike under the First Republic that followed, illiterate men could vote. Censorship was low by any comparison and would not be as low again until the late twentieth century.
  • Economically it was a century lost: a rural, illiterate, miserable country in which failed harvests brought unemployment, hunger, begging and banditry, diverging further from Western Europe every decade, and entering the twentieth century, in the book’s words, as the poorest state in Europe except Albania, with illiteracy at 75% of those over ten, among the highest in Europe.

What kind of regime

  • The Portuguese Republican Party (PRP) took power by coup in October 1910 and dominated everything that followed: fiercely anticlerical, Jacobin, heavily masonic. About half of all ministers and members of parliament were freemasons.
  • Palma’s characterisation is unsparing: a sectarian and radical regime, permanently at war over culture, whose partisans thought themselves the owners of truth and did little beyond acting in their own interest.
  • It was also less democratic than the monarchy it replaced. Elections were multi-party but neither free nor fair, and the PRP machine (later the Democratic Party) distorted the results. The republican press treated every critic of the government as a traitor.
  • An irregular political police, the Formiga Branca (White Ant), answered to PRP leaders and ran intimidation and violence, including raids on newspapers. The heavily armed Guarda Nacional Republicana (GNR) was created as the regime’s praetorian guard.
  • In October 1921 the camioneta-fantasma, the ghost lorry, crewed by GNR men and sailors, seized and killed several political figures, among them the outgoing prime minister António Granjo and two heroes of the 1910 revolution itself, Machado Santos and José Carlos da Maia.
  • Alfredo da Silva, founder of the CUF industrial group, was beaten and shot and fled the country; he called Portugal “a great madhouse” in 1925 and did not return until 1927. This is not, Palma observes drily, an environment for industrial development.

The franchise, narrowed on purpose

  • In opposition from 1891 the republicans had promised the vote to all men. In power they broke the promise.
  • In 1911 the vote went to men over 21 who could read and write or had been heads of household for a year, about 840,000 people. Universal male suffrage would have meant about twice that.
  • In 1913 the three main parties took the vote from illiterate heads of household. Afonso Costa, the regime’s dominant figure, told parliament that people who never left their parish and had no clear ideas about anything or anyone should not be allowed to vote. The reason given in the debates was frank: the rural population was Catholic, conservative and monarchist, and would vote against secularisation.
  • Registered voters came to about a quarter of adult men, and those who actually voted to around 10%, a smaller share than in the last decades of the monarchy.
  • Women were excluded too. Carolina Beatriz Ângelo, a doctor, voted in the 1911 Constituent Assembly election by exploiting an ambiguity in the law; the law was amended in 1913 to exclude women explicitly. Women first voted under the dictatorship, with the first women deputies in the National Assembly in 1935. In France, women voted for the first time in 1945.

The republican school

  • The declared aim was a new man: republican, nationalist, secular, physically and mentally vigorous, and ready to defend the regime. Education was blamed on the fallen monarchy and the “obscurantist” Church, and was proclaimed the regime’s first priority.
  • A Ministry of Instruction was created; compulsory schooling was set at three years and extended to five in 1919; teacher training and pay were improved; universities were founded in Lisbon and Porto; new schools were built. Most of the rest never left the paper, and the building programme fizzled out soon after it started.
  • The Law of Separation of Church and State, whose author Afonso Costa is reported to have said at a masonic gathering in March 1911 that it would wipe out Catholicism in Portugal within three generations, nationalised church property, put worship under state supervision and reorganised parish life around lay committees that excluded priests.
  • Two years later lay committees existed in 6% of parishes, and a fifth of the clergy were drawing the state pension the law offered.
  • The Jesuits were expelled for the third time in Portuguese history three days after the revolution, simply by declaring the decrees of 1759 and 1834 still in force. The next month Afonso Costa proposed moving Pombal’s remains to the national pantheon.
  • Crucifixes were banned from classrooms; religious teaching was removed from primary schools in October 1910; schools were made co-educational, boys and girls together in classrooms and playgrounds. The regime’s chief education ideologue, João de Barros, wrote that the Republic had liberated the Portuguese child by eliminating the Jesuit influence.

Why it failed

  • And that, Palma and Reis argue, is where the programme died. In a country that was rural, poor, Catholic and socially conservative, children were real labour: gathering pasture and pine cones, carrying water and manure, minding animals and younger siblings, and working alongside adults from about twelve.
  • Parents were not going to give that labour up in order to have their children indoctrinated in republican secularism. An official report of 1921 admitted enrolments were falling, especially of girls, and named parents’ aversion to co-education among the reasons, noting a drop in the very year it became general policy.
  • The state built schools and got fewer children per school each year (see the figure). Several republican intellectuals concluded that building more was pointless, since the children did not come.
  • Forty ministers of Instruction came and went between 1913 and 1926, one every four months, not counting acting ministers. The 1923 Estatuto da Educação Nacional, which the historian Jaime Cortesão called the most serious political document ever issued by a Portuguese government, never left the paper: its government fell that November.
  • Basílio Teles, a republican economist, proposed in desperation closing every school that was not a primary school so that illiteracy could be dealt with first. Leonardo Coimbra resigned as minister because his own party regarded religious feeling as an infirmity found only in the old and in children, and would not allow religious teaching even in private schools.
  • The result: literacy among those aged ten and over rose only from 31% in 1911 to 38% in 1926. Among twenty-year-old men examined by the army in 1924 and 1930, schooled under the Republic, there was no progress at all.

It is not possible to change a whole society suddenly, from the top down, by decree; and it is far easier to change it in keeping with the culture of most of its people than against it.The chapter’s lesson (paraphrased)

  • That sentence is the beam under Chapter 9, and it is why Palma treats the First Republic and the Estado Novo as a single natural experiment in education policy rather than as two moral categories.

The economy, and the war

  • On the economy the verdict is also negative but more forgiving. The regime kept the traditional protectionism.
  • Entry into the Great War in 1916 was a deliberate political choice, taken by an unusually broad coalition, to defend the African empire and win international recognition. It cost roughly 100,000 men mobilised and 25,000 casualties, with severe financial damage.
  • But it was a choice made a decade before the Estado Novo existed, which undercuts the idea that sacrificing the country for the overseas empire was that regime’s invention.
  • He also corrects a favourite Estado Novo myth in the other direction: by 1923–24 the last republican governments had already stabilised the public finances. Salazar restored order to the military dictatorship’s accounts, not to the Republic’s.

The fence

  • Palma builds the fence before the argument. The Estado Novo was a dictatorship. Political prisons, censorship, crimes of opinion and puppet elections are not merely condemnable but unacceptable, and no economic success justifies gagging liberty, because liberty is an end in itself. He has, he says, no political sympathy whatever for the regime.
  • Having said so, he asks what actually happened, and refuses to let the answer be decided by what would be convenient.
  • A necessary distinction runs the other way from earlier chapters. With Pombal and Afonso Costa, the danger was mistaking impeccable plans for delivered results. With Salazar the danger is the reverse: his public rhetoric praised poverty, rural life and austerity, and in 1935 he publicly doubted the value of teaching people to read (“to read what?”). That remark has misled generations of intellectuals.
  • It was very far from describing what the regime did, which included propaganda posters boasting of its victories over illiteracy and the growth of technical schooling. Salazar, as one of the regime’s critics put it, chose to teach the masses to read while controlling what they read.

Repression, measured rather than asserted

  • Comparatively, the regime was not especially bloody. It did not restore the death penalty, abolished in Portugal for political crimes in 1852, civil crimes in 1867 and military crimes in 1911.
  • There were extrajudicial killings: the opposition leader Humberto Delgado and his secretary Arajaryr Campos were murdered by the political police, the PIDE, near Badajoz in 1965. Conditions at the Tarrafal camp in Cape Verde amounted to a death sentence for some prisoners, though fewer than forty died there.
  • Deporting political prisoners to the colonies was not a Salazarist invention; the monarchy and the First Republic had both done it.
  • Palma explains the low level of violence not by the mild manners of the leaders but by the lack of need. Repression rose in 1943–49, when the regime felt threatened, and fell afterwards; censorship was looser overseas during the colonial war than at home. Most of the country was conservative, rural and illiterate, and politics passed it by.

Not fascist, and why the label matters

  • Real fascist regimes were proud of it. The Estado Novo never defined or behaved as one: no totalitarian party, no mass movement, no personality cult, no state religion despite Salazar’s Catholic origins.
  • Salazar suppressed the nearest Portuguese equivalent, Rolão Preto’s National Syndicalists (the blue shirts), in 1934, and the União Nacional was an “anti-party” that never sought a mass following.
  • The English communist Ralph Fox, visiting in 1936 on his way to die in Spain, wrote that on the theatrical side Salazar lacked the minimum qualifications for the dictators’ union: he never appeared in public or spoke on the radio, reviewed no troops, wore no uniform, killed no enemies with his own hands and had no photograph in shop windows.
  • Palma insists on accuracy not to launder the regime (it was antidemocratic, antiparliamentary, nationalist and antiliberal, and never hid it) but because, as Chapter 10 argues, the false label has done political work ever since.

Growth: the external explanation is not enough

  • Portuguese income per head held at about 37% of the Western European average from about 1910 until the late 1940s, then climbed to 57% by 1973, the fastest convergence in the country’s history. (The apparent gain during the Second World War is an artefact: other economies shrank, Portugal did not rise.)
  • The post-war boom is the obvious external explanation. Palma accepts it as necessary and rejects it as sufficient: Europe had grown fast before, through the nineteenth-century industrialisations, and Portugal had not joined in. Something inside the country had changed.
  • Convergence then stopped from 1975 to 1985. The chapter is about what had changed before that.

The answer: the country stopped fighting its school

  • The evidence, published by Palma and Reis in 2021, comes from over nine thousand individual records drawn from the army’s inspection books in the Army General Archive, a random sample of at least a quarter of each county’s records for 1924, 1930, 1940 and 1950. Every twenty-year-old man had to be inspected, so this is not a sample of volunteers: it covers the whole male cohort.
  • Each record gives height as well as literacy. In poor societies height tracks family circumstances in childhood, so the men can be sorted into fifths from shortest to tallest to see who was being reached.
  • Men examined in 1924 and 1930 were schooled under the Republic; those examined in 1940 and 1950 under the dictatorship. The line between them is a sharp break: little change under the Republic, then a jump and continued growth. Spain, where literacy had already been rising in 1924–30, shows no such break.
  • Controlling for height, life expectancy, local school density and region, a twenty-year-old of average height was about 50% more likely to be literate under the Estado Novo (literacy up 19.4 points from a base of about 40%).
  • Every social group gained, including the poorest: the shortest fifth under the Estado Novo did better than the tallest fifth under the Republic (see the figure). That disposes of the claim, still common, that the dictatorship never created conditions for educating the poor.
  • The comparison stops at 1950 on purpose, so that the two regimes are compared across the break rather than across half a century of unrelated change.

The mechanism is not money

  • Eleven days after the coup of 28 May 1926 the government ordered boys and girls separated in school buildings and playgrounds. The crucifixes went back on the walls, with the slogan “God, Fatherland, Family: the trilogy of national education”. Textbooks gave dozens of pages to Catholic religion.
  • Teaching was reduced to a single national programme and a single national textbook, built around reading, writing, arithmetic and socially conservative civic virtue. Schooling stayed free.
  • Compulsory schooling was cut from five years to four and then to three. Many readers take this as proof of hostility to education; Palma reads it as the opposite. The four- and five-year requirements had never been enforced and were fictions. The regime legislated for the country it had, and then expanded from there.
  • It expanded the network cheaply: lower pay and qualifications for teachers, and in remote places postos escolares, rudimentary classrooms run by barely qualified assistants. A 1936 decree set the aim as the minimum of culture essential to life and to fighting illiteracy energetically and efficiently.
  • It later raised the requirement again, to six years under the minister Galvão Teles in the 1960s, who was explicit that the point was for the reform to become effective rather than stay on paper, “as it effectively did”.
  • The decomposition in the Palma–Reis paper attributes over 70% of the rise in literacy to the change in regime and its cultural policies, about 20% to better material conditions (proxied by height and life expectancy), and only about 10% to more schools.
  • The identifying evidence is geographic: the Estado Novo’s effect on literacy was largest in the most Catholic counties (measured by self-declared Catholics and by mass-going), where families were most reassured by the new regime, and the link is strongest away from Lisbon.
  • There was a political motive too. For the dictatorship, educating the rural masses carried no risk of having to give them a meaningful vote, and the conservative countryside was its base; the Republic had feared exactly that.
  • School building did happen and mattered. The Plano dos Centenários of 1940 projected a 60% increase in primary schools and, as Pedro Maia Gomes and Matilde Pinto Machado have shown, extended the network especially in the poorest areas of the south and interior. Education took about 12% of public spending, against 7% under the Republic. But empty schools educate nobody.
  • The 1952 Plan for Popular Education aimed to finish the job. Its own preamble admitted that 20% of children aged 7–11 were still not enrolled despite free meals, clothes and books for the poor.
  • This is where Palma’s separation of levels from trends does most of its work. Portugal in 1974 genuinely had the worst education levels in Western Europe. It does not follow that the dictatorship caused them: under it, the country had been catching up on this measure faster than at any point in its history.

“One sardine between three”

  • The claim that people went hungry under the Estado Novo is usually made without comparison to what came before. Palma’s work with Alexandra López-Cermeño and Renato Pistola tests it against three archives: the Hospital de São Roque of the Lisbon Misericórdia, the Casa Pia orphanage of Lisbon, and the army’s conscription records.
  • Hunger and disease neither began with the regime nor ended with it. What is not in dispute is that both fell drastically during its time, and that the fall began long before democracy or the national health service.
  • Portuguese men had averaged about 1.65 m and had stopped growing, or shrunk, in the second half of the nineteenth century. From the 1920s average height rose about a centimetre a decade, faster in the 1960s. Stunting among twenty-year-olds, about 40% nationally in 1924 (nearly ten points less in Lisbon), had halved by 1968.
  • The improvements were the plainest kind: piped water, sewers, better housing, better food, and from 1965 the National Vaccination Programme, which within a decade more than halved deaths from communicable disease among children aged one to four, the largest such fall in Portuguese history.

The reforms behind the growth

  • Industrialisation accelerated in the 1950s, roughly a decade before joining EFTA, while about half the population still worked on the land.
  • The Electrification Law of 1944 and the Industrial Development Law of 1945 signalled that the pre-war system of industrial licensing (condicionamento industrial) was becoming obsolete; the Development Plans (Planos de Fomento) of the 1950s made electricity for industry, not irrigation, the main purpose of the new dams.
  • Fertiliser use tripled from ten to thirty kilos a hectare between the late 1930s and the late 1950s, releasing labour from the fields: the level Germany, Belgium and the Netherlands had reached around 1910.
  • Opening the economy, through the OEEC and the Marshall Plan and then EFTA in 1960 and GATT in 1962, was an internal political decision, and it changed what Portugal sold. Wine and lightly processed food had been two-thirds of merchandise exports in the first half of the century; by the last quarter, clothing and footwear were a quarter of the total, the share port wine had held a century earlier.
  • Real wages rose. At the CUF works at Barreiro, a detailed study found average real wages up 250% between 1925 and 1974, less than GDP per head, implying a shift towards capital; but the share of the very richest fell sharply from the mid-1950s to the mid-1980s, and the gap between skilled and unskilled pay narrowed in nearly every sector from the 1960s.
  • Emigration, often given as the cause of the growth, peaked at over 180,000 people in 1970, and Palma turns it into evidence against the claim: the country was absorbing most of the labour released from farming in the very decade it exported the most people, and other periods of heavy emigration (to Brazil in the nineteenth century, to northern Europe today) produced no convergence at all.

The one explanation that fails: Africa

  • The Marxist–Leninist claim is that Portugal converged by exploiting its African colonies. The numbers say otherwise. Exports to the colonies were about 3% of GDP in both 1950 and 1973.
  • Trade with the overseas provinces was 22% of Portugal’s total trade in 1950 and 14% in 1973, while total trade rose from 15% to 20% of GDP: the external sector grew through Europe, not Africa, as it did for Spain and Greece, neither of which had colonies worth the name.
  • In 1900–14, over 15% of exports went to the African colonies and re-exports of African products were 21% of external trade, more than France’s or Britain’s share. Portugal was not growing then.
  • Angola’s colonial budget ran a heavy deficit for almost all of 1819–1914, and from 1961, with the colonial wars, Africa was a growing charge on the budget: defence took roughly 30–46% of public spending between 1961 and 1973.
  • Palma’s own view of that spending is that it would have been far better spent on schools, and that a democracy would have faced public pressure to end the war sooner. The regime believed the empire was essential; that belief is not evidence that the empire caused anything.

Why the hatred, and what it is for

  • “When politicians fail, they feel the need to create smokescreens.” Each regime since 1759 has defined itself against the last, and each has thereby repeated its predecessor’s central mistake (see the figure).
  • Pombal could not see that he had no way of replacing the Jesuits on the ground. The nineteenth-century liberals could not see that their anticlerical crusade distracted them from the causes of divergence in a profoundly Catholic and conservative country. The republicans ignored the same lesson more radically still. And the Estado Novo concluded, from the chaos of the Republic, that Portugal could not be a democracy.
  • The present-day link is the one Palma cares about, because it does live political work. School history curricula still teach that the Estado Novo “prevented the economic and social modernisation of the country”, and a national schools broadcast during the pandemic told ninth-grade pupils that “without any doubt the fundamental step” towards European income levels was joining the EEC in 1986.
  • Blaming a regime that ended half a century ago, he writes, serves mainly to absolve what has been done since. A true democracy does not need to base its legitimacy on a historical lie, taking credit for a convergence that began decades earlier.

The fact to be explained

  • Convergence with richer Europe ran from about 1950, stalled for the decade after the 1974 revolution, resumed through the late 1980s and 1990s, and stopped around the turn of the century.
  • Since then Portugal has been overtaken by nearly every Central and Eastern European country that joined the EU, most of which started far poorer in the 1990s.
  • It has not been compensated by greater equality: the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Slovenia and Latvia grow faster and have lower income and wealth inequality. Portugal stands out across the OECD for how unlikely the children of the poor are to escape their parents’ situation.

Proximate and fundamental causes

  • The chapter’s structural move is the one that organises the whole book. Low productivity, low human capital, extractive elites, slow courts, an over-centralised state, high taxes for the quality of public goods: all real, and all endogenous. They are things to be explained, not explanations.
  • What has to be explained above all is the absence of demand for reform. Not that reforms would help, which is almost trivial, but why a country that can identify its problems does not want them fixed.
  • This is why Palma declines to name a guilty party. In his framing, a different set of leaders in the same context would have produced roughly the same present.

The transfers

  • Portugal received €133 billion from the EU between 1986 and early 2023, with €57 billion more to come by 2030, including the post-pandemic Recovery and Resilience Plan (PRR) of €22.2 billion, of which €5.9 billion are loans, to be spent by 2026. Portugal’s own contribution to the EU budget over the same period is about a third of the €133 billion.
  • Cohesion policy is 0.3% of the EU’s GDP as a whole and about 3% of Portugal’s, nearly ten times the average. For the Azores, the Commission’s own figure is about 3.5% a year.
  • For perspective: the funds are worth more each year than Autoeuropa, the Volkswagen plant that is one of the country’s largest exporters, at roughly 1.5% of GDP, which is also about the value of all emigrant remittances combined. On Palma’s arithmetic, enough to build fourteen large hospitals a year.
  • Until the eastward enlargement, Portugal was the member that benefited most systematically from structural funds relative to GDP, and with Greece it is the one that has depended on them longest.

The two channels, restated for the present

  • The economic channel is familiar. Money coming in pushes capital and labour towards non-tradables (property, public works, often unnecessary ones), at an indirect cost to exporters.
  • The empirical work he cites finds that firms receiving EU funds achieve higher sales but no gain in productivity, that even the sales effect is confined to non-tradable sectors, and that much of the money goes to very small firms with no transformative potential, for which the funds are simply a way of making a living.
  • The political channel is the one he thinks decisive. Money from outside, without counterparts, lets governments hand out rents that no productivity gain has earned. It breaks the link in time between bad policy and recession, hiding the problem behind a screen and protecting incumbents: voters reward the short-run effects, and the crisis arrives long after the decisions that caused it.
  • In a democracy the ballot box is how the public removes incompetent politicians, but that requires the public to feel the consequences in its pocket. The funds are an aspirin that masks the symptom and disables the mechanism.
  • He supports this with episodes rather than a model: the prosecutor who came first in the selection for the European Public Prosecutor’s Office, the body that would police fraud in the funds, and was passed over by the government; the President of the Court of Auditors not reappointed after opposing looser procurement rules for EU money; years without a Portuguese judge on the European Court of Auditors; and an Inspector-General of Finance who admitted changing an opinion, in the name of the “national interest”, so that the tap of European money would not close.

Almost everyone worries that the execution rate is too low. The optimal execution rate is zero: the less spent, the better.Chapter 10 (paraphrased)

Why Eastern Europe took the money and converged anyway

  • This is the obvious objection, and Palma meets it head on. Several members have received funds and converged, but none as much, or for as long, relative to the size of their economies.
  • More importantly, they drew different lessons from their own recent history. Direct experience of communist dictatorship killed any romance about the heavy hand of the state. Even in countries with worrying democratic trajectories such as Hungary and Poland, there are no illusions about communism, and centre parties are far more moderate about the role of the state than in Portugal.
  • He allows, in a footnote, that the funds may be doing net harm there too, just not enough yet to stop convergence.

The settlement of 1974–76

  • Portugal’s post-1974 cultural settlement did the opposite. A right-wing dictatorship was mischaracterised as low-intervention and free-market (an error: public employment grew enormously under it and the state intervened heavily in several sectors) and then labelled fascist, so that the entire right, democratic, moderate and liberal included, could be tarred with it.
  • Capitalism was identified with the old regime and socialism with democracy. The 1976 constitution’s preamble promised to open the way to a socialist society, and privatising the nationalised firms was constitutionally prohibited until 1989.
  • Francisco Sá Carneiro, founder of what became the main centre-right party, called himself centre-left and wanted his party in the Socialist International. Francisco Lucas Pires, who defended a market economy “for the sake of the workers”, failed because in that context he had to fail.
  • The revolution of 1974–75 was, economically, costly: land occupations, nationalisation of the banks and most large sectors (breweries included), flight of capital and of managers, and wage rises without productivity rises. The IMF had to intervene in 1977 and again in 1983.
  • Spain, whose transition after 1975 was negotiated rather than revolutionary (arguably because Spaniards had watched what happened next door), is the comparison. Luciano Amaral’s productivity series shows the two countries growing in parallel until 1974 and diverging permanently afterwards.
  • What corrected the worst of it was the reform period from the late 1980s, EEC accession, the PEDIP industrial programme (which the Commission later held up as a model for Central and Eastern European transition), and the slow restoration of a market economy. Then, from around 2000, that model exhausted itself against competition from Eastern Europe and China, the imbalances built up, and the resource curse took hold.

The axis that matters

  • Not left against right, which Palma regards as limited ideas, but against competition or for it. On that axis the country has changed remarkably little across the twentieth century, and may have got worse under democracy.
  • Public monopolies were often replaced by private ones in energy, telecoms and banking; regulators were captured or politically controlled; the competition authority is shared out by party quota; the specialist competition court is not efficient.
  • Even the self-described liberal right confuses defending markets with defending the profits of particular firms and professional orders. Privatising is not liberalising, and failing to see the difference is, he argues, one of the fundamental sources of today’s backwardness.

The state of the patient

  • Palma’s forecast, offered with the caveat that forecasting is not his trade: if present trends continue, and he sees nothing to suggest they will not, Portugal could be the poorest country in the European Union within a decade. (How that looks now is under What held up.)

Epilogue

The Patient and the Medicine

Most economic historians are optimists; it is hard not to be, looking backwards. Palma ends pessimistically, because he cannot honestly end any other way.

  • The problem, he writes, is not that nobody knows which policies would produce convergence. The solutions are known. The patient does not want the medicine, and keeps voting for the same parties and the same prescriptions.
  • Since no Portuguese government will ever refuse the money, and even if one did nothing would stop the next from asking again, change can only come from outside, from the EU itself. He suspects it will take a German recession to open the debate.
  • The book’s last lines turn, briefly, the other way: throughout its history Portugal has faced challenges, some of which seemed insurmountable. And we are still here.

Debates and pushback

Where a careful reader should argue back, and where the book sits in live disputes.

The 1690s counterfactual carries heavy weight Ch. 6

The claim that Portugal was on an industrialising path rests on about two decades, a handful of state-sponsored factories and one very good set of numbers from Covilhã. Palma concedes the scale was tiny by later standards. Whether the gains from clustering would have survived the loss of Brazil is asserted more than shown. The synthetic-control estimate in the published paper (40% lower income by 1800) helps, but it is built from other countries’ series that each carry at least 10% of error, and the underlying GDP paper itself finds a pause in Portuguese growth in 1690–1710.

Did the gold cause the decline, or only accompany it? Macedo 1982; Kedrosky and Palma 2025

The older Portuguese view (Jorge Borges de Macedo) held that the economy recovered once the gold slowed. Palma answers with data: income kept falling after the inflows shrank. The strongest part of his case is the timing of the political channel, Cortes silent from 1698; the weakest is proving that Portugal’s decline after 1770 was caused by the gold rather than by what came after (Pombal, war, the loss of Brazil), which he treats as consequences.

Culture is dismissed, then used Ch. 5, 9, 10

Chapter 5 says culture follows politics and cannot be a fundamental cause. Chapter 9’s key finding is that schooling worked because it aligned with the culture, and Chapter 10’s second pillar is “cultural conditioning of political origin”. That culture is downstream of politics is a claim about direction, not a demonstration.

The two resource curses are not symmetric Ch. 10

Gold ran at 4–7% of GDP with essentially no conditions into a state with no parliament. EU funds run at 2–3% into a parliamentary democracy with external audit and a body of evaluation. Palma acknowledges the difference and argues past it. The political-accountability argument is strong; the Dutch-disease analogy is weaker.

Eastern Europe is the live test, and it is unfinished Ch. 10

His explanation for why the East converged despite the funds, that communism inoculated it against statism, is cultural, and so sits awkwardly with the point above. He also allows in a footnote that the funds may yet be doing net harm there too, not enough to stop convergence for now. That position is hard to falsify until it isn’t.

Was the Estado Novo fascist? Lucena 1976; Rosas; Costa Pinto

Palma’s “not fascist” is a respectable position in a long Portuguese argument, close to António Costa Pinto’s, and opposed by historians such as Fernando Rosas. His further claim, that the label now does political work, is an argument about the present, not about the regime.

The Estado Novo chapter is fenced, but the fence is not the argument Ch. 9

Palma says plainly that dictatorship is condemnable whatever its results, and that a democracy from 1945 might have spent less on war and more on schools. But the chapter’s structure (the good news carefully quantified, the counterfactual left “highly speculative”) inevitably reads as more favourable than the sentences of condemnation, and he knows it will be quoted that way.

Nobody is to blame, which is convenient Ch. 10

He declines to blame any current party because party behaviour is a product of circumstances, and doubts different leaders would have produced a different present. That is consistent, untestable, and, taken seriously, leaves no domestic actor with a reason to try.

The broader finding that windfalls often leave countries poorer, through economics and, more lastingly, politics.
Synthetic control
A counterfactual built as a weighted blend of other countries matched to the case before the shock.
Methuen Treaty (1703)
Anglo-Portuguese trade treaty: English woollens admitted, Portuguese wine favoured over French.
Real Mesa Censória
Pombal’s royal board of censorship, founded 1768.
Aula da Esfera
The mathematics and navigation lectures at the Jesuit college of Santo Antão, Lisbon, 1590–1759.
Devorismo
“The devouring”: the auction of church property after 1834.
Mata-Frades
“Friar-killer”: nickname of Joaquim António de Aguiar, author of the 1834 law abolishing the male religious orders.
Regeneração, Fontismo
The stable regime from 1851, and Fontes Pereira de Melo’s programme of railways and roads.
Formiga Branca
“White Ant”: the First Republic’s irregular political police.
Camioneta-fantasma
The “ghost lorry” of October 1921, whose crew killed António Granjo and others.
Estado Novo
The “New State”: Salazar’s regime, formally from the 1933 constitution to 1974, after the military dictatorship of 1926–33.
PIDE
The Estado Novo’s political police.
Postos escolares
Rudimentary village classrooms staffed by barely qualified assistants, the Estado Novo’s cheap extension of schooling.
Plano dos Centenários
The 1940 school-building plan, named for the centenaries of Portugal’s founding and restoration.
EFTA
The European Free Trade Association, which Portugal co-founded in 1960.
PEDIP
The EU-funded industrial development programme of the late 1980s and 1990s.
PRR
The post-pandemic Recovery and Resilience Plan, €22.2 billion to 2026.
Execution rate
The share of allocated EU funds actually spent; Palma’s ideal is zero.

Using it

Reading it

There is no English edition. The book is in Portuguese, 404 pages, published by Dom Quixote in 2023. Nearly all the underlying research is published in English, much of it open access.

  1. Chapters 3 and 6 first

    The GDP series and the golden curse are the load-bearing walls; everything else supports them or follows from them.

  2. Chapter 2

    The Cortes against the English Parliament, written largely against Why Nations Fail.

  3. Chapters 4–5

    The two favourite explanations, empire and religion, cleared away.

  4. Chapters 7–8

    The nineteenth century and the Republic: intentions against results.

  5. Chapter 9, last and slowly

    The one designed to be misquoted; the fencing around it is part of the argument.

  6. Chapter 10 and the epilogue

    The present-day warning and the case against EU funds.

The research behind it

  • Palma and Reis, “From Convergence to Divergence: Portuguese Economic Growth, 1527–1850”, Journal of Economic History (2019): the series in Chapter 3.
  • Henriques and Palma, “Comparative European Institutions and the Little Divergence, 1385–1800”, Journal of Economic Growth (2023): the Cortes in Chapter 2.
  • Kedrosky and Palma, “The Cross of Gold: Brazilian Treasure and the Decline of Portugal”, Journal of Economic History (2025): Chapter 6.
  • Palma and Reis, “Can Autocracy Promote Literacy? Evidence from a Cultural Alignment Success Story”, Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization (2021): Chapters 8–9.

Read alongside

  • Why Nations Fail, summarised on this site: the institutional account Chapter 2 corrects on Iberia, and the frame Palma’s own story still fits.
  • Guns, Germs, and Steel, summarised on this site: the long-run comparative frame, and a different answer to why Europe built empires.
  • Leonor Freire Costa, Pedro Lains and Susana Münch Miranda, An Economic History of Portugal, 1143–2010 (2016): the standard survey in English, on which the gold chapter draws.
