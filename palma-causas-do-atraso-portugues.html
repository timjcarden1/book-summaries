The Causes of
Portuguese
Backwardness Why Portugal is rich by world standards and poor by European ones — a quantitative economic history that dates the fall precisely, and blames almost nobody the country expects.

Palma is an economic historian at Manchester who spent a decade rebuilding Portuguese GDP, wages, prices, population and fiscal capacity from the archives, year by year, back to 1527. This book is what those numbers say, and what they say is uncomfortable in every direction: Portugal was not behind in 1700. It became behind in the eighteenth century, and the cause was Brazilian gold. Everything the country normally blames — empire, Catholicism, the Inquisition, the 1755 earthquake, Napoleon, the Estado Novo — either happened at the wrong time or points the wrong way.

Orientation

The Book, and the Shape of Its Argument

What Palma is claiming, why it is unusual, and how the ten chapters fit together.

The question in the first line of the preamble is the whole book: why is Portugal today a rich country by world standards but a poor one in a European context? Palma's answer has two halves. The historical half says the backwardness is roughly three centuries deep and has a datable origin. The contemporary half says the mechanism that produced it in 1720 is running again now, under a different name — EU structural funds.

The book opens where every Portuguese discussion of decline has opened since 1871: with Antero de Quental at the Casino Lisbonense, arguing that Portugal fell in the sixteenth century because it stayed Catholic and submitted to the Counter-Reformation. Palma's opening move is to say that Quental — whose lecture Eduardo Lourenço called the founding act of modern Portuguese culture, and who shot himself on a garden bench in Ponta Delgada twenty years later — was wrong about both the date and the cause. The rest of the book is the demonstration.

The four things this book is arguing against
Myth 1

Empire made Portugal rich

Intercontinental trade was a marginal share of a farming economy in the 16th and 17th centuries. It mattered to the Crown's budget in some decades. It never made the country rich, and in the eighteenth century it made it poor.

Myth 2

Catholicism held it back

Literacy, numeracy, family structure and income show no Catholic–Protestant gap before the eighteenth century. Belgium industrialised second. Ireland is now among the richest countries in Europe.

Myth 3

Absolutism was ancient

The Cortes rejected 7.3% of royal tax requests between 1385 and 1600; the English Parliament rejected none. Real Portuguese absolutism is an eighteenth-century phenomenon, and it has a cause.

Myth 4

The Estado Novo caused the gap

Portugal was already the poorest and most illiterate country in Western Europe when the regime inherited it. Under it, income per head multiplied by five and child illiteracy went from 58% to 3%.

"Everyone who has governed in Portuguese affairs by discourse has erred and been lost; and by this route were lost, even among us, those whom men held to be of the greatest judgement."Padre António Vieira · epigraph to the book

A note on method, because the method is the argument

Palma's authority here is not interpretive but archival and statistical. With Jaime Reis he reconstructed Portuguese real GDP per head annually from 1527 to 1850 out of account books from convents, monasteries, hospitals, misericórdias, palaces, farms, the University of Coimbra, the cathedrals of Lisbon and Porto, and municipal chambers — prices, wages and rents, converted into a modern metric system out of a chaos of local units where an almude meant 16.8 litres in Lisbon and 25.4 in Porto. The consumption basket used to deflate it is Portuguese rather than the standard Strasbourg one: 182kg of bread, 26kg of meat, 5.2 litres of olive oil, five chickens, 52 eggs, 28 litres of wine, soap, linen, candles and firewood, with wine substituted for beer and olive oil for butter.

He is blunt about what that buys him and what it does not. Levels before the nineteenth century carry at least ten per cent of error, so small differences between countries mean nothing; what the series identifies reliably is direction and timing. That distinction does almost all the work in the book. It is why he can say with confidence that Portuguese income fell sharply from the 1770s without claiming to know exactly how rich Portugal was in 1750 — and why a great deal of received Portuguese history turns out to be about the wrong decade.

Part I

Portugal Since the Late Middle Ages

Three chapters of facts, deliberately kept separate from the interpretation that follows.

Part I is the shorter half and the one Palma calls objective: population, institutions, economy, each traced from the late Middle Ages to the early nineteenth century in detail and then forward to the present in summary. Its purpose is to fix the facts that Part II will have to explain — and, in the process, to remove several of the explanations the country is most attached to before the argument even begins.

Population

Portugal has existed in a coherent linguistic and geographical continuity since the twelfth century — remarkable in Europe and almost unique in the world, and all the more so because, as Orlando Ribeiro showed, the country has no natural geographical unity at all. The south differs sharply from the north, the coast from the interior; the borders are a medieval political construction, unchanged since the Treaty of Alcañices at the end of the thirteenth century apart from the loss of Olivença in 1801. Whatever else Portugal has lacked, it has never had an identity problem. Palma notes the oddity of this in passing and then moves to the numbers.

The counting starts with the numeramento of 1527–32, a national count of hearths (fogos) — each inhabited house living in common economy — which at four to five people per hearth gives a population slightly over a million. A second hearth count in 1706–12 gives 2.35 million; a parish survey in 1758, three years after the earthquake, gives nearly 2.5 million; two crude censuses around 1800 give almost three million. Between the snapshots, parish registers of baptisms and burials let the series be interpolated annually. Antero de Quental, incidentally, claimed Portugal had three million people under João II and one million by 1640 — a collapse that fitted his thesis and never happened.

West of the Hajnal line, on every measure

In 1965 John Hajnal proposed a line from St Petersburg to Trieste, west of which the "European Marriage Pattern" prevailed: late female marriage, high permanent celibacy, and — later work added — consensual marriage, property rights for women and higher female labour-force participation. This matters because an influential Dutch literature argues that within Western Europe there was a second, north–south split: women in Italy, Spain and Portugal supposedly married younger, worked less, were paid a customary half of men's wages regardless of the market, and therefore invested less in their children's schooling. That claim, if true, would neatly explain southern backwardness in cultural terms.

Palma's work with Jaime Reis and Lisbeth Rodrigues finds it is simply false. Female age at first marriage in Portugal was generally above 25 nationwide; female celibacy exceeded 10%; women worked after marriage and self-employed at the same rate as in the Netherlands; their wages ran at about two-thirds of men's — the same ratio as northern Europe, not one-half; inheritance regimes were comparable; and the sex ratio of survivors, far from favouring boys as in China, India and the Middle East, sometimes ran the other way. In Porto between 1680 and 1780 most abandoned foundlings were boys, and in the mid-nineteenth century infant mortality was generally higher among boys.

What chapter 1 removes from the table Family structure, fertility norms and the treatment of women were, until at least the late nineteenth century, indistinguishable in Portugal from those of the countries that grew rich. Since parental investment in children is the standard cultural channel from family structure to human capital, this closes off the most popular non-political explanation of the gap before the argument has properly started.

Political Institutions

The Cortes were a real constraint on the Crown — and by several measures a better one than the English House of Commons, until the middle of the seventeenth century.

This is the chapter that most directly attacks the international literature. The standard story — North and Thomas, Tilly, Ertman, Maddicott, and above all Acemoglu and Robinson in Why Nations Fail — holds that Iberian crowns were more despotic than the English one from the late Middle Ages onward, and that England's advantage has medieval roots. Palma and António Castro Henriques went and counted.

In late-medieval Portugal the Cortes assembled the three estates, and the decisive voice was not the clergy or the nobility but the Povos — the procurators of the municipalities, who alone were taxed and who therefore alone voted on taxation. They were elected. In Loulé in 1385 the town council summoned "the good men of the said town and the greater part of the people", including the poor, to elect the procurators to the Cortes of Coimbra, which then elected João I king. The oldest surviving electoral roll, Porto 1497, lists 34 named voters — three cobblers, a notary, a knight — and the clerk adds that "many others of the people" voted whose names he did not know. Lisbon in 1580 had an electorate of about a hundred, including 48 representatives of the craft guilds; Porto's seventeenth-century Cortes elections averaged 128 voters and were routinely won by a handful of votes.

The English contrast is not flattering. Until 1628 elections in Newcastle, Bristol and Gloucester were controlled outright by endogamous local oligarchies. The Earl of Pembroke intervened decisively in nearly ninety elections for the parliaments of 1614 and 1628 and was responsible for the choice of 98 members. Non-resident "carpet-bagger" members were common in England, forbidden in Castile until the seventeenth century and very rare in Portugal. Municipalities in the Indies had the right to sit in the Iberian Cortes — Goa from 1645, Salvador da Bahia 1653, São Luís do Maranhão 1676 — and the absence of that right in the English system was, famously, one cause of American independence.

The test that settles it: who could actually say no
The inversion, in four other markets

The reversal shows up wherever the two systems can be compared. Forced and semi-forced loans, and money levied without parliamentary consent, were more frequent in England than in Castile or Portugal until the late sixteenth century, and the pattern flips thereafter. In the early sixteenth century both Iberian crowns could borrow at long — even perpetual — maturities at low international rates, which England could not do at any price until the second half of the seventeenth century. Iberian crowns then paid lower rates than private borrowers; by the eighteenth century they paid higher ones, because, as the Count of Castanheira observed in 1541, a merchant can have a debtor jailed but cannot do the same to a king. England went the other way. And where England's Court of Wards was described by its own historians as "fiscal feudalism", confiscating the estates of minors it was supposed to protect, its Portuguese counterpart the Juízo dos Órfãos behaved considerably more evenhandedly.

History written by the winners Palma's phrase for the Anglo-Dutch literature that projects eighteenth-century Iberian absolutism backwards to the Magna Carta. It has a domestic accomplice: the nineteenth-century Portuguese "liberals", Herculano above all, who invented an Old Regime despotism reaching back to the sixteenth century because they needed a tyranny to have overthrown. Both versions are still taught. Neither survives the sources.

He is careful to put the twentieth century in proportion in both directions. Portugal in 1950, at $2,086, was poorer than Mozambique in 2010 and clearly poorer than Cape Verde in 2008. Expansion and Empire

"Only one problem: none of that happened." Palma on the conversation he has with compatriots abroad about the vast lands Portugal supposedly conquered.

Palma's claim is not that the empire was small in extent — it was enormous on a map — but that it was structurally incapable of enriching Portugal, and that European empires are better understood as a consequence of prior institutional and technological advantage than as its cause.

Portugal never had a land empire and could not have had one. Asia around 1600 held over 350 million people against Europe's 75 million; conquest was arithmetically out of the question. What Portugal had was a thalassocracy resting on the two military technologies where Europe was genuinely ahead — warships carrying effective artillery, and fortresses built to European designs. The feitorias were trading posts with warehouses, chapels and administrative buildings, usually negotiated with local rulers. Presence depended on local tolerance: at Macau the Chinese could threaten to close the Porta do Cerco and starve the city, and did so more than once. In Japan the Portuguese stayed until the emperor decided otherwise, and then did not.

The seventeenth-century losses were mostly not Dutch victories at all but shifts in Asian geopolitics: Syriam in 1612, Hormuz in 1622, Hooghly in 1632, expulsion from Japan in 1638, the Canara ports in 1654. Where the Dutch did win, in the Moluccas and at São Jorge da Mina, Palma attributes it to better use of force and diplomacy rather than to any superior commercial organisation — and points out that in Brazil the West India Company, capitalised more heavily than the famous VOC, was decisively beaten.

Revenue to the Crown is not wealth to the country

This is the distinction Palma says generations of historians collapsed. The empire mattered to the royal budget in some decades — the archives, including the oldest surviving estado da fazenda of 1526, allow it to be measured — but the state was a very small part of the economy. Both things can be true at once: imperial receipts significant for the treasury, imperial trade marginal for the country. The economic manifestation of empire in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, he writes, can be described without much exaggeration as the arrival of a few ships a year at the Terreiro do Paço.

On slavery as a development strategy Palma's argument runs the same way here and is deliberately unsentimental. Since the empire did not make Portugal rich, it is also necessarily false that Portuguese wealth was produced by slavery — there was no such wealth to explain. His work with Guilherme Lambais shows unskilled living standards in Bahia comparable to Europe's in the late sixteenth century and declining through the seventeenth and eighteenth as slave arrivals rose; with Hélder Carvalhal, that unskilled living standards in Luanda and Benguela fell across the nineteenth and much of the twentieth, because coerced labour distorted the labour market. Slavery enriched some colonial elites and impeded the development of everywhere it touched. Between the mid-nineteenth and mid-twentieth centuries, average incomes in Portugal were not much above those in Brazil or in its own colonies.

He is equally unimpressed by the counterfactual that Dutch colonisation would have served Brazil better: Suriname is today less developed than Brazil, and Dutch rule in Java was extractive well into the nineteenth century — at its peak in the 1850s the batig slot transfers were nearly 4% of Dutch GDP and over half of total government revenue, a far heavier extraction than anything Portugal managed. What he does concede is that Portugal transmitted to Brazil an institutional design inferior to the one England left in the United States, and he explains why in exactly one sentence: because by the eighteenth century, when the transmission happened, Portugal's own institutions had been wrecked by gold.

The comparative case against the Protestant-ethic thesis is assembled quickly. Detailed local data for late-nineteenth-century Prussia — a genuinely mixed population — show no differences between Catholics and Protestants in savings, incomes or literacy once the discriminated Polish minority is excluded; the only real gap is ethnic, and Max Weber was himself a German nationalist hostile to Polish interests. England's sustained growth begins more than a century after Henry VIII's death. The second country to industrialise was Belgium, Catholic and long ruled by the Spanish Habsburgs. France, Catholic, raised incomes by a different route entirely, becoming the first country in the world to undergo a fertility transition — decades before 1789. Ireland is now among Europe's richest. And there were books printed continuously in Portugal from the end of the fifteenth century, whatever the international datasets that record Portugal as zero may imply.

The Inquisition, sized honestly

Palma's treatment here is the passage most likely to be quoted against him and the one he argues most carefully. He does not defend the institution; he asks what it can and cannot explain. The Portuguese and Spanish Inquisitions were courts — they required evidence, acquitted many, and executed a small share of the accused, and the executions themselves were carried out by the civil authorities. Their preoccupation was heresy, blood purity and morals — sodomy, bigamy, belief — rather than science. They regulated rather than censored the disciplines we have since abandoned: judicial astrology was forbidden, natural astrology permitted, so one could forecast for navigation, medicine and agriculture but not predict the death of kings or the success of a marriage. Where Catholic authorities censored books, Protestant ones burned them.

The Dutch Republic, usually held up as the tolerant exception, prosecuted homosexuality more ferociously than almost anywhere in Europe: after the courts decided in the early 1730s that "this vice must be exterminated", dozens were executed including young men, hundreds fled the country, and roughly two hundred were killed before the Napoleonic Code decriminalised same-sex relations in 1811. In England, Alan Turing was chemically castrated in 1952. Many Portuguese Jews chose the Levant over the Netherlands — and the Levant did not become a dynamic economy either.

What actually shut Portuguese science down, and who did it

The chapter's hinge is a single example. The University of Évora, run by the Jesuits, taught science as well as theology — its azulejo panels from the 1740s illustrating the vacuum and magnetism can still be visited. It was closed in 1759 with the expulsion of the Society and did not reopen for more than two hundred years. Its lecture rooms became stables. And the man who ordered it was not a churchman: he was the Marquês de Pombal, and the Inquisitor-General who condemned the Jesuit Gabriel Malagrida to death was Pombal's own brother, Paulo António de Carvalho e Mendonça, installed after Pombal jailed his predecessor.

What looks on the surface like religious persecution was in fact the political power instrumentalising the resources and prerogatives of the Inquisition.Chapter 5

The other example is Bento de Moura Portugal (1702–66): early Newtonian, Fellow of the Royal Society, inventor of a steam engine, denounced anonymously in January 1759, imprisoned in the Junqueira fort in July 1760 on charges of criticising the government and sympathising with Malagrida and the Távoras, and dead in his cell in 1766. Rómulo de Carvalho called him one of Pombal's victims. These catastrophes, Palma writes, had no cultural or religious origin whatever. They had a political one.

Culture reacts to politics at least as much as the reverse His standing rebuttal to cultural determinism is that the alleged fixed traits keep changing. J. Rentes de Carvalho wrote in the 1970s that Portuguese were constitutionally timid and submissive and that the gap between rich and poor countries would only widen — and the period since has contained the largest and fastest exit from poverty in history, in China from the late 1970s and India from the 1990s. Portugal did develop with tremendous force in the twentieth century while still a deeply Catholic country. Whatever the constraint was, it was not the mass.

The Golden Curse

Gold found in Minas Gerais in 1694; the news public by 1697; the Cortes summoned for the last time in 1697–98 and never again for 122 years. Palma does not think that sequence is a coincidence.

Before the mechanism, the counterfactual — because the force of the chapter depends on Portugal having had somewhere else to go. In the last three decades of the seventeenth century, after the Treaty of Lisbon ended hostilities with Spain in 1668, Portugal was growing, industrialising and politically improving simultaneously. Income per head rose from about 900 dollars in 1640–60 to over 1,200 by the early 1690s. The share of the workforce in agriculture fell from 64% to 59% between 1650 and 1700 while the rural non-agricultural share rose from 24% to 29%. By 1680, Portuguese industry could cover domestic and colonial demand and was exporting textiles to Castile.

This was policy, not luck. A sequence of laws between 1672 and 1698 — the Leis Pragmáticas — pursued import substitution and deeper integration with the colonies, drawing on Duarte Ribeiro de Macedo and on French Colbertism, and driven above all by D. Luís de Meneses, third Count of Ericeira, Vedor da Fazenda, roughly a modern minister of economy and finance. Foreign technicians were brought in; state–private partnerships were set up; spinning stayed in rural households while the factories did carding, dyeing and finishing.

1694: gold

Europeans had been looking for gold in the Brazilian sertão since 1531, when an expedition left São Vicente and never returned. In 1694 large quantities were found in what is now Minas Gerais. The prospectors tried to keep it quiet; the news reached the coast the following year and was public by 1697. By 1705 some fifty thousand prospectors and their slaves were living in the gold towns, fed by an emigration from Portugal of three to ten thousand people a year. The colonial administrator D. João de Lencastre worried in 1701 that Brazilian gold would end up benefiting England and Holland rather than Portugal, exactly as American silver had done for Spain. He was right, and nobody acted on it.

The two channels

The price evidence is direct: over the eighteenth century the ratio of non-tradable to tradable prices rose about 30%, tracking the gold cycle. Meat and eggs — non-tradable before refrigeration — rose relative to wheat, olive oil and textiles. In the southern Alentejo, rising meat prices drove large-scale conversion of low-yielding cereal land to pasture from about 1710, and the same happened in the Algarve; by 1722 Algarve livestock could supply not only its own province but others. This was a rational response by farmers to relative prices, and it left the country importing 5.5–7% of its grain consumption against England's 3%, with imported cereals supplying 55% of Lisbon's consumption in 1729 and 72% by 1778.

The trade deficit rose from two billion réis in 1720 (2.4% of nominal GDP) to over four billion by 1750 (3.9%) and six billion in 1756 (6.1%), and tracked the gold arrivals closely throughout. Britain sent 18% of its manufactured exports to Portugal in 1741–45 and over half of certain products for much of the century; the gold left Lisbon for Plymouth on regular packet boats. A visitor in 1760 wrote that the Portuguese were very rich in gold and jewels but that their wealth did not come from anything produced in Portugal, and was astonished that even the shoes people wore came from England or France.

Methuen, demoted Since Friedrich List in the nineteenth century, the 1703 Methuen Treaty — 23% preferential tariffs for English textiles against a third off the duty on Portuguese wine relative to French — has carried the blame for Portuguese de-industrialisation. Palma inverts the causation. The treaty was itself a consequence of the gold that was already arriving and was known to be arriving for a long time to come: it was signed over the objection of D. Luís da Cunha, Portugal's ambassador in London and a partisan of Ericeira's manufacturing policy, who was removed from the negotiation and spent the following decades arguing that the treaty was bankrupting factories that were "the remedy of innumerable peoples". Methuen was a mechanism. It was not the cause.
Where the money went

Private owners spent it on imported consumption goods, on churches and convents, and on land. The Crown was no better. João V (r. 1706–50) built the vast palace-convent at Mafra, spent heavily on a numerous clergy, and sent a spectacular embassy to Pope Clement XI whose three gilded triumphal coaches can still be seen at the Museu dos Coches in Belém. The credit markets developed miserably: a 5% legal ceiling on certain contracts from 1698, further distortion by Pombal in 1757, no land registry so collateral could be mortgaged to exhaustion, and in the 1770s an outright ban on the Misericórdia of Lisbon — in practice a bank — lending to private borrowers, so that the money could be channelled to the state instead. The Misericórdia of Porto and others kept lending only to the private sector, which tells you what lending to the Crown was worth.

Pombal

Palma's insistence is that Pombal is a symptom before he is a cause. By the time the 1755 earthquake let Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo become the central figure of the state, gold had been distorting the economy for half a century and the Cortes had not met for fifty years. Royal power in Portugal was already absolute more than half a century before José I came to the throne. Pombal could do what he did because there was nothing left to stop him — and, tellingly, his power ended the moment the king died in 1777. What he did with it, however, Palma judges without any softening at all.

The single worst decision: 1759

In the middle of the eighteenth century Portugal had two universities and a network of pre-university schools across the whole country, and its human capital was only slightly behind the most advanced parts of Europe. Roughly twenty thousand students were in pre-university education, essentially all of them taught by the Society of Jesus, and taught free — free tuition was standard practice in Jesuit colleges. The Colégio de Santo Antão in Lisbon had between 2,500 and 3,000 pupils; even the small colleges had a few hundred. The Aula da Esfera at Santo Antão ran uninterrupted from 1590 to 1759, taught in Portuguese, open to laymen, free, covering logarithms, the telescope and the Mercator projection, with distinguished foreign teachers brought in.

Palma is careful about what the claim is and is not. The Jesuits could not by themselves have delivered mass education; there were not enough of them. What matters is that they were the base from which a later expansion — by the state, if necessary — could have been built, because you need human capital to make human capital, and in a country of illiterates the missing input is teachers. The Director-General of Studies appointed to replace them, D. Tomás de Almeida, could not recruit staff and reported in 1763 that inhabitants could not afford the masters' salaries and were not sending their children, "so that many talents are lost that would be useful to the Fatherland". Two years later he found that in several of the few remaining schools teachers were still using Jesuit grammars; the teachers were suspended and the books burned in public. A law of 1772 laying the foundations of the future school system stated in its preamble that most of the population did not need to be literate, being reserved "to the rustic and humble service of the State".

Portugal became a country without schools. It would not have twenty thousand pupils in pre-university education again until the 1930s — with a population nearly three times larger.Chapter 6

The verdict, stated plainly "It does not seem excessive to write that Pombal was the worst politician ever to govern Portugal." Palma adds that he is not generally in favour of pulling down statues, but finds it curious that this one stands in the country's best-known roundabout — a monument, he says, to the triumph of propaganda over truth two and a half centuries on, erected by a later regime (the First Republic) whose own gap between declared educational intention and delivered result would be just as wide.

By 1800 the share of Portuguese adults who could sign their name was well behind the rest of Western Europe — in sharp contrast to fifty years earlier. The country entered the nineteenth century with its industrial base destroyed, an archaic political system, and not even enough schooling to educate the minimum elite from which a later expansion could grow, precisely as most of Western Europe was preparing to have industrial revolutions. That, in one sentence, is the book's thesis about where Portuguese backwardness comes from.

Palma's problem with this period is terminological before it is empirical. "Constitutional Monarchy" implies that constitutional rules had not operated before, which chapter 2 showed to be false. "Absolute Monarchy" implies a limitless power the Miguelists — who called themselves realistas — would not have recognised. And "Liberal Monarchy" is simply wrong: in economic policy the regime was never liberal at all.

This last was demolished by Jaime Reis in a series of papers in the 1980s. Far from being a free-trading regime, Portugal kept some of the highest tariffs in Europe from the schedule of 1837 onward, straight through the first age of globalisation. Part of the reason was fiscal — the state depended on customs revenue and could not touch the schedule — and part was that the public discourse of "liberalism" held Anglo-Portuguese trade treaties responsible for the country's backwardness, so free trade had no advocates. The liberal label survives only in a legal sense: nominal equality before the law, nominal abolition of privilege. In practice, as an English visitor observed, "the rich oppressor exults in the certainty of obtaining legal support, while the poor victim of his tyranny drains a bitter cup and then falls silent."

The dissolution of the monasteries, and what it cost

In 1832 Mouzinho da Silveira abolished ecclesiastical tithes. In 1834 Joaquim António de Aguiar — a freemason, and the leader of the Cartistas, remembered ever after as Mata-Frades, the friar-killer — signed the law of 30 May extinguishing all convents, monasteries, colleges and religious houses of the male orders, roughly 450 of them, and putting their property up for public auction. The female orders were forbidden novices and thus condemned by attrition. Many of today's state hospitals and public buildings are that expropriation.

The auctions were known as devorismo — the devouring — and with reason: Treasury bonds were accepted at face value however deeply discounted in the market, and the most valuable properties were auctioned in Lisbon rather than in the district seats, which is a fairly precise description of a mechanism for transferring assets to people with information and cash. What Palma emphasises is the collateral damage. Whatever else the monasteries were, they were the last remaining literate institution present across the whole territory, and thus the only remaining cheap base for a national school system. Primary schooling was made compulsory by law in 1835 — and remained a dead letter for the rest of the century. Nothing had been learned from Pombal.

Fontismo: capital without people

The Regeneração of 1851 finally delivered stability, and Fontes Pereira de Melo delivered an ambitious programme of railways, macadamised roads, bridges and telegraph lines, financed by deficits and largely by British credit. The returns fell well short of expectations, and Palma's diagnosis is that they were bound to: there was no commensurate investment in human capital to go with the physical capital. In the mid-nineteenth century, all of Portugal's "factories" together were worth less than two ships loaded with port wine. The 1881 Industrial Enquiry has the directors of the Tomar cotton mill saying it in their own words: "One of the greatest difficulties we struggle with comes from the lack of qualification of the personnel. The lack of general instruction and the almost absolute absence of technical instruction means we have great difficulty not only in acquiring good workers but also in obtaining competent foremen." Literacy in the textile sector ran below 20%, often at 10%.

The latifundia were not the problem Another myth Reis destroyed, and Palma repeats: the great Alentejo estates were not inefficient. Steam threshing was adopted there when it became profitable to adopt it — barely before 1900, dominant by the early 1930s — which is exactly what profit-maximising landowners should have done given costs and the politics of cereal protection. No appeal to backward mentalities is required.

The regime's end was financial and humiliating in that order. Emigrant remittances from Brazil had become a material share of national income; the Brazilian republican revolution of November 1889 collapsed the exchange rate and cut the value of those remittances by more than 80%. That was enough. Portugal left the gold standard and defaulted in 1891, and in 1898 Britain and Germany went so far as to agree how they would divide the Portuguese colonies if the debts went unpaid. Eça de Queirós wrote to a friend: Portugal acabou — Portugal is finished. The year before the default had come the British Ultimatum over the territory between Angola and Mozambique, which Portugal could not conceivably have resisted militarily and which became the single greatest propaganda gift the republicans ever received. A Portuguesa was written in 1890 as one of several patriotic songs against the Ultimatum, and the republicans took it.

The verdict he gives it

Politically, Palma marks it moderately positive, and the reasoning is worth following because it is the mirror image of his verdict on the Estado Novo. Parliaments met continuously; from the 1878 extension the franchise reached 72% of men over 21, one of the highest proportions in Europe, and unlike the First Republic that followed, illiterates could vote. Censorship was low by any comparative standard and would not be that low again until the late twentieth century. Economically it was a century lost: a rural, illiterate, miserable country in which failed harvests produced unemployment, hunger, beggary and banditry, diverging further from Western Europe with every decade, and entering the twentieth century as the poorest state in Europe except Albania.

The Partido Republicano Português took power by coup in October 1910 and dominated everything that followed: fiercely anticlerical, Jacobin, heavily masonic — about half of ministers and members of parliament were freemasons. Palma's characterisation is unsparing: a sectarian and radical regime, permanently at war over culture, whose partisans considered themselves the owners of truth and did little beyond acting in their own interest. It was also less democratic than the monarchy it replaced. Elections were neither free nor fair; the PRP's machine distorted the results; the republican press treated every critic of the government as a traitor; an irregular political police, the Formiga Branca, answered to PRP leaders and ran intimidation and violence including raids on newspapers; the heavily armed Guarda Nacional Republicana was created as the regime's praetorian guard.

In 1921 the camioneta-fantasma — the ghost lorry — driven by GNR and naval personnel arrested and executed several opposition figures, among them the outgoing prime minister António Granjo and two of the protagonists of the 1910 revolution itself, Machado Santos and José Carlos da Maia. Alfredo da Silva, founder of the CUF industrial group, was beaten and shot and fled the country; he called Portugal "a great madhouse" in 1925 and did not return until 1927. This is not, Palma observes drily, an environment for industrial development.

Why the republican school failed

The declared aim was to create a new man — republican, nationalist, secular, physically and psychologically vigorous, and ready to defend the regime. A Ministry of Instruction was created, compulsory schooling set at three years and extended to five in 1919, teacher training and pay addressed, universities founded in Lisbon and Porto, new schools built. Most of it never left the paper. The Law of Separation of Church and State, presented by Afonso Costa at the Masonic Palace in March 1911 — the meeting at which he said he would destroy Catholicism in Portugal within three generations — nationalised church property, put worship under state supervision, and reorganised parish life around lay commissions that excluded priests. Two years later, lay commissions existed in 6% of parishes and a fifth of the clergy were drawing the state pension. The Jesuits were expelled for the third time in Portuguese history, three days after the revolution, by the simple device of declaring the decrees of 1759 and 1834 still valid. In the following month Afonso Costa proposed moving Pombal's remains to the national pantheon.

Crucifixes were banned from classrooms; religious doctrine was removed from primary schooling in October 1910; schools were made co-educational, boys and girls together in classrooms and playgrounds. And that, Palma and Reis argue, is where the programme died. In a country that was rural, poor, Catholic and socially conservative, where children were real labour — gathering pasture and pine cones, carrying water and manure, minding animals and younger siblings, and working alongside adults from twelve — parents were not going to give that labour up in order to have their children indoctrinated by republican atheism.

The generalisable lesson "It is not possible to change an entire society suddenly, from the top down, by decree. And it is far easier to change a society if the change is made in consonance with the culture of most of its people than against it." This sentence is the load-bearing beam under chapter 9, and it is why Palma treats the First Republic and the Estado Novo as a single natural experiment in education policy rather than as two moral categories.

On the economy the verdict is also negative but more forgiving. The regime kept the traditional protectionism; entry into the Great War in 1916 was a deliberate political choice, taken by an unusually broad coalition, to defend the African empire and win international validation, and it cost roughly 100,000 men mobilised and 25,000 casualties along with severe financial damage — but Palma notes it was a choice, and one made a decade before the Estado Novo existed, which undercuts the idea that sacrificing the country for the Ultramar was that regime's invention. He also corrects a favourite Estado Novo myth in the other direction: by 1923–24 the final republican governments had already stabilised the public finances. Salazar restored order to the dictatorship's accounts, not to the Republic's.

Palma builds a fence before he builds the argument, and it is worth quoting the shape of it. The Estado Novo was a dictatorship. Political prisons, censorship, the crime of opinion and puppet elections are not merely condemnable but unacceptable, and no economic success justifies gagging liberty, because liberty is an end in itself. He has, he says, no political sympathy whatever for the regime. Having said that, he then asks what actually happened, and refuses to let the answer be determined by what would be convenient.

A first and necessary distinction: with Pombal and with Afonso Costa, the danger was mistaking impeccable plans for delivered results. With Salazar the danger runs the other way. His public rhetoric extolled poverty, rurality and austerity; in 1935 he publicly doubted the benefits of teaching people to read — "to read what?" — and that remark has misled generations of intellectuals. It was very far from describing what the regime actually did, which included propaganda posters boasting about its own victories against illiteracy and the growth of technical schooling.

On repression, measured rather than asserted

Comparatively, the regime was not especially repressive: it did not restore the death penalty, abolished in Portugal for political crimes in 1852, civil crimes in 1867 and military crimes in 1911. There were extrajudicial killings — Humberto Delgado and Arajaryr Campos, murdered by the PIDE near Badajoz in 1965 — and the sanitary conditions at Tarrafal amounted to a death sentence for some of those sent there, though fewer than forty died. Deportation of political prisoners to the colonies was not a Salazarist invention; the monarchy and the First Republic had both practised it. Palma's explanation for the low level of violence is not the mild manners of the leadership but the absence of need: repression rose in 1943–49 when the regime felt threatened and fell afterwards; censorship was looser in the Ultramar during the colonial war than in the metropolis. Most of the time it did not need to be more repressive, because most of the country was conservative, rural and illiterate, and politics passed it by.

Not fascist — and the distinction is not pedantry Real fascist regimes were proud of it. The Estado Novo never defined or behaved as one: no totalitarian party, no mass movement, no personality cult, no state religion despite Salazar's Catholic origins. Salazar suppressed the nearest Portuguese equivalent, Rolão Preto's National-Syndicalism, banning it in 1934, and the União Nacional was an "anti-party" that never sought a popular clientele. The English communist Ralf Fox, visiting in 1936 on his way to die in Spain, wrote that on the theatrical side Salazar did not meet the minimum qualifications for membership of the dictators' union: he never appeared in public or spoke on the radio, reviewed no troops, wore no uniform, killed no enemies with his own hands and had no photograph in shop windows. Palma insists on the accuracy of the label not to launder the regime — it was antidemocratic, antiparliamentary, nationalist and antiliberal, and never hid it — but because, as chapter 10 argues, the false label has been doing political work ever since.
The answer: the country stopped fighting its school

The evidence Palma and Reis published in 2021 comes from more than nine thousand individual records of twenty-year-old conscripts drawn from military inspection books in the Army General Archive, covering 1924–1950 and the whole country. Because inspection was compulsory the sample is representative for men, and because height correlates strongly with socioeconomic background in poor societies, it can be sorted into quintiles and used to ask who was being reached.

The mechanism is the interesting part, and it is not spending. Eleven days after the coup of 28 May 1926 the government ordered the separation of the sexes in school buildings and playgrounds; the crucifixes went back on the walls; textbooks devoted dozens of pages to Catholic religion; teaching was reduced to a single national programme and a single national manual built around reading, writing, arithmetic and socially conservative civic virtue. Required schooling was cut from five years to four and then to three — which many readers have taken as proof of hostility to education, and which Palma reads as the opposite: the four and five year requirements had never been enforced and were therefore fictions. The regime legislated the country it had, and then expanded from there. It later raised the requirement again, reaching six years under Galvão Teles in the 1960s, who was explicit about the goal: "so that the reform would not remain on paper and would become effective, as it effectively did."

The decomposition in the Palma–Reis paper attributes about 70% of the fall in illiteracy to cultural alignment generating demand from families, 20% to improved material conditions, and only 10% to school building as such — with the largest and fastest falls in the most Catholic regions of the country, which is the identifying evidence. School construction did happen, and mattered: the Plano dos Centenários of 1940 projected a 60% increase in primary establishments and, as Pedro Maia Gomes and Matilde Pinto Machado have shown, expanded the network especially in the poorest areas of the south and interior. But empty schools educate nobody.

"One sardine between three"

The claim that people went hungry under the Estado Novo is usually made without comparison to what came before. Palma's work with Alexandra Cermeño and Renato Pistola tests it against three archives: the Hospital de São Roque of the Misericórdia of Lisbon, the Casa Pia of Lisbon, and the Army's conscription records. Hunger and disease neither appeared with the regime nor ended with it. What is not in dispute is that both fell drastically during the period it covers, and that the fall began long before democracy or the national health service.

The reforms behind the growth, and the one thing that was not behind it

Industrialisation accelerated in the 1950s, roughly a decade before EFTA, while about half the population still worked on the land. The Electrification Law of 1944 and the Industrial Development Law of 1945 signalled that the pre-war regime of industrial licensing was becoming obsolete; the Fomento Plans of the 1950s made electricity for industry, not irrigation, the primary purpose of the new dams. Fertiliser use tripled from ten to thirty kilos a hectare between the late 1930s and the late 1950s, releasing labour from the fields — a level Germany, Belgium and the Netherlands had reached around 1910. The decision to open the economy, via the OEEC and the Marshall Plan and then EFTA in 1960 and GATT in 1962, was an internal political decision, and it changed what Portugal sold: from wine and lightly processed food, two-thirds of merchandise exports in the first half of the century, to clothing and footwear, which by the last quarter of the century were a quarter of the total — the same share port wine had held a hundred years earlier.

Real wages rose: at the CUF works at Barreiro, a detailed study found average real wages up 250% between 1925 and 1974, less than GDP per head, which implies inequality rising against capital — but inequality relative to the very richest fell sharply between the mid-1950s and the mid-1980s, and wage inequality between skilled and unskilled workers fell across nearly every sector from the 1960s. Emigration, often given as the cause of the growth, peaked at over 180,000 people in 1970 and Palma turns it into evidence against the claim: the country was absorbing most of the labour released from agriculture in the very decade it was also exporting the most people, and other periods of heavy emigration — the nineteenth century to Brazil, today to northern Europe — produced no convergence at all.

Convergence with richer Europe ran from about 1950, stalled for the decade after the 1974 revolution, resumed through the 1990s, and stopped around the turn of the century. Since then Portugal has been overtaken by nearly every Central and Eastern European country that joined the EU, most of which started far poorer in the 1990s. It has not been compensated by greater equality: the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Slovenia and Latvia grow faster and have lower income and wealth inequality. Portugal remains, across the whole OECD, exceptional for the low probability that the children of the poor escape their parents' situation.

The chapter's structural move is the one that organises the whole book: separate the proximate causes from the fundamental ones. Low productivity, low human capital, extractive elites, a dysfunctional justice system, an over-centralised state, high taxes relative to the quality of public goods — all of these are real and all of them are endogenous. They are things to be explained, not explanations. What has to be explained above all is the absence of demand for reform: not that reforms would help, which is almost trivial, but why a country that can identify its problems does not want them fixed.

The political channel, restated for the present

The economic channel is familiar: money entering pushes factors toward non-tradables — real estate, public works, often unnecessary ones — with indirect costs to the export sector. The empirical work Palma cites finds that firms receiving EU funds achieve higher sales but no change in productivity, and that even the sales effect is confined to non-tradable sectors, with much of the money going to very small firms that lack any transformative potential and for which the funds are simply a way of making a living.

The political channel is the one he thinks is decisive. Money from outside, without counterparts, lets governments distribute rents that no productivity gain has earned. It breaks the temporal link between bad policy and recession, hiding the extent of the problem behind a screen and protecting incumbents; voters reward short-run effects, and the crisis arrives long after the decisions that caused it. In a democracy the ballot box is how the public expels incompetent politicians — but that requires the public to feel the consequences in its pocket. The funds are an aspirin that masks the symptom and disables the mechanism.

He supports this with a series of episodes rather than a model: the prosecutor who came first in the competition for the European Public Prosecutor's Office — the body that would oversee fraud in the funds — and was passed over by the government; the President of the Court of Auditors not reappointed after opposing the reform of public procurement rules for EU funds; Portugal for years without a judge at the European Court of Auditors; and an Inspector-General of Finance who admitted modifying an opinion, in the name of the "national interest", so that "the tap of European money does not close."

Almost everyone insists the concern is the execution rate — that it should not be too low. I say the optimal execution rate is zero: the less executed, the better.Chapter 10

Why Eastern Europe took the same money and converged anyway

This is the obvious objection and Palma meets it directly. Several EU members have received funds and converged — but none as much, or for as long, relative to the size of their economies. More importantly, they drew different conclusions from their own recent history. Direct experience of communist dictatorship killed any romance about the heavy hand of the state; even in countries with worrying democratic trajectories such as Hungary and Poland, there are no illusions about communism, and centre parties are far more moderate on the role of the state than in Portugal.

Portugal's post-1974 cultural settlement did the opposite. A right-wing dictatorship was mischaracterised as low-intervention and free-market — an error, since public employment grew enormously under it and the state intervened heavily in several sectors — and then labelled fascist, so that the entire right, including the democratic, moderate and liberal right, could be tarred with it. Capitalism was identified with the old regime and socialism with democracy. The 1976 constitution's preamble promises to open the way to a socialist society; privatisation of the nationalised firms was constitutionally prohibited until 1989. Sá Carneiro described himself as centre-left and wanted his party in the Socialist International. Francisco Lucas Pires, who defended a liberal economy "for the sake of the workers", failed because in that context he had to fail.

The distinction that actually matters Not left versus right, which Palma regards as limited concepts, but against versus in favour of competition. On that axis the country has changed remarkably little across the twentieth century and may have got worse under democracy. Public monopolies were often replaced by private ones in energy, telecoms and banking; regulators were captured or politically controlled; the competition authority is distributed by party quota; the specialised competition court is not efficient. Even the self-described liberal right confuses defending markets with defending the profits of particular firms and professional orders. Privatising is not liberalising, and failing to see the difference is, he argues, one of the fundamental sources of contemporary backwardness.

One counterweight he does not spare. The revolution of 1974–75 was, economically, costly: land occupations, nationalisations of banks and of most large sectors including breweries, capital flight, managerial expertise flight, and wage increases without productivity increases. IMF emergency intervention was needed in 1977 and again in 1983. Comparing with Spain, whose transition after 1975 was negotiated rather than revolutionary — arguably because Spaniards had watched what happened next door — Luciano Amaral's productivity series shows the two countries growing in parallel until 1974 and diverging permanently thereafter. What corrected the worst of it was the reform period from the late 1980s, EEC accession, the PEDIP industrialisation programme — which the Commission later held up as a model for Central and Eastern European transition — and the slow restoration of a market economy. Then, from around 2000, that model exhausted itself against competition from Eastern Europe and China, the imbalances accumulated, and the resource curse took hold.

Epilogue

Reading It, and Arguing With It

Chronicle of a lethargy foretold — and what a sceptical reader should keep hold of.

"Most economic historians are optimists. It is hard not to be, when you look backwards." And then he ends pessimistically, because he cannot end any other way. The problem, he writes, is not that we do not know which policies would produce convergence. The solutions are known. The patient does not want the medicine and keeps voting for the same parties and the same prescriptions. The counterfactual in chapter 6 is doing heavy lifting

The claim that Portugal was on a mercantilist-exporter trajectory in 1690 rests on one decade, a handful of state-sponsored factories and one very good set of numbers from Covilhã. Palma concedes the scale was incomparable to the twentieth century. Whether agglomeration effects would really have survived the loss of Brazil is asserted with more confidence than it is demonstrated.

02

Culture is dismissed as a cause and then used as one

Chapter 5 argues that culture is endogenous to politics and cannot be a fundamental cause. Chapter 9's central finding is that the Estado Novo's schooling succeeded because it aligned with the country's culture, and chapter 10's second pillar is "cultural conditioning of political origin". The conditioning is always said to be downstream of politics — but that is a claim about direction, not a demonstration of it.

03

The two resource curses are not really symmetric

Gold ran at 4–7% of GDP with essentially no conditionality into a state with no parliament. EU funds run at 2–3% into a parliamentary democracy with external audit and a body of empirical evaluation. Palma acknowledges the difference and then argues past it. The strongest part of his case here is the political-accountability mechanism; the weakest is the Dutch-disease analogy.

04

Eastern Europe is the live test, and it is unfinished

His own explanation for why the East converged despite the funds — that communism inoculated it against statism — is cultural, and therefore sits awkwardly against the argument above. He also allows in a footnote that the funds may yet be doing net damage there too, insufficiently to halt convergence for now. That is an unfalsifiable position until it isn't.

05

The Estado Novo chapter is fenced, but the fence is not the argument

Palma says plainly that dictatorship is condemnable regardless of results, and that a democracy from 1945 might have invested less in war and more in schools. But the chapter's structure — the good news carefully quantified, the counterfactual left "highly speculative" — inevitably reads as more favourable than the sentences of condemnation, and he knows it will be quoted that way.

06

Nobody is to blame, which is convenient

He declines to assign responsibility to any current party on the grounds that party behaviour is endogenous, and doubts a different set of leaders would produce a different present. That is an intellectually consistent position and also one that cannot be tested — and which, taken seriously, leaves no domestic actor with any reason to try.

What to take from it
  1. Timing is the whole argument.Almost every conventional explanation of Portuguese decline fails on chronology alone. Build the annual series first, then see which candidate causes actually sit on the right side of the turning point — a method that generalises far beyond Portugal.
  2. Windfalls remove the constraint that makes states negotiate.The Crown stopped summoning the Cortes because it no longer needed their money. That is the resource curse's real bite: not inflation and an appreciating exchange rate, but the quiet disappearance of the reason to bargain.
  3. Destroying an institution is instant; rebuilding one takes centuries.Twenty thousand pupils in 1759; not twenty thousand again until the 1930s, in a country three times larger. The single decision to expel the Jesuits without a replacement is, on Palma's accounting, still visible in Portuguese education today.
  4. De jure is not de facto, and Portuguese history is mostly the gap between them.Compulsory schooling in 1835 and 1844; the 1923 Estatuto; Pombal's education reform; the constitution that promises more rights than almost any other in the world. The recurring national failure is not bad intentions but unenforced ones.
  5. Reform that fights the culture it governs will lose.The First Republic built schools and the children did not come. The dictatorship separated the sexes, put the crucifixes back, and the same families sent the same children. Palma draws no moral from this beyond the practical one, which is the uncomfortable part.
  6. Levels and trends are different questions.Portugal in 1974 had the worst education in Western Europe and had been improving faster than ever before. Confusing the two lets any regime take credit for a stock it inherited or assign blame for one it improved.
  7. The proximate causes everyone debates are symptoms.Taxes, productivity, bureaucracy, the courts, "bad politicians". All true, all superficial. The question worth asking is why a country that can name its problems generates no demand to solve them.
  8. Every regime for three centuries has blamed its predecessor.Pombal the Jesuits, the liberals absolutism, the republicans the monarchy, the Estado Novo the Republic, the present regime the Estado Novo. Recognising the pattern is the book's most durable contribution, and it is not a claim about Portugal alone.
Practical notes for a reader
  • There is no English edition. The book is in Portuguese, 404 pages, published by Dom Quixote in 2023. The underlying research is almost all in English, however, and is where to go for the evidence.
  • Start with chapters 3 and 6. The GDP series and the golden curse are the load-bearing walls; everything else either supports them or follows from them.
  • Read chapter 9 last and slowly. It is the one designed to be misquoted, and the fencing around it is part of the argument rather than decoration.
  • The primary sources for the numbers are Palma & Reis, "From Convergence to Divergence: Portuguese Economic Growth, 1527–1850" (Journal of Economic History, 2019); Henriques & Palma, "Comparative European Institutions and the Little Divergence, 1385–1800" (Journal of Economic Growth, 2023); Kedrosky & Palma on gold and de-industrialisation; Palma & Reis on literacy under the two regimes (2021); and Cermeño, Palma & Pistola on health and nutrition (2023).
  • Read alongside Acemoglu & Robinson's Why Nations Fail, which chapter 2 is largely written against, and Pedro Lains or Luciano Amaral for the twentieth-century economic history Palma builds on rather than disputes.
