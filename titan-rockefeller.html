Titan

The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr.

“All my future seemed to hinge on that day, and I often tremble when I ask myself the question: what if I had not got the job?”

Rockefeller, remembering 26 September 1855

Ron Chernow · 1998

The whole book in one breath

A sixteen-year-old bookkeeper who tithed from his first wage became, in seventeen years, the master of nine-tenths of American oil refining, by consolidation, secret railroad deals and a management machine nobody else had. The state spent thirty years trying to break what he built and finally did in 1911, which roughly tripled his fortune. He then gave away more money than anyone before him, through a new kind of institution he invented for the purpose. Chernow’s argument is that the saint and the monopolist were not a mask and a face. The same patience, secrecy and certainty of being right produced both, and the book refuses to strike a balance between them because Rockefeller never felt the need to.

Foreword · the book itself

The most written-about businessman in America, and the least known

Chernow says that early in his research he realised he was confronting a sphinx. Rockefeller trained himself to reveal as little as possible, gave almost no interviews, testified evasively, and left a public image made half by his enemies and half by his publicists. Two traditions had hardened: the muckrakers’ monster, fixed by Ida Tarbell in 1904, and the misunderstood organiser of Allan Nevins’s authorised biographies of 1940 and 1953, which strove to vindicate him.

What Chernow had that others did not

  • The Inglis interviews. Between 1917 and 1920, for an hour each morning, Rockefeller answered questions from William O. Inglis, a New York journalist hired to prepare an authorised account. Inglis read him Tarbell’s charges line by line and wrote down the replies. The transcript was never published; it is the nearest thing to a Rockefeller confession, and it had barely been used.
  • The family papers. The Rockefeller Archive Center at Sleepy Hollow, New York, holds the papers of Rockefeller, his son, Frederick Gates and others, including tens of thousands of pages of letters, many only recently opened.
  • The other side’s files. Tarbell’s papers at the Drake Well Museum in Titusville, Henry Demarest Lloyd’s at the State Historical Society of Wisconsin, and Nevins’s at Columbia.
  • Rockefeller wrote in a guarded, elliptical style, so the letters people wrote to him often say more than his own. Chernow reads both, and reads the silences.

What kind of book it is

  • Three strands, braided. The business story runs from Cleveland in 1858 to the Supreme Court in 1911. The family story runs alongside it and is often the more disturbing. The giving starts as a boy’s habit and ends as the invention of the modern foundation.
  • A biography, not a business history. Oil technology and the producers are sketched rather than analysed, and Chernow states the case that Standard cut prices and rationalised a chaotic industry, and the case that it did so by coercive and secret means, without the economist’s apparatus to settle it. Its centre is character.
  • Its thesis is that the saint and the monopolist were not a mask and a face. The same temperament, patient, secretive and certain of being right, produced both.
  • Structure. A foreword, a prelude and thirty-five chapters in roughly chronological order, each with a short, pointed title. The summary below follows them one by one.

Prelude

Poison Tongue

The book opens not with a birth but with an old man answering back, in private, to the charges he never answered in public.

  • The scene is the Inglis interviews of 1917–20. Rockefeller, near eighty, is lean and fit, his voice ranging from the breathy pitch of age to the mellow baritone of his early adulthood. He would stretch out on a lounge and seem to doze while Inglis read a passage aloud, then correct a detail.
  • On the rivals he bought out in 1872 he is unrepentant and self-pitying at once. They were all, he says, in a sinking ship if cut-throat competition continued; he had rescued them, generously, and they repaid him with lies. He peppers his talk with references to his own forgiving nature.
  • On Ida Tarbell he loses his composure. He railed against “the poison tongue of this poison woman”, who, he said, sought to poison the public mind. The title comes from that outburst.
  • Chernow uses the scene to state the puzzle of the book. The man who could not see anything to apologise for was also the most generous giver of his age, and the pious, gentle grandfather of the dimes was the same man who designed the drawback. The chapters that follow try to explain how.

If you remember nothing else

Twelve things to keep

Character

Two parents, two halves

His mother gave him the discipline and the faith; his father gave him the poker face and the habit of concealment. They never merged.

Strategy

Stay out of the lottery

Drilling was boom and bust. He took the narrow, capital-hungry middle of the chain, refining, where scale and freight decided everything.

Cost

Own every input, waste nothing

Barrels, tank cars, acid, pipe and every by-product in the barrel. A rival could not see Standard’s true cost from his own books.

Leverage

Volume is a weapon

A shipper with enough traffic is a counterparty, not a customer. Cleveland’s three routes let him auction his trainloads.

The line he crossed

The drawback

A rebate is a volume discount. A payment on every barrel a rival shipped is not defensible on any theory, and it turned opinion for good.

Consolidation

Buy the losers, keep the best

He called it cooperation. Most of Standard’s senior men arrived as competitors who were bought, not beaten.

Secrecy

Hidden ownership poisoned everything

Firms kept their old names while Standard owned them. Concealment, more than rebates, made a strategy look like a conspiracy.

Organisation

He invented modern management

Committees, comparable cost statistics and decision by consensus at 26 Broadway, long before most American firms.

Reputation

Silence was the fatal error

Thirty years of never answering handed the whole public record to his enemies.

The market

The monopoly was eroding anyway

Texas, California, Russia, Sumatra and the electric light cut Standard’s share before any court did.

1911

The break-up made him richer

Thirty-four transparent companies were worth far more to the market than one opaque one.

Giving

Wholesale, not retail

Fund the institution that removes the cause, hire the best expert, and leave them alone: the model of the modern foundation.

Who’s who

Cast of characters

Chernow keeps about two dozen people in play. These carry most of the action. Notice the middle group: nearly every senior Standard man arrived as a rival who was bought rather than beaten.

Inside the family

Father

William Avery Rockefeller

“Big Bill”, the flimflam man. Pedlar of patent cures, absent for months, from 1855 a bigamist with a second wife, Margaret Allen, living as Dr William Levingston. Taught his son to bargain hard and trust no one.

Mother

Eliza Davison Rockefeller

Devout, thrifty, severe, and often left alone with the children. The source of the discipline, the faith and the tolerance for silence.

Wife

Laura “Cettie” Spelman

Married 1864. From an abolitionist family, and more pious than he was. His closest counsel for decades.

Brother

William Rockefeller

Standard’s New York and export man. Sociable where John was not, and drawn into the Amalgamated Copper stock scheme.

Brother

Frank Rockefeller

Sold out, felt cheated, and feuded for life; he moved his two dead children from the family plot rather than have them near John.

Son

John D. Rockefeller, Jr.

The only son. Scrupulous to the point of illness, and spent his life turning the fortune into institutions and the name into respectability.

Joined 1867. The negotiator behind the railroad rebate strategy; later built Florida’s east-coast railway and Palm Beach.

Partner

The silent money behind Flagler, whose investment in 1867 made the expansion possible.

Rival to ally

The life at a glance

Ninety-seven years, seventeen of them decisive

Everything that made Standard Oil unbeatable happened between 1865 and 1882, when he was 25 to 42. He then spent fifty-five more years as its owner, its defendant and its donor.

Chapter 1 · pages 3–14

The Flimflam Man

The father first, because Chernow thinks you cannot understand the son without him: a big, charming con man whose life was a sequence of disguises, and whose son organised his own life as a refutation of him while keeping several of his gifts.

The family behind him

  • The Rockefellers came from Sagendorf, near Koblenz in the Rhineland. Around 1723 Johann Peter Rockefeller, a miller, brought his family to America; John D. later put up a monument to him at his grave in Flemington, New Jersey.
  • The grandfather, Godfrey Rockefeller, married Lucy Avery in Great Barrington, Massachusetts, in 1806. She was a former schoolteacher, better educated than her husband, and claimed descent from the English king Edmund Ironside. Godfrey was, in Chernow’s telling, sadly mismatched with her: amiable, feckless, and at one point talked into swapping his farm for supposedly richer land in Tioga County, New York.
  • The family drifted into the hill country of the southern tier of New York. Richford, where John was born, was then a stagecoach stop in wooded country, and the place where the family lived would forever bear the melancholy name of Michigan Hill.

Big Bill

  • William Avery Rockefeller (1810–1906), “Big Bill”, was tall and broad-chested with a thick auburn beard, a crack shot who made the circuit of shooting contests, and a man who never worked with his hands if he could help it. He ran a small sawmill on Michigan Hill and dealt in salt, furs, horses and timber, but his real trade was selling.
  • He met Eliza Davison at her father’s farm near Niles and Moravia while posing as a deaf-mute pedlar of cheap novelties, a disguise he kept up until, at a social gathering in Richford, a neighbour who had seen him “deaf” heard him talking and marvelled at his cure. He smiled and said he could talk better than when she saw him last.
  • Eliza’s father, John Davison, a prosperous Baptist farmer with 150 acres in Cayuga County, opposed the match. They married anyway on 18 February 1837.
  • Big Bill did not give up his girlfriend, Nancy Brown. He brought her into the cramped house as a “housekeeper” and fathered children by both women in alternation: Eliza’s daughter Lucy in 1838, Nancy’s daughter Clorinda, then John on 8 July 1839, and several months later Nancy’s second daughter, Cornelia. Eliza, against expectation, pitied Nancy rather than hating her. Clorinda died young; Cornelia grew up.
  • Rumours followed him about the district that he was a gambler, a horse thief, a desperado. Chernow notes that no evidence ties him to the horse-thieving gang of local legend, and that Nevins, after much examination, rejected the story.
  • The more serious charge is documented. On 26 July 1849, according to papers at the Auburn Court House, Bill was indicted for assaulting a young woman who worked for the family, Anne Vanderbeak, on 1 May 1848. He fled the county; the case lapsed, and nobody knows why.

Dr William Levingston

  • In the 1850s Bill reinvented himself as a travelling herbal doctor, “Doc Rockefeller”, selling remedies he partly believed in. On the road near Owego he met a gentle teenager from Norwich, Ontario, Margaret Allen; he was forty-two.
  • On 12 June 1855, still married to Eliza, he married Margaret Allen in Nichols, New York, just south of Owego, and started a secret second household, in Philadelphia from the late 1850s and from 1875 in Freeport, Illinois, where the neighbours knew him as Dr William Levingston. He lived both lives, returning to Eliza’s household at longer and longer intervals, for half a century.
  • The second family stayed a secret for decades; it came out only when newspapers went hunting for the old man in the 1900s (Chapter 26).

What the son took from him

  • Bill traded with his boys and cheated them when he could, and said so: “I want to make ’em sharp.” He taught John to bargain, to keep a straight face and to hold his cards close.
  • Chernow’s reading, which the whole book develops: John organised his life as a refutation of his father (no drink, no gambling, no show, one wife, every cent recorded), yet the poker face, the concealed arrangements and the ease with ruthlessness in business were his father’s gifts dressed in his mother’s morality.

Chapter 2 · pages 15–36

Fires of Revival

The mother’s half of the inheritance: a Baptist household on the shores of Owasco Lake, in the region revivalism had set alight, where a boy learned thrift, silence, self-command and the moral bookkeeping that would last him ninety-seven years.

Moravia and the lake

  • Around 1843 Bill paid a thousand dollars for a 92-acre farm near Moravia, thirty miles north of Richford, on grassy upland sloping down to Owasco Lake, one of the Finger Lakes, three miles from the Davison farm, where Eliza wanted to be.
  • Moravia was a step up from the frontier: already a stronghold of temperance and anti-slavery feeling. Bill, improbably, stocked the lake with pickerel and headed the local temperance society.
  • With Bill away for months at a time, Eliza was often alone with the children. The boy took on responsibility early, as the man of the house.

The burned-over district

  • Western New York and Ohio were known as the Burned-Over District, for the fires of religious revival that swept them again and again in the 1820s–40s; Charles Grandison Finney was the most celebrated of the revivalists.
  • Chernow stresses his subject’s evangelical Baptist faith as the passkey to many of his mysteries. The Baptists’ autonomy of individual congregations, their suspicion of church hierarchy, their populist spirit and their demotion of sacraments suited a practical, rational, self-reliant temperament; salvation was a matter of personal conduct, and conduct could be recorded.
  • Eliza’s Christianity was stern and practical. Her maxims (“willful waste makes woeful want” is the one her son quoted all his life) were rules of household economy as much as of faith.

The mother’s discipline

  • She punished with a birch switch, strapping the boy to the apple tree. Once, sent to cut a willow switch, he nicked it in several places so that it would break at the first blow; she sent him back for another, “and see that it is not slashed”. The quiet cunning, Chernow notes, would become a pronounced trait.
  • He took her lessons in self-control to heart: to keep his temper, say little, and never let anyone see what he wanted.
  • Music came from her side too; he loved hymns all his life.

The first ledger habits

  • He raised turkeys as a boy, fed on milk curds his mother gave him, sold them, and kept the money in a blue china bowl on the mantel. Eighty years later he still enjoyed studying a flock of turkeys.
  • He lent a farmer $50 at 7% interest and collected $3.50 at the end of the year, without lifting a finger, while three days digging potatoes for a farmer had earned him 37½ cents a day. The lesson, as he told it: let money work for you.
  • Already he was a saver and a giver at once: small sums to the church came out of the turkey money.

Owego

  • After Bill’s indictment in 1849 the family moved south to Owego, a lively town on a broad bend of the Susquehanna, decidedly more cosmopolitan than anything they had known.
  • In August 1852 John and his brother William entered Owego Academy, founded in 1827 and among the best schools in the region. He was a plodding, lacklustre student, with no sign yet of anything out of the ordinary.
  • By then Bill’s absences had become a second life (Chapter 1), and Chernow sees the teenager’s gravity, his secretiveness and his precocious sense of responsibility for his mother and siblings as the product of that half-absent, half-dazzling father.

Chapter 3 · pages 37–62

Bound to Be Rich

Cleveland, school, a few months of bookkeeping, and six weeks of walking the streets: how a sixteen-year-old with no connections talked his way into the one job he wanted.

Ohio

  • In 1853 Bill moved the family by train to Strongsville, a prairie town about a dozen miles south-west of Cleveland, and then to a ten-acre farm by a creek in Parma. He returned only three or four times in the first year. One day a Strongsville resident, Joe Webster, checked into a hotel on the road and found Bill there under another name, and word got round.
  • John and William were settled as boarders with a Mrs Woodin on Erie Street in Cleveland, at a dollar a week each, to go to school. Talk at her table ran over many topics; she was violently opposed, Rockefeller remembered, to lenders charging high interest.

School and a trade

  • He finally entered high school (later Central High School) in 1854, at fifteen. A close friend there was Mark Hanna, later the Republican boss who managed McKinley’s campaign. He also befriended two bright sisters, Lucy and Laura Celestia Spelman; he took a special fancy to Laura.
  • Classmates remembered him as sober and reserved, and one described him as sane in every respect save one: he was money mad.
  • Instead of finishing high school he took a three-month course at E. G. Folsom’s Commercial College, one of a chain with branches in seven cities, learning double-entry bookkeeping, penmanship and the principles of banking and exchange.
  • He told a businessman one day, it was said, that he was “bound to be rich”, and repeated it, louder each time.

The six-week search

  • In the heat of the summer of 1855 he made a list of Cleveland’s most substantial firms (railways, banks, wholesalers) and called on them one after another, day after day, six days a week, dressed in a dark suit. Rejected, he started again at the top of the list, some firms three times.
  • On the morning of 26 September 1855 he walked into the offices of Hewitt and Tuttle, commission merchants and produce shippers on the waterfront, and was taken on as an assistant bookkeeper once his handwriting had been scrutinised and he was told they would give him a chance. Pay was not discussed.
  • For the rest of his life he honoured 26 September as “Job Day” and celebrated it with more genuine enthusiasm than his birthday. In old age he said that he often trembled to think what would have happened if he had not got the job.

Chapter 4 · pages 63–74

Baptism in Business

An apprenticeship on the Cleveland waterfront, a red notebook bought for a dime, and a church that became his second school: by nineteen he was a partner, a giver and a man who checked every bill twice.

The clerk

  • He served, in effect, an unpaid apprenticeship. It was three months before Hewitt handed him his first, retroactive pay: $50 for the quarter, slightly more than 50 cents a day.
  • From his high stool, bent over the ledgers, he could see the ships on the Cuyahoga and the lake. He loved the work as both a technical and a moral discipline, and treated the books as the truth about a business: they told you when you were making money and when you were losing it.
  • Given the job of paying the bills, he checked each one line by line, catching errors that others waved through, and chased debts patiently and politely until they were paid.
  • When Tuttle quit in January 1857, Rockefeller, seventeen, was elevated to chief bookkeeper. Hewitt raised his pay to $600 a year by 1858. He wanted $800, and the bickering made him receptive when an opening came.

Ledger A

  • When he started work in September 1855 he paid a dime for a small red book, which he called Ledger A, and recorded in it every cent he received and spent.
  • For the rest of his life he treated it as his most sacred relic, kept in a safety-deposit vault like a priceless heirloom and brought out, with visible emotion, to show visitors.
  • Chernow thinks it deserves the reverence, because it settles one question: whether his giving began as a way of cleansing a “tainted” fortune. It did not. In his first year on the job the clerk gave about 6% of his wages to charity, some weeks much more: to his church, to missions, to the poor, and in 1859 to a black man in Cincinnati trying to buy his wife out of slavery.

The church

  • He found a spiritual home in the Erie Street Baptist Mission Church, a poor congregation founded in the aftermath of the revivals of the 1850s. He liked its egalitarian atmosphere, and it gave him responsibility early: he taught Sunday school, served as clerk and later as trustee.
  • When the church fell behind on interest on a $2,000 mortgage held by a deacon, the teenager campaigned among the congregation for pledges, week after week, until the debt was paid. Chernow sees in it the same preternatural confidence he would bring to business.
  • He would be born again in business, as Chernow puts it, much as he had been born again in the Erie Street church.

Clark and Rockefeller, 1858

  • Maurice B. Clark, a 28-year-old Englishman who had started out as a gardener in Wiltshire and worked down the street at another produce firm, proposed a partnership in which each would put up $2,000.
  • Rockefeller had saved about $800. He asked his father for the rest. Bill had always promised his children $1,000 at twenty-one; he advanced it early, at 10% interest, above the going rate, and would sometimes demand it back at awkward moments to test his son.
  • So on 1 April 1858, aged eighteen, he left Isaac Hewitt and became a partner in Clark and Rockefeller, commission merchants, at 32 River Street. The firm sold farm produce on commission and, to win business, offered shippers generous advances.
  • He was the office man: meticulous, persistent and good with bankers, and long before he was twenty-one, he recalled, men called him “Mr Rockefeller”. The firm made money from the start and soon occupied four numbers on River Street.
  • The Civil War made it prosperous. Food and supplies for the Union armies sent prices up, and by 1862 annual profits were $17,000, almost four times the first year’s.
  • He did not fight. Like many prosperous Northerners he legally paid for a substitute, and he gave about $300 a year to substitutes and their families on top of his other giving. He and Clark followed the war on large maps tacked to the office wall. Though only twenty-one when the war began, he was in effect supporting his mother and siblings; Chernow still finds his later explanations a little too smooth.

Chapter 5 · pages 75–96

The Auction

Oil, a refinery on a muddy creek, a quarrel with his partners settled by the strangest sale in Cleveland’s history, and a marriage: 1859 to 1865, the years that made him an oilman at twenty-five.

Rock oil

  • Oil had always oozed from springs into Oil Creek in western Pennsylvania (the name dates from the eighteenth century), and was skimmed off and sold as medicine; Big Bill himself peddled such cures.
  • The Pennsylvania Rock-Oil Company leased land along the creek and sent Edwin Drake, an unemployed railway conductor given the courtesy title of “Colonel”, to drill for it. In August 1859 he struck oil near Titusville, then an idyllic valley of dense pine forest.
  • The rush that followed was chaos: derricks, mud, gamblers and boomtowns. The paradigm was Pithole Creek, where oil came in January 1865: a town sprang up almost overnight, and within a few years it had collapsed so completely that it counted just six voters. An observant eight-year-old girl watched it all: Ida Tarbell.
  • Kerosene from refined crude was the new light of the age, cheaper and brighter than whale oil or candles. The secret of cleansing oil with sulphuric acid, which we call refining, was then a high mystery.

Andrews, Clark and Co.

  • It was the friendship between Maurice Clark and Samuel Andrews, an Englishman from Clark’s home town in Wiltshire who knew the chemistry of refining, that drew Rockefeller in. He knew Andrews and his wife from the Erie Street church.
  • In 1863 Rockefeller and Clark pledged $4,000 for half the working capital of a new refining venture, Andrews, Clark and Co., with the 24-year-old Rockefeller as its financial man. They built the Excelsior Works on the red-clay banks of Kingsbury Run, which flowed into the Cuyahoga and so to the lake, beside the new railway line.
  • Andrews ran the plant; Rockefeller ran everything else. He was often seen at the works at 6:30 in the morning, in the cooper’s shop rolling out barrels, stacking hoops or carting away shavings.
  • Soon a string of other refineries had sprouted along Kingsbury Run, and Cleveland, with about 44,000 people, was becoming a refining centre.

The quarrel with the Clarks

  • Maurice Clark brought in his brothers, James and Richard. James, an ex-prizefighter, was a powerful, bullying young man who tried to intimidate Rockefeller; he found he could not, and Rockefeller came to detest him.
  • The real disagreement was about speed. Rockefeller wanted to borrow and expand; Maurice Clark was very angry when he borrowed money to extend the business and threatened to dissolve the partnership whenever he did it again.
  • Rockefeller made sure the next threat would be taken at its word. Playing into his hands, the Clarks proposed to split up, and the partners agreed to sell the refinery to whichever side bid higher.

The auction, February 1865

  • They held the auction among themselves, with the Clarks’ lawyer as auctioneer. The bidding began at $500, advanced to $60,000 and by slow stages to $70,000. When the Clarks bid $72,000, Rockefeller said at once, “$72,500”. Clark said he would go no higher: the business was his.
  • Rockefeller kept the refinery and gave up his interest in the commission business. Chernow reckons the price at about $650,000 in the money of the 1990s.
  • He was twenty-five. He later called it the day that determined his career. The new firm of Rockefeller and Andrews was already the largest refinery in Cleveland.

Cettie

  • Laura Celestia Spelman, always called Cettie, came from a family of Massachusetts Puritan stock. Her father, Harvey Buel Spelman, a direct descendant of the Puritans, was a man of burning fundamentalist convictions; her mother, Lucy Henry Spelman, was dignified and industrious and loved singing hymns. They were staunch abolitionists and temperance crusaders, and their home had been a station on the Underground Railroad.
  • Cettie and her sister Lucy had spent a year at the Oread Collegiate Institute in Worcester, Massachusetts, founded by the abolitionist Eli Thayer. She was sensible, cheerful, and even more devout than he was.
  • After a discreet six-month engagement they married on 8 September 1864, hard on the heels of Sherman’s march into Atlanta. The honeymoon ran a month, to 8 October, by way of Niagara Falls (where he peppered the guide with questions), Montreal and the White Mountains of New Hampshire; at Oread they met two teachers, Sophia Packard and Harriet Giles, who would later found the school that became Spelman College.
  • They set up house on Cheshire Street. Their first child, Bessie (Elizabeth), was born there in 1866.

Chapter 6 · pages 97–118

The Poetry of the Age

After the Civil War, business was the poetry of the age, and Rockefeller was one of its most single-minded poets: a new partner with a gift for deals, a secret railroad bargain, and the discovery that the cheapest refiner could set the terms for everyone.

The Gilded Age begins

  • Chernow opens with the setting: the years after 1865 were the most exuberant era of American enterprise, with millions of acres of natural resources to exploit, railways crossing the continent and very few rules.
  • For a devout young Baptist it posed no moral problem. Making money honestly and giving it away generously were, in his upbringing, two halves of one duty; as he put it later, he had been taught by the minister as a boy to get all he could honourably and give all he could.
  • Oil in particular was a wild business. Crude prices swung from a few dollars a barrel to ten cents in the gluts, and teamsters still charged $3 or more to haul a barrel over the rough roads to the railway.

William in New York

  • In 1866 he set up his brother William with a second refinery, the Standard Works, and then sent him to New York to run an export office, William Rockefeller and Company, selling kerosene abroad and buying supplies.
  • The combined Excelsior and Standard Works made Rockefeller the leading refiner in Cleveland. William was sociable where John was reserved; the division of labour lasted forty years.

Flagler

  • Henry Morrison Flagler, nine years older than Rockefeller, was the son of a Presbyterian minister, the Reverend Isaac Flagler. A dashing man with roguish good looks, he had made $50,000 in the grain business in Bellevue, Ohio, then lost it in a salt company in Saginaw, Michigan, when prices collapsed, an experience of ruinous competition he never forgot.
  • In Cleveland he met Rockefeller in the grain trade. Rockefeller needed capital and found it through Flagler’s relative by marriage, Stephen V. Harkness, one of the city’s richest men, who invested $100,000, a third of the new firm’s capital, on condition that Flagler be taken in to watch it.
  • In 1867 the partnership of Rockefeller, Andrews and Flagler was formed, with Harkness as a silent partner. Rockefeller and Flagler shared an office and developed a collective letter-writing style, passing drafts back and forth, each making small improvements, until both were satisfied.
  • Flagler had a rare gift for drawing up contracts. Rockefeller loved his dictum that a friendship founded on business was better than a business founded on friendship.

The Lake Shore deal

  • Cleveland was, on paper, a poor place to refine: crude had to be brought 100 miles or more from the Pennsylvania wells, at about 50 cents a barrel, before refined oil was shipped back east. Its advantage was choice. It had two trunk railway routes to New York and, in summer, the lake and the Erie Canal.
  • In 1867–68 Flagler approached General J. H. Devereux, the newly installed vice-president of the Lake Shore Railroad, part of Vanderbilt’s New York Central system. The plum he dangled was a promise of sixty carloads of refined oil every day.
  • Guaranteed volume let the railway run solid oil trains instead of assembling mixed freight, cutting its costs sharply, and in return it granted Rockefeller’s firm a large secret discount off the published rate. Their dealings rested on oral agreements that were never put on paper.
  • Rockefeller lacked the volume to fill sixty cars a day on his own; the promise itself pushed him to grow. Chernow calls the deal a turning point: from here on, freight rates, not refining skill, decided who survived.
  • Rockefeller always defended rebates as volume discounts that any big shipper would get, and asked whether a railway should charge the same to those who gave it 5,000 barrels a day, 500, or 50.

Economies of scale

  • Faced with a chronic shortage of barrels, and having paid small coopers up to $2.50 each for white-oak barrels, he built his own cooperage, bought timber tracts, dried the staves in kilns before hauling them, and cut the cost of a barrel to a fraction.
  • He bought his own tank cars, warehouses and boats, made his own sulphuric acid and recovered the spent acid, and hired his own plumbers by the month rather than pay a plumber’s bill.
  • At first the business was kerosene and naphtha; by the mid-1870s Standard also sold benzine, paraffin wax (for candles and chewing gum), petroleum jelly, lubricants, tar and asphalt, from residues that others poured into the rivers. By-products made his true cost of kerosene lower than any rival could calculate.
  • On 10 January 1870 the partnership was replaced by a joint-stock company, the Standard Oil Company of Ohio, with $1 million of capital, about $11 million in 1990s money. The name echoed the Standard Works and promised a uniform, standard kerosene when impurities still made lamps explode. Rockefeller was president, William vice-president and Flagler secretary and treasurer; Rockefeller held the largest block of stock.
  • It already had about a tenth of American refining capacity. Andrews ran production, Flagler the railways, William exports, and Rockefeller everything.

Millionaires’ Row

A rich young man on Cleveland’s grandest street who lived as if he were not rich: the household, the church and the religious bookkeeping of the years around 1870.

Euclid Avenue

  • In 1868 the family moved from Cheshire Street to a solid brick house at 424 Euclid Avenue. The avenue, lined with elms and mansions, had richly earned its nickname of “millionaires’ row”.
  • The move dramatised how far he had come from Richford, but the house was plain by the standards of the avenue, and they lived frugally in it. They kept it in constant repair long after they had left and never renounced a sentimental attachment to it.
  • The children came in these years: Bessie (Elizabeth) in 1866; Alice in July 1869, who died a year later; Alta in 1871; Edith in 1872; and in 1874 the only son, John D. Rockefeller, Jr.

The church moves up the street

  • Soon after the Rockefellers moved, the Erie Street congregation followed them and rebuilt itself as the Euclid Avenue Baptist Church. Even in its fancier incarnation its membership stayed plebeian, and he liked it that way.
  • He served for decades as superintendent of the Sunday school, until 1905, and as a trustee, and he and Cettie remained members even after they moved to New York.
  • Cettie’s charities were of a piece with his: temperance, missions, and practical help such as a free medical dispensary, staffed entirely by women doctors, for poor women.

Getting and giving

  • Chernow pauses here on the question the rest of the book keeps asking: how a man so devout could be so ruthless. His answer begins with the faith itself. To this evangelical Baptist, making money was a religious duty (“to get all I could honorably and to give all I could”), and prosperity was, if anything, a sign of stewardship faithfully performed.
  • The chapter draws on Max Weber’s The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, and on the idea of a spiritual double-entry bookkeeping: good works and gifts entered against the day’s business, the books always balanced.
  • He neither drank nor smoked, and kept the Sabbath strictly, boycotting shops that opened on Sundays. The self-command his mother had drilled into him became a way of doing business: patience, silence, never showing excitement at a good deal or dismay at a bad one.

A glut and a remedy

  • By 1871 refining capacity far exceeded demand, prices had collapsed and most refiners were losing money. Rockefeller’s lifelong conclusion was that competition this ruinous could not cure itself; the industry needed combination.
  • The instrument came from Tom Scott, the overlord of the Pennsylvania Railroad, through Peter H. Watson, an official of the Lake Shore. Scott proposed an alliance between the three great trunk railways (the Pennsylvania, the New York Central and the Erie) and a small group of refiners.
  • The vehicle was a dormant Pennsylvania charter with the blandly misleading name of the South Improvement Company, one of many bought from a venal legislature after the war.

How it would have worked

  • The railways would raise freight rates for all refiners, but members would get rebates of up to 50% on crude and refined shipments.
  • In the most deadly innovation, members would also receive drawbacks: a payment on every barrel shipped by a rival refiner.
  • And the railways would hand members the waybills of their rivals’ shipments: who shipped what, how much, and to whom.
  • In return the refiners would divide the oil traffic among the railways in agreed proportions, ending the railways’ own rate wars.
  • Of the 2,000 shares, Rockefeller’s group held 900. Watson was president.

The explosion

  • The scheme leaked in February 1872, when the new rates were posted before the contracts took effect. The oil regions erupted in mass meetings and torchlight parades. Producers formed the Petroleum Producers’ Union and embargoed crude to South Improvement members, Standard above all.
  • One of the angry producers was Franklin Tarbell, who built wooden oil tanks and drilled a little; his daughter Ida, a flabbergasted fourteen-year-old, watched it all. She would remember the crisis for thirty years.
  • The railways retreated and the scheme was abandoned that spring; Pennsylvania revoked the charter. It never moved a barrel. But it left, as Tarbell later wrote, a new name to hate: that of the Standard Oil Company replacing the South Improvement Company in popular contempt.

The Cleveland Massacre

  • While the fight raged he visited his Cleveland rivals one by one. The first and most important was Clark, Payne and Co., the city’s largest refiner; Payne consented to a $400,000 price, took Standard stock, and became Standard’s treasurer and one of its richest men.
  • To the others he showed Standard’s books, offered an appraisal in cash or stock, and pointed out that he could survive what was coming and they could not. By the time the scheme was scuttled, in a few weeks that historians came to call the Cleveland Massacre, he had absorbed 22 of his 26 Cleveland competitors.
  • Rockefeller told Inglis, with patently religious imagery, that the Standard had been an angel of mercy reaching down from the sky: get into the ark, put in your old junk, we will take all the risks. Chernow notes that the timing of the takeovers strongly suggests the South Improvement threat was the lever that made them sell.
  • Some sellers felt robbed. Robert Hanna, Mark Hanna’s uncle, summoned about his firm Hanna, Baslington and Co., testified that he had been told that if they did not sell out they would be “crushed out”; the takeover ended in a bitter lawsuit. Most of those who took cash rather than stock spent the rest of their lives telling the story, and the ones who took stock became rich and loyal.
  • Standard, now about a quarter of the whole industry, dominated the country’s largest refining centre. He was thirty-two.

Chapter 9 · pages 157–172

The New Monarch

Having taken Cleveland, he tried to organise the whole industry by agreement, failed, and then did it by quiet conquest: the strongest refiners of Pittsburgh, Philadelphia and New York joined him in secret.

The Pittsburgh Plan

  • Late in 1872 he carried to Titusville a plan for a National Refiners’ Association, popularly dubbed the Pittsburgh Plan: a cartel of refiners that would allot output and hold up prices. It theoretically embraced all comers, and he was its president.
  • The producers organised in turn, and the two associations signed what was called the Treaty of Titusville, at a hotel in New York: refiners would buy crude only from the producers’ association, at an agreed price, and producers would sell only to association refiners.
  • It fell apart almost at once. Producers cheated by drilling more and selling outside the agreement, and refiners cheated on quotas. The lesson Rockefeller drew was that voluntary cartels leak: members take the benefits and dodge the discipline. On 24 June 1873, at Saratoga Springs, he gathered the refiners and dissolved the short-lived association.
  • What would work, he concluded, was ownership. Instead of asking rivals to cooperate, he would bring them inside one company.

Depression as opportunity

  • The Panic of 1873 opened six years of depression. Standard kept its credit, paid its dividends and bought, while weaker firms failed.
  • In April 1874 he struck a clever bargain with the Erie Railroad, which turned over control of its oil terminal at Weehawken, on the Hudson opposite Manhattan, to Standard; in return Standard shipped a share of its traffic by the Erie. The biggest shipper was becoming a partner of the carriers.

The secret alliance of 1874–75

  • That summer Rockefeller and Flagler met their Pittsburgh and Philadelphia counterparts, Charles Lockhart and William G. Warden, at a secret summit in Saratoga Springs and made the case: join Standard, share its advantages in freight and finance, and end a war all of them were losing.
  • They agreed. Rockefeller was especially delighted to snare Lockhart, a bearded Scot with a frosty, taciturn manner, the leading refiner of Pittsburgh; Warden was one of the three foremost refiners of Philadelphia.
  • In New York he took over Charles Pratt and Company. Pratt, a short, self-made man with a sandy beard, had made his high-quality kerosene, Astral Oil, a household word. With him came Henry H. Rogers, who in 1872 had led the New York refiners against the South Improvement scheme and would become one of Standard’s most energetic and swaggering figures.
  • In the oil regions Standard bought a third of Vandergrift and Forman, controlled by Captain Jacob J. Vandergrift, a husky former steamboat captain with a Santa Claus beard, and its pipelines; in West Virginia it bought J. N. Camden and Company of Parkersburg and renamed it Camden Consolidated Oil. Camden was also a West Virginia politician, useful in Washington.
  • In the oil regions too a young, hard-drinking Titusville refiner who had fought the 1872 scheme, John D. Archbold, was recruited; from 1875 he ran the Acme Oil Company, a front organisation that bought up local refineries.

Secrecy as a method

  • All of this was hidden. Acquired firms kept their names, letterheads and apparent owners, while Standard held the stock through trustees. Customers who thought they were buying from an independent were not.
  • Agents who watched rivals’ shipments mailed their reports to Box 164 at the Cleveland post office. Standard’s reputation as a pervasive, all-seeing presence began here.
  • Chernow sees the secrecy, more than any single rebate, as what poisoned the company’s name: when the facts came out, a business strategy looked like a conspiracy, because it had been run as one.

Chapter 10 · pages 173–182

Sphinx

A portrait of the chief executive in his mid-thirties: the silent, courteous, relentlessly controlled manager whose style became Standard Oil’s culture.

The office

  • Standard had begun in a modest suite of offices in the four-storey Cushing Block on Cleveland’s Public Square. In April 1874, as befitted an oil colossus, it moved into a new four-storey building that Rockefeller and Harkness had put up.
  • He arrived at the stroke of nine, gliding in with a kind of spectral stealth, as if on a cushion of air, and worked through the day at an even pace, with a snack of crackers and milk and a nap after lunch, habits designed to conserve energy and help him last.
  • He listened far more than he talked, and weighed every word he did say.

How he managed

  • To orchestrate so large an operation he had to delegate, and part of the Standard Oil gospel was to find able men, give them responsibility and not interfere. He worked by subtle hints, nudging people along, and doled out praise sparingly. His highest compliment for a taciturn colleague was that he was the kind of man he would like to go fishing with.
  • He never raised his voice or lost his temper in public; the style of understated authority was his mother’s, who never scolded anyone.
  • He inspired loyalty among subordinates and raw terror among adversaries.
  • He wrote in a guarded, elliptical style and, where he could, left important agreements unwritten, as with the Lake Shore.

The man behind the mask

  • Chernow describes a man of great depth and force with eerie self-control. Once or twice in the book his famous granite composure breaks, and he lets forth a torrent of anger; the rarity is the point.
  • He reports, without endorsing it, a psychobiographer’s suggestion that the icy self-control was a reaction to repressed fantasies of murdering his father.
  • The one extravagance he allowed himself was fast horses. He had a passion for trotters, paid $10,000–12,500 for thoroughbreds with names like Midnight, Flash, Jesse, Baron and Trifle, and after work drove his black geldings down Euclid Avenue among the fashionable carriages.
  • Chernow’s title fits the chapter’s argument: the more power he gained, the less anyone could read him, and the more his enemies filled the silence with their own portrait.

Chapter 11 · pages 183–196

The Holy Family

Inside the household: warmth, games and total control, run on the assumption that money was a danger to everyone in it. The children were raised as if they were not rich, and paid, in different ways, for the experiment.

Forest Hill

  • In 1873 Rockefeller bought seventy-nine wooded acres at Forest Hill, on the heights east of Cleveland, crossed by ravines and streams. The big house had been built as a hotel and showed it, with an office by the entrance; the family took time to adjust.
  • It became the summer home and, for decades, the family’s sovereign cure for illness. He laid out roads, planted trees and made a private world he could control, with forest roads for driving and a lake where, on winter days, as many as fifty people skated as his guests.
  • He had a talent for inventing outings: on moonlit nights he took the children on drives and games through the grounds. At home he was playful, balancing fine china plates or crackers on the tip of his nose, and gentle with babies; he would get up from his nap the moment he heard one cry.

Raising children against money

  • The children were not told they were rich. The girls wore simple gingham dresses and hand-me-downs; Junior, the youngest, wore his sisters’ cast-offs, as he confessed sheepishly in later life.
  • They kept careful account books, like their father, and earned pocket money on a published schedule: two cents for killing flies, ten cents for sharpening pencils, five cents an hour for practising their instruments, and payments for mending vases and broken pens.
  • When they clamoured for bicycles, Rockefeller suggested buying one each. Cettie refused: they would buy one and learn to share it.
  • Sunday was a heavily regimented day, beginning with morning prayers and Sunday school and continuing through church and prayer meeting.
  • Cettie, in Chernow’s portrait, was even stricter than her husband, a gentle but unbending evangelical for whom worldliness was sin. Rockefeller supplied the play; she supplied the rules.

The children

  • Bessie (1866), the eldest, was serious and intellectual; in 1889 she married Charles Strong, a prodigious young philosopher and a perfect reasoning machine who had lost his faith, and her health collapsed in her thirties (Chapter 21).
  • Alta (1871) was mischievous and warm; after scarlet fever at eight or nine she suffered terrible headaches; she was desperate to marry and resented Edith’s earlier match.
  • Edith (1872) seemed the family changeling: where the others were submissive, she was recalcitrant, strong-willed and extravagant, and her father teased her for greed.
  • John Jr. (1874), the only son, shy, anxious and conscientious to the point of illness, grew up surrounded by women and prayer, groomed for a burden nobody named.

The family doctor

  • The household’s medicine was homeopathy, and its physician was Dr Hamilton F. Biggar, a Canadian-born small-town doctor of the old school who stitched a wound one day and stayed for decades. Harvard doctors thought him inept; Rockefeller trusted him completely, and in time it was hard to tell where Biggar ended and Rockefeller began.
  • The paradox Chernow keeps in view: the man who founded modern American medical research (Chapter 23) was personally loyal all his life to a homeopath.

Chapter 12 · pages 197–216

Insurrection in the Oil Fields

The late 1870s: Standard beat America’s largest corporation, the Pennsylvania Railroad, in open war, and then faced a revolt of the oil producers, a criminal indictment and a pipeline that threatened to make the railways, and his whole freight strategy, obsolete.

The war with Empire, 1877

  • The Pennsylvania Railroad’s fast-freight subsidiary, the Empire Transportation Company, owned some five hundred miles of pipelines and fleets of tank cars, and its president, Colonel Joseph D. Potts, a civil engineer from a family of Quaker ironmasters, aspired to be Rockefeller’s equal. He began buying refineries in New York and Philadelphia.
  • Rockefeller read it as a declaration of war. He demanded a meeting with Tom Scott and A. J. Cassatt of the Pennsylvania, got no satisfaction, and pulled Standard’s traffic off the railway, sending it by the Erie and the New York Central instead, while cutting kerosene prices wherever Empire’s refineries sold. He shut his own Pittsburgh refineries, which depended on the Pennsylvania, to make the point.
  • The railway was already weakened by the depression, and in July 1877 the great railroad strike, with riots and burned rail yards in Pittsburgh, hit it hardest. Scott gave in. That autumn the Pennsylvania sold Empire’s refineries, pipelines and tank cars to Standard and made peace, with rebates to follow.
  • Rockefeller and Potts traded insults for years. Afterwards Cassatt admitted the railway had had to grant such large concessions because Standard controlled the traffic.
  • By the end of the decade Standard refined nearly 90% of the oil refined in the United States.

Bradford and the producers’ revolt

  • Just when experts predicted the wells would run dry, a vast new field was found at Bradford, in north-western Pennsylvania. Between April and November 1878 oil in storage there swelled from about one million barrels to 4.5 million. Prices collapsed.
  • Standard’s pipeline company, United Pipe Lines, ran out of tanks and in 1878 announced it would take oil only for “immediate shipment” to refineries, at a price below the market. Producers who would not sell at that price watched their oil run on the ground. The immediate-shipment controversy bred lasting mutual hatred; Rockefeller was warned that if he went to the oil regions he would never come back alive.
  • The producers revived their union, sued, and lobbied in Harrisburg. On 29 April 1879 a grand jury in Clarion County, Pennsylvania, indicted nine Standard officials, Rockefeller among them, for criminal conspiracy to monopolise the business and extort rebates.
  • Rockefeller scoffed that the case would never be tried, but he feared extradition from New York and avoided Pennsylvania. Standard’s defendants used the pending indictment as a reason to refuse to testify elsewhere. In 1880 the case was settled: the producers dropped the prosecution in return for promises about rates and discrimination that Standard largely ignored.

The Hepburn hearings

  • In 1879 the New York Assembly held hearings, chaired by Alonzo Barton Hepburn, on the railways’ secret dealings. They exposed for the first time the scale of the rebates to Standard, and the fact that firms presented as independents were Standard-owned.
  • Standard witnesses stonewalled or declined to answer on the advice of counsel. Like many later exposés, the hearings fuelled public anger, and the report gave credence to what had been rumour.

Tidewater

  • The most dangerous challenge was technological. A group of independents led by Byron Benson set out to pipe crude over the Allegheny Mountains to the seaboard, bypassing the railways and Standard at once.
  • Blocked from crossing Maryland, the Tidewater Pipe Line Company built a 110-mile line from Bradford to Williamsport in central Pennsylvania, where it met the Reading Railroad. It began pumping in 1879, one of the great engineering feats of its day, and Benson became a hero of the oil regions.
  • Faced with a rare defeat, Rockefeller adapted. Standard used every lever against Tidewater (its tank-car fleet, pressure on refiners, rate wars), then built its own long-distance trunk pipelines faster and bigger, and bought a minority stake in Tidewater. By 1883 a market-sharing agreement confined it to a fixed share of the trade.
  • Chernow notes that Benson turned out to be no more enamoured of free markets than Rockefeller: once in the business, he wanted a cartel too.
  • The pipeline ended the era of railway rebates as Standard’s main weapon. From now on its power lay in owning the pipes.

A new legal form

  • An Ohio corporation could not own stock in companies chartered in other states, so for years the empire’s shares had been held in the names of trusted individuals as trustees. It was clumsy, risky and legally shaky.
  • The solution came from Samuel C. T. Dodd, a lawyer and Presbyterian elder from the oil regions, enormously fat and a wizard at legal draftsmanship, who became Standard’s chief lawyer.
  • The Standard Oil Trust agreement, dated 2 January 1882, conveyed the stock of some forty companies to nine trustees, Rockefeller at their head, who issued trust certificates to the owners. The public knew nothing of the contrivance, which created a $70 million enterprise controlling about 90% of American refining. Rockefeller held about a quarter of the certificates.
  • Chernow notes the irony that runs through the rest of the book: “trust” had meant a relationship of confidence. Within a decade it meant monopoly, and the laws to break monopolies were called antitrust.

To New York

  • Rockefeller and Oliver Payne transferred to New York in late 1883 and early 1884; the family kept Forest Hill for summers. They first lived in hotels, then bought a four-storey brownstone mansion at 4 West Fifty-fourth Street, previously the opulent home of Arabella Worsham (later the wife of the railway magnate Collis Huntington), with its furnishings.
  • The prudish Rockefellers left her decor largely in place, stained glass, paintings by Corot and Meissonier and all, but lived as plainly in it as they had in Cleveland.
  • Each morning he walked a stretch and took the Sixth Avenue elevated train downtown for a nickel, like any clerk, his mind already on the day’s problems.

26 Broadway

  • The trust first worked from unprepossessing quarters at 44 Broadway. On 1 May 1885, after spending nearly a million dollars on it, Standard Oil moved into an impregnable new building at 26 Broadway, near the tip of Manhattan.
  • To its enemies the address became a byword: a reporter wrote that many worthy men were convinced it was the most perilous shelter on earth, and others compared it with the offices of the Inquisition.
  • Inside, the executive committee lunched together every day at a long table in a top-floor room decorated with hunting and fishing trophies, and decisions were reached by discussion. In his self-effacing way Rockefeller yielded the head of the table to his most frequent adversary, Charles Pratt, and rarely overruled anyone. He asked questions, and waited.

Management by committee

  • Under the executive committee came a battery of specialised committees for transportation, pipelines, domestic trade, export trade, manufacturing, lubricating oils, purchasing and so on. They set standards for the subsidiaries, which kept their own names and managers.
  • The committees encouraged rivalry among local units by circulating comparable cost figures for every plant, so each manager could see who refined a gallon cheapest. Standard knew the output of nearly every well and refinery in the country.
  • Chernow credits Rockefeller with inventing much of modern corporate management: decentralised operations under central financial control, consensus at the top, and statistics as the common language. The combine now employed 100,000 people and exported oil across the world.
  • The structure also served secrecy. Subsidiaries that appeared independent took orders from 26 Broadway, and in the absence of any federal law on incorporation, the trust existed nowhere and everywhere.

Chapter 14 · pages 245–272

The Puppeteer

The 1880s empire at full stretch: a worldwide trade threatened by Russian oil, a marketing machine that reached every town in America, and the small, ugly episodes (Marietta, Buffalo) that fixed its reputation. From his ledgers Rockefeller pulled the strings.

The world market

  • Oil was an export business from the start: by 1866 two-thirds of Cleveland’s kerosene went overseas, and Standard Oil long exercised global dominance in illuminating oil.
  • The challenge came from Baku, the Russian port on the Caspian, where oil had seeped for centuries. In 1873 Robert Nobel, of the Swedish family, arrived with 25,000 roubles meant for buying walnut wood for rifle stocks and spent it on a refinery instead; the family built the Nobel Brothers Petroleum Producing Company, with pipelines, tankers and storage, into a modern integrated rival.
  • Then the Paris Rothschilds, led by Baron Alphonse de Rothschild, backed the railway linking Baku on the Caspian to Batumi on the Black Sea and entered the trade, and cheap Russian kerosene began to take markets in Europe and Asia.
  • Standard fought with price cuts, new marketing companies abroad (its first overseas affiliate, the Anglo-American Oil Company, soon dominated the British market) and, at times, talks with its rivals. Chernow shows it forced to deal with the Nobels and the French bankers rather than crush them: the first limit on its power, and it was set by geology, not law.

Marketing down to the wagon

  • At home Standard moved from refining into selling. It disbursed millions to build storage tanks and thousands of horse-drawn tank wagons to serve every American town; the wagons filled at local depots and, over the furious opposition of storekeepers, even sold door to door.
  • Standard’s share was overwhelming: government figures later showed it handling the great bulk of both exported and domestic kerosene.
  • Its field agents reported on every rival shipment, and the reports flowed up to the executive committees. Rockefeller’s ledger books, Chernow writes, let him play the puppeteer and manipulate his empire by invisible strings.

Marietta: George Rice

  • George Rice, a Vermont-born independent refiner from Marietta, Ohio, with a bulldog face, refused every offer to sell and spent the rest of his life fighting Standard in court and in print.
  • In 1885 Standard’s men got a small Ohio railway to charge Standard 10 cents a barrel and Rice 35 cents, and to pay Standard the 25-cent difference on every barrel Rice shipped: the drawback, revived. When it came out in court, Standard’s leaders disowned it, but the story became one of Tarbell’s centrepieces.
  • Standard’s southern marketing firm, Chess, Carley and Co. of Louisville, half-owned in secret since 1873 and run by F. D. Carley, a lapsed Methodist minister, became a byword for vicious tactics. When Carley learned that Rice had shipped a mere seventy barrels of kerosene to a Louisville merchant, he cut prices in that town until the order was cancelled.
  • In West Virginia, J. N. Camden, now secretly Standard’s, wrote of the little refiners at Parkersburg and Marietta that they would either get them or starve them. He had persuaded John W. Garrett of the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad, who thought he was fighting Standard, to grant a ten-cent drawback on all refined oil the B&O carried.

Buffalo

  • In the late 1870s the trust bought control of the Vacuum Oil Works in Rochester, a lubricating-oil firm owned by Hiram and Charles Everest, father and son. Rockefeller once touched Hiram Everest on the knee and asked whether he would not be better off out of the fight; Everest said he was a fighter, and Rockefeller just smiled.
  • Three Vacuum employees, J. Scott Wilson, Charles B. Matthews and Albert Miller, defected to start a rival, the Buffalo Lubricating Oil Company. Miller repented and went back to Everest. A still at the new refinery was then mishandled until it blew, and the Buffalo men sued and prosecuted.
  • At the 1887 trial the Everests were convicted and fined $250 apiece, a sum that mirrored the jurors’ doubts; the senior Standard men charged with them, Henry Rogers among them, were cleared. Rockefeller, testifying, lost his famous composure and shook his fist at Matthews.
  • Chernow thinks Standard’s senior men probably did not order sabotage, and were guilty chiefly of luring Miller away, but the case gave every critic a story of a refinery literally blown up.

Scofield, Shurmer and Teagle

  • Not every rival was bought or broken. In Cleveland, William Scofield, who had sold out in 1872, set up a new firm, Scofield, Shurmer and Teagle. In 1876 Rockefeller cut a secret deal with them, investing $10,000 and agreeing to buy a fixed quota of their output.
  • When they produced far beyond their quotas, Standard complained that they were a lot of pirates; they sued, and published their side. The firm’s young Walter Teagle would one day run Standard Oil of New Jersey.

The first critic

  • In the March 1881 issue of the Atlantic Monthly, its editor William Dean Howells published “The Story of a Great Monopoly” by Henry Demarest Lloyd, a Chicago journalist married into the wealthy Bross family, co-owners of the Chicago Tribune, for which he had written withering editorials since 1878.
  • It was the first serious national indictment of Standard Oil and the railways that served it, and it was chock-a-block with errors. Standard, true to its rule, did not answer. Lloyd would return to the subject at book length in 1894 (Chapter 18).

Chapter 15 · pages 273–282

Widow’s Funeral

The family secrets of the 1880s: a bigamous father kept at arm’s length and paid for, a mother buried as a “widow” while her husband was alive, and a brother whose resentment curdled into a lifelong feud.

Keeping Bill quiet

  • While John was consolidating the oil industry, Big Bill roamed between his two lives, telling people he had made John D. rich. He reportedly boasted that his Standard Oil investment was now worth $375,000. On visits he gave his grandchildren rifles and taught them to shoot.
  • In 1881 John agreed to buy his father a 160-acre ranch at Park River, North Dakota, on one simple condition: that he never take Margaret Allen there. Bill spent lazy summers hunting and fishing there with a younger protégé, Charles Johnston, on the adjoining land.
  • Bill was a frequent guest at Frank’s ranch in Kansas too, and it was plain he felt warmly towards Frank, the son most like him, and warily towards John, who held the purse strings and the secret.
  • Chernow reads the arrangement as a business deal: money for silence and distance. John never spoke of his father in public, and the press would spend decades hunting for the old man.

Eliza’s death, 1889

  • Eliza Davison Rockefeller died in March 1889, aged seventy-six; in old age she bore a comical resemblance to her famous son.
  • Bill did not come. John instructed the preacher to describe her at the funeral as a widow, and the death certificate kept up the fiction. The announcement even spoke of her years of widowhood.
  • After the funeral John was still fuming over his father’s absence, and for weeks insisted on it to anyone who would listen. Chernow notes that the lie, told to protect the family’s respectability, was also the son’s verdict on the father.

Frank

  • Frank Rockefeller, the youngest brother, was his father’s son: sociable, reckless, fond of hunting and speculation, perpetually in debt to John. He incessantly gambled in stocks and commodities, and his brother kept bailing him out.
  • John gave him chances in the business. Frank invested in a fleet of Lake Erie boats and John gave him a contract to carry Standard’s oil; while Frank was away hunting, Standard urgently needed more shipments, and John bought out his interest and reprimanded him sharply.
  • In 1870 Frank married the tall, handsome Helen E. Scofield, of the family of William Scofield, a refiner John despised and who had joined the pirates of Scofield, Shurmer and Teagle. Frank sided with his in-laws and nursed a grievance that he had been cheated in the 1872 takeovers.
  • After public outbursts against his brother Frank often came to John and apologised, and John would sadly murmur “Poor Frank!” and lend him more. John and William carried the mortgage on Frank’s Kansas ranch and financed more land.
  • When John put up a towering granite obelisk over the family plot in Cleveland’s Lake View Cemetery in 1898, Frank had the caskets of his two dead children moved out of it, so that they would not lie near his brother.

Chapter 16 · pages 283–300

A Matter of Trust

The late 1880s: Standard bet on a field of stinking oil nobody could refine and won, while the word “trust” became a political curse, New York’s Senate put Rockefeller on the stand, and Congress passed the Sherman Act.

The Lima gamble

  • By the mid-1880s experts again predicted that Pennsylvania oil would run out. Then a vast field was found around Lima, in north-western Ohio: within a few years more than 250 derricks had sprouted around the town and spilled over into Indiana.
  • The crude was almost useless. Its high sulphur content gave kerosene a vile smell (it was called “skunk oil”), left a film over lamps and corroded machinery. Even so, the trust bought every barrel offered and stored it in huge tank farms, and Daniel O’Day’s Buckeye Pipe Lines Company told drillers, with more force than subtlety, that they had to sell to it.
  • In July 1886 Rockefeller hired Herman Frasch, a vainglorious, German-born chemist nicknamed the Wild Dutchman, who had already patented a process for removing sulphur from sour Ontario oil, with simple orders: banish the odour from Lima crude.
  • The dilemma was whether to buy leases before Frasch succeeded, or wait and risk losing the best properties. A faction led by Charles Pratt obstinately resisted spending millions on the gamble. Rockefeller, who had always thought Pratt weak-kneed, offered to put up the money himself and take the risk; Pratt gave way: they would go it together.
  • By February 1887 Frasch had partial success using copper oxides. The breakthrough came on 13 October 1888, when Standard’s Lima manager Feargus Squire wired Rockefeller the news.
  • Standard became, for the first time, a major producer of crude, and built America’s premier refinery at Whiting, Indiana, seventeen miles from downtown Chicago, to serve the Midwest and West. The move from refining alone to owning the oil itself began here.

Politics

  • In 1884 the Ohio legislature elected Henry B. Payne, father of Standard’s treasurer Oliver Payne, to the United States Senate amid charges that Standard money had bought the votes; it was rumoured that Oliver Payne had sat behind a desk in a Columbus hotel room doling out bills to legislators. Rockefeller insisted he had opposed the election, as a Republican and never anything else.
  • Chernow shows Standard as a generous contributor to both parties, with Rockefeller always quietly doing his part, and politicians who came asking for payoffs; one demanded his payoff in January 1888, saying his campaign committee had a debt of almost $1,200.

The trust question

  • In 1888 the New York State Senate investigated trusts and summoned Rockefeller. Standard hired the eminent lawyer Joseph H. Choate, who was slow to appreciate his client: in consultation Rockefeller stretched out on a couch with a languid air and revealed nothing. On the stand he was polite, vague and unfailingly unhelpful, and presented Standard as one company among many; he submitted a list of 111 competing refineries to show it was not a monopoly.
  • Trusts spread to so many industries that the national platforms of both parties harshly condemned economic concentration. On 2 July 1890 President Harrison signed the Sherman Antitrust Act, introduced by Ohio’s senator John Sherman, brother of the general, which outlawed trusts and combinations in restraint of trade. Chernow notes that the device Standard had pioneered in 1882 led straight to it, and that for years it proved an ineffectual law.

Chapter 17 · pages 301–330

Captains of Erudition

The turn from giving to philanthropy: a Baptist college in Atlanta, a great university in Chicago, and the arrival of Frederick T. Gates, the man who would build the machinery for giving a fortune away.

Half the country

  • By the late 1880s, Chernow writes, it seemed as if half the country wanted to lynch John D. Rockefeller, while the other half only wanted his money. Thousands of begging letters arrived each month; strangers accosted him in the street and at church.
  • He still gave in the old way, personally and case by case, investigating each request. It was exhausting, and the fortune was growing far faster than he could give it away.

Spelman

  • Sophia Packard and Harriet Giles, the New England teachers the Rockefellers had met on their honeymoon, opened a school for black women in the dank, dilapidated basement of the Friendship Baptist Church in Atlanta in 1881, the Atlanta Baptist Female Seminary; the first class had eleven students, mostly mothers, and most of them illiterate. Rain dripped down the walls.
  • When Rockefeller visited, he and Cettie were moved by what they saw. He gave generously, through Henry L. Morehouse of the American Baptist Home Mission Society, and the school took the Spelman name in honour of Cettie’s abolitionist parents: Spelman Seminary, renamed Spelman College in 1924.
  • Chernow notes that giving to black education was a consistent thread in Rockefeller’s philanthropy from its first years, rooted in his wife’s family and his own anti-slavery upbringing.

A Baptist university

  • The Baptists had no great university. Augustus Strong, a powerful New York theologian (and later the father-in-law of Rockefeller’s daughter Bessie), lobbied him for years for a Baptist university in New York, on a grand scale, with Rockefeller’s money. Rockefeller distrusted the scheme and its pushiness.
  • The rival idea, a college in Chicago to replace a failed Baptist one, came from the American Baptist Education Society and its executive secretary, Frederick T. Gates, who had recently resigned a pastorate in Minnesota. Rockefeller invited Gates and Morehouse to lunch on 21 January 1889 and liked Gates at once.
  • The academic star was William Rainey Harper, a thirty-year-old biblical scholar being wooed by Yale, a prodigy who had finished his PhD at eighteen, whom Rockefeller knew and liked.
  • Gates took a low-key approach. On a clear spring morning in May 1889, after breakfast, Rockefeller told him on a walk that he would give $600,000 (Gates had held out for that stunning figure, about $9.5 million in 1990s money) if the Baptists raised $400,000 more within a year. They did.
  • He declined to have the university named after him. It opened on 1 October 1892, without ceremony, as he wished, as if it were the continuation of work already under way. Harper was its president.

The price of Harper

  • Harper was a builder who spent ahead of his income and came back for more. Rockefeller poured in gift after gift, some $35 million in his lifetime, while trying to impose budgetary discipline; he had sworn he would never cover operating deficits, and had to renounce the policy.
  • He kept his distance from the academic side and declined to interfere in appointments or teaching, even when faculty attacked monopolies.
  • His verdict was the one he gave about his best causes: it was the best investment he ever made.

Gates takes charge

  • In 1891 Rockefeller asked Gates to come to New York and help him with his giving. Gates became, in effect, chief of staff of the fortune, handling both philanthropy and many of Rockefeller’s private investments outside oil.
  • He brought method: investigate thoroughly, give to institutions rather than individuals, insist on matching gifts from others, and fund the causes of problems rather than their symptoms. In 1917, asked by the journalist B. C. Forbes to name the greatest businessman he had ever encountered, Rockefeller startled him by naming Gates.

Chapter 18 · pages 331–346

Nemesis

Just as Rockefeller tried to turn from business to giving, the law and the pen caught up with him: Ohio ordered the trust dissolved, a crusading attorney general accused Standard of bribery, and Henry Demarest Lloyd published the first great indictment. His body gave way at the same time.

Ohio v. Standard Oil

  • Ohio’s young Republican attorney general, David K. Watson, wandered into a shop one evening and happened upon a slim volume on trusts by William W. Cook, cheaply bound in imitation leather, which printed the Standard Oil Trust agreement of 1882. He saw at once that the Standard Oil Company of Ohio had illegally handed control of itself to trustees outside the state, and in 1890 he sued.
  • Standard’s friends in Ohio politics, Mark Hanna among them, pressed him to drop the case; the pressure only stiffened his resistance. On 2 March 1892 he won a famous victory when the Ohio Supreme Court ordered the trust dissolved.
  • The dissolution was largely a fiction. Rockefeller was designated one of eight liquidating trustees, and they procrastinated for years. Trust certificates were exchanged for shares in twenty companies, and the same leading shareholders, many of them the liquidating trustees, held a majority of each and elected the same directors. Chernow calls it legal legerdemain.
  • Watson’s successor, Frank Monnett, reopened the fight in the late 1890s with contempt proceedings. He alleged that Watson had been offered bribes on six occasions to drop the case, once as much as $100,000 in cash, but never provided corroborating evidence. Senator Joseph B. Foraker of Ohio, who was on Standard’s payroll, gave Monnett a brief and unforgettable education in Ohio political realities at a meeting in Washington.

The holding company

  • The escape came from New Jersey, which changed its corporation law to let one company own stock in others anywhere. In 1899 Standard Oil of New Jersey became the legal parent of the whole combine, controlling stock in nineteen large companies and many smaller ones, openly, with Rockefeller as its president.
  • Today we would call it a holding company; at the time it seemed an imaginary entity. It would take the federal government twelve more years to reach it.

Wealth Against Commonwealth

  • Lloyd had been at work for years on a book-length indictment. His former editor William Dean Howells was bowled over by the manuscript and steered him to Harper and Brothers, who published Wealth Against Commonwealth in 1894.
  • It was savage and moving, and it made Standard the emblem of industrial wrongdoing for a generation of reformers. Lloyd omitted all names, although everyone knew who was meant; in private he insisted that the executives ought to be in the penitentiary.
  • Chernow judges it chock-a-block with errors, as the Atlantic article had been, and blind to anything Standard had done to lower prices and organise a chaotic industry, despite its often accurate insights. Standard did not answer. Rockefeller, in his late seventies, was still furious when Inglis read passages aloud to him.

The body gives way

  • In the early 1890s, carrying the trust, the University of Chicago’s finances and the flood of begging letters at once, Rockefeller came close to a breakdown. He had serious digestive problems, perhaps stress-related. He said later he had worked himself almost to a nervous breakdown groping his way in giving without sufficient guide.
  • In 1893 his hair loss suddenly worsened. It became generalised alopecia, which in time stripped away even his eyebrows and eyelashes (his mother, he knew, had lost all her hair too). He tried restoratives, then a black skullcap, then wigs from a fashionable maker on the rue Castiglione in Paris and later from Cleveland, and in the end grew to love his wig.
  • He withdrew gradually from daily work and handed operations to John D. Archbold, who took charge from about 1895. Rockefeller kept the titular presidency, went to the office less and less, and stopped going to 26 Broadway altogether in 1897. He recovered from the digestive troubles, which never recurred.
  • The gaunt, hairless face of the photographs of the 1900s appeared just as the muckrakers turned on him, and fixed his public image: the man looked most sinister when he was least involved in the business.

Chapter 19 · pages 347–360

The Dauphin

The heir: a shy, dutiful boy raised among women and prayer, a college man who dared to dance, a husband who married into political glamour, and a young executive whose first adventure on Wall Street was a humiliation.

A sheltered boy

  • John D. Rockefeller, Jr., born in 1874, grew up the youngest child and only son in a house of sisters, governesses and strict evangelical rules. He was anxious, conscientious and prone to headaches and nervous exhaustion.
  • His father set up a tiny private school in New York, the Browning School, run by a talented instructor, John A. Browning, partly to educate his son among suitable boys and partly to keep him out of the fashionable schools of the rich; it was one more attempt to prevent the money from spoiling the children.

Brown

  • He went to Brown University, a Baptist foundation in Providence, and graduated in 1897. He was a good enough student to make Phi Beta Kappa and especially enjoyed economics and sociology, which he hoped would teach him how to handle a fortune.
  • He spent his freshman year without dancing, then succumbed to the vice in his sophomore year and danced all evening at a party. When he and friends mailed out invitations to a gathering, the infernal word “dancing” appeared on them, to be explained at home.
  • As manager of the football team in his senior year he endured endless teasing; his frugality was the stuff of campus legend.
  • At Brown he fell in love with Abby Aldrich, daughter of the Rhode Island senator Nelson Aldrich, the most powerful Republican in the Senate, though at first he could not muster the courage to ask her to dance.

Abby and the Aldriches

  • Abby grew up in a lively, worldly household of art, travel and political gossip, everything the Rockefellers were not. Senator Aldrich, a financier-politician of the Gilded Age who built a 99-room chateau at Warwick Neck on Narragansett Bay and sailed a 200-foot yacht, followed a policy his future in-laws knew well: deny nothing, explain nothing.
  • After a long, hesitant courtship they married on 9 October 1901 in an extravagant wedding at Warwick, Rhode Island, that reflected the senator’s cosmopolitan style rather than the groom’s family’s.
  • Abby became the warm, humorous centre of the next generation. In 1929, with Lillie Bliss and Mary Sullivan, she founded the Museum of Modern Art, on land next to the family houses on West Fifty-fourth Street; she and Junior had bruising quarrels over modern art, but were devoted to each other.

26 Broadway

  • In 1897 Junior went straight to work at his father’s office at 26 Broadway. His father never said a word to him about what he was to do there, and left him in limbo; he joined his father’s private staff, handling family business and philanthropy rather than oil.
  • In the fall of 1899 a Wall Street operator named David Lamar, later styled the Wolf of Wall Street, cultivated him. With his father’s own private secretary, George Rogers, as the gullible go-between, Lamar let it be known that the celebrated stock trader James R. Keene had taken a big position in a stock, and Junior bought heavily on the tip. It was a scam: Lamar was selling. Junior lost a large sum of his father’s money.
  • He went to his father in agony. Rockefeller asked for the details, said nothing in reproach, and quietly covered the loss. By showing generosity, Chernow writes, he enlisted his son’s loyalty forever.

Chapter 20 · pages 361–396

The Standard Oil Crowd

The 1890s fortune beyond oil: Gates as investment manager, the iron ranges of Minnesota acquired almost by accident, a duel with J. P. Morgan, and the Wall Street clique around William Rockefeller and Henry Rogers whose copper speculation gave Standard’s name a second stain.

Gates the businessman

  • Frederick Gates, hired to manage giving, turned out to be a gifted investor too. Rockefeller had scattered money into railways, mines, timber and land in the Northwest, much of it badly managed by others; Gates investigated, cut the losers and reorganised the rest. By the 1890s Rockefeller had $24 million invested outside oil and gas, a one-man holding company.
  • Some ventures were fiascos: a scheme to build up the town of Everett, Washington, where the Northern Pacific met the sea, lost heavily. Gates’s frank reports on them won Rockefeller’s trust.
  • Standard itself paid lavish dividends even in hard times (in one year, to prove doubters wrong, 105% on its stock), and dividends and appreciation made Rockefeller the richest man in the world.

The Mesabi Range

  • The pioneers of the Mesabi iron range in Minnesota were the backwoods Merritt family, “seven men of iron”, four brothers and three nephews, who found a broad band of rich, powdery ore near the surface and borrowed recklessly to build a railway to Lake Superior.
  • In the panic summer of 1893 they ran out of money; workers besieged their railway’s office for overdue wages. Rockefeller advanced them money, took their shares as collateral, and set up a holding company, the Lake Superior Consolidated Iron Mines. When the Merritts could not repay, he owned most of the Mesabi, almost by accident: his last great business project.
  • The Merritts, who had first hailed him as their saviour, sued him for fraud. Leonidas Merritt had met him only once, briefly; Gates told the Merritts that talking to him was talking to Mr Rockefeller. Rockefeller settled the case, and Chernow judges that the Merritts were not blameless and that Rockefeller, who liked to cast himself as the reluctant saviour of troubled ventures, was not either.
  • Through the Cleveland-based Bessemer Steamship Company, Rockefeller acquired fifty-six steel ore carriers, the biggest fleet on the Great Lakes, and his position in lake shipping became so strong that he could dictate rates to the steelmakers, Andrew Carnegie included. (He and Carnegie, the other great giver of the age, exchanged gifts: a paper vest from the teetotaller, excellent whisky in return.)

Morgan and U.S. Steel

  • In 1901 J. P. Morgan set out to consolidate the steel industry into United States Steel, and needed the Mesabi ore and fleet. Morgan was not thrilled about dealing with Rockefeller, whom he disliked, and Rockefeller found Morgan very haughty, very inclined to look down on other men.
  • Morgan urged Henry Clay Frick to visit Rockefeller at his Pocantico estate; Frick took a carriage from the station to avoid publicity. Rockefeller sent his son to negotiate. When Morgan asked his price, Junior replied that there must be some mistake: he had not come to sell; he understood Morgan wished to buy. Lake Superior Consolidated and Bessemer Steamship went into U.S. Steel on excellent terms for Rockefeller.

William, Rogers and Stillman

  • William Rockefeller had become a Wall Street figure. His friendship with James Stillman, who as the youngest director and then president of National City Bank recruited the Standard Oil men as depositors, made it New York’s largest bank, the ancestor of Citicorp. Two of William’s sons married Stillman daughters.
  • In 1899 William, Henry Rogers and their associates acquired secret control of the Anaconda Copper company of Butte, Montana, restyled the new holding company Amalgamated Copper, and fobbed it off on a gullible public for $75 million, paying off the purchase price and pocketing the difference. When copper fell, small investors were ruined.
  • The “Standard Oil crowd”, as Wall Street called them, became a byword for stock manipulation. John D. had no part in Amalgamated and disapproved, but the public made no such distinction; the scandal, exposed by the Boston promoter Thomas Lawson in 1904, fused with Tarbell’s series.

Retirement and Pocantico

  • Having stopped going to 26 Broadway in 1897, Rockefeller divided the year among his houses: Lakewood in New Jersey, Pocantico in late spring, Forest Hill for the summer, and Pocantico again in October.
  • At Pocantico Hills, in Westchester County, he had been assembling land since the 1890s above the Hudson; it would grow into a vast estate. He ran the estates with an internal market: when Pocantico “sold” trees to Lakewood, Pocantico was credited in the books.
  • Serene in his conscience, convinced that he had done nothing wrong, he played golf, landscaped, and watched the fortune grow. The attacks were about to begin.

Chapter 21 · pages 397–424

The Enthusiast

Retirement brought an unexpected passion, golf, and a sudden physical change; it also brought the daughters’ marriages, and the first of the family’s private tragedies.

Golf

  • As Rockefeller receded from the business world he found a new absorption. In 1899, staying at a hotel in Lakewood, New Jersey, he was pitching horseshoes with a friend, Elias Johnson, who praised his easy style and nearly unbeatable aim and suggested he try golf.
  • He played his first complete game, nine holes in sixty-four strokes, and took up the sport with a convert’s zeal: once, after inches of snow had fallen on Westchester County, Johnson was taken aback to get a call proposing a round. His best score for nine holes was fifty-three.
  • A golf professional from nearby Ardsley, William Tucker, coached him regularly, and in 1901 he hired a golf architect, William Dunn, to lay out a twelve-hole course at Pocantico; in one year he spent $27,537.80 on golf, some $450,000 in 1996 dollars. Desperate to extend Pocantico’s short golf season, he found a winter place in Lakewood.
  • For a man who shrank from intimate discussion, golf provided an outlet for sociability on his own terms: jokes, stories and gentle teasing on the course, with a circle of cronies, ministers and doctors.
  • Dr Biggar, his physician and frequent partner, credited golf with rejuvenating him. Rockefeller himself declared that he knew of no man in better health.

Generalised alopecia

  • The hair loss that had worsened in 1893 became, by the turn of the century, generalised alopecia, the total loss of body hair, which, Chernow notes, has been attributed to many causes, genetic factors among them. It stripped away even his eyebrows and eyelashes.
  • For a time he wore a black skullcap, which gave him an impressively gaunt look; then wigs, which he came to love.
  • The transformation came just as he became the most caricatured man in America. The photographs of the gaunt, hairless tycoon of 1904 fixed his image for a generation.

The daughters marry

  • Bessie had married, in 1889, Charles Strong, son of the theologian Augustus Strong: a prodigious young philosopher who had lost his faith, moody and aloof, who taught briefly and then withdrew into private study; to the Rockefellers the household remained an impenetrable mystery.
  • Around 1902, at thirty-six, Bessie suffered fresh medical problems, a stroke or heart ailment that left her sadly demented. In 1904 Charles took her to Cannes to consult neurological experts; she never recovered, and died in 1906.
  • Edith married Harold McCormick in 1895, a friend of Junior’s from the Browning School and son of Cyrus McCormick, inventor of the mechanical reaper and founder of the family firm that became International Harvester. The press called them the Princess of Standard Oil and the Prince of International Harvester.
  • Alta, openly envious, was desperate to marry. In 1901 she married E. Parmalee Prentice, general counsel of the Illinois Steel Company in Chicago, cold and smart, a rigid perfectionist. Chernow notes that both elder daughters married men who resembled their father in some ways, yet neither Charles Strong nor Parmalee Prentice had his redeeming qualities.
  • In their teens the children had formed their own family quartet, with Bessie on violin, Alta at the piano, Edith on cello and John.

A daughter of the oil regions

  • Ida Tarbell grew up among the derricks: she had watched Pithole rise and fall as a child, and her father’s tank business had suffered in the South Improvement war of 1872, which she saw at fourteen.
  • A serious, independent woman, she enrolled at Allegheny College in Meadville, Pennsylvania, in 1876, later studied in Paris, and became a star writer for S. S. McClure’s magazine with popular biographies of Napoleon and Lincoln. Her brother, William Walter Tarbell, was a leading figure in forming the Pure Oil Company, the most serious domestic challenger to Standard.
  • McClure wanted analysis rather than scandal, and chose the trusts as a subject; Tarbell proposed Standard Oil. With a research assistant, John M. Siddall, short, pudgy and bespectacled, she worked for years through court records, congressional and state testimony, and the papers of Standard’s victims, George Rice above all.

Henry Rogers, informant

  • Her most surprising source was Henry H. Rogers, Standard’s most senior active executive, introduced to her through his close friend Mark Twain. Rogers met her repeatedly, answering questions and supplying documents, apparently hoping to shape the story or to settle scores inside the company.
  • Chernow suggests Rogers badly misjudged her. He cooperated most on the episodes where he thought Standard had a case, and the series was savage anyway. She held her breath when the first instalment appeared, expecting him to cut her off once he realised what she was trying to prove.

The series

  • The first instalment appeared in November 1902, against an especially timely backdrop: an anthracite coal strike that winter had turned the public against monopolists. It ran in nineteen instalments and was published as a book, The History of the Standard Oil Company, in 1904. McClure’s circulation rose to 375,000.
  • She was most effective where she showed something small and mean-spirited. Her set pieces were the South Improvement scheme, the Cleveland takeovers, the drawbacks on George Rice’s oil and the case of Mrs Fred M. Backus, “the Widow Backus”, whose Cleveland lubricating plant Standard bought in 1878 for $79,000 ($19,000 for the oil on hand, $60,000 for the works) when she thought it worth far more.
  • Chernow checks the Backus story and finds the plant among the most backward in the business: it was lucky for her, he says, that she bowed out, because Standard soon built far more modern works. But a forlorn widow cheated by a scheming cad perfectly fitted Tarbell’s portrait.
  • Then, in July and August 1905, she capped the series with a two-part “Character Study” of Rockefeller: a man of concentration, craftiness, cruelty and something indefinably repulsive, a “living mummy”, hideous and diseased. She mocked his looks, his religion and his giving as conscience money, and dragged in his father.

Rockefeller’s silence

  • In public he said nothing. With golf cronies he joked about “Miss Tarbarrel” and “his lady friend”, but would not be drawn into serious discussion. “Not a word,” he said. “Not a word about that misguided woman.”
  • Standard’s silence was, in Chernow’s view, its most self-defeating decision. Nobody answered the charges point by point, and the story Tarbell told became the story.

Chernow’s verdict on Tarbell

  • On the methods her account is essentially sound: rebates, drawbacks, espionage, hidden ownership and local price-cutting were real, and she documented them better than anyone.
  • But the oil industry was not, as she implied, a morality play of evil Standard Oil against brave, noble independents: the independents begged for rebates and cartels too, and Standard’s efficiency and falling prices barely appear. In her morality play Rockefeller was the venomous toad in the garden.
  • Her personal portrait was unfair, driven partly by her father’s losses. Yet her series was one of the most influential pieces of journalism in American business history, and it made the federal case against Standard politically possible.

Roosevelt

  • Theodore Roosevelt, president from 1901, created a Bureau of Corporations in 1903; headed by James R. Garfield, it began to gather data on Standard Oil. At one point Standard made a peace offering that Garfield called, in his diary, a really astonishing proposal; Roosevelt was not moved. The stage was set for the government’s case.

Chapter 23 · pages 467–480

Faith of Fools

The founding of the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research: how a Baptist minister reading a medical textbook on holiday, a grandson’s death from scarlet fever and a homeopath’s patient combined to create America’s first great biomedical research centre.

A counterfactual

  • Chernow opens with a thought experiment. Had Rockefeller died in 1902, at the outset of the Tarbell series, he would be remembered as a great and ruthless businessman who gave generously to Baptist causes and founded a university. Everything that makes him a figure of the first rank as a philanthropist came after.

Gates reads Osler

  • On a summer holiday in 1897 Frederick Gates tackled a book of door-stopping length, William Osler’s thousand-page Principles and Practice of Medicine, the most renowned medical text of the day, from the Johns Hopkins Medical School.
  • He came away convinced that medicine could cure very few diseases and knew the causes of few more. Medicine, he concluded, could hardly become a science until qualified people were endowed to study it full time, free of practice, as at the Pasteur Institute in Paris (founded 1888) and the Koch Institute for Infectious Diseases in Berlin (1891).
  • He told Osler that this line of philanthropy was almost wholly neglected, and put the case for an American institute for medical research to Rockefeller.
  • There was a delicate problem: Rockefeller favoured homeopathy and his crony Dr Biggar was its champion; Gates dismissed the German founder of homeopathy, Samuel Hahnemann, with contempt. Rockefeller resolved it in his own way, saying he would favour an institution that was neither allopathic nor homeopathic but simply scientific.

Jack

  • In the winter of 1900–01, Edith’s two little sons, Jack and Fowler McCormick, were staying at Pocantico when both contracted scarlet fever. Nothing was then known about its cause or treatment. Fowler recovered; John Rockefeller McCormick, known as Jack, the grandson Rockefeller doted on, died aged four.
  • Jack’s death strengthened Rockefeller’s resolve. Edith and Harold would lose another child, one-year-old Editha, in 1904; in Chicago the McCormicks endowed an institute for infectious diseases in Jack’s memory.

The institute

  • Junior did the legwork. His chief adviser was Dr William H. Welch, professor of pathology and first dean of the Johns Hopkins medical school, who oversaw a faculty trained mostly in Germany and became president of the institute’s board.
  • The Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research was founded in 1901 with a modest initial pledge spread over ten years, which slowed the pace of development at first. Welch recruited as director his protégé Simon Flexner, whom he considered his most gifted pupil; a stellar team was soon assembled.
  • Rockefeller’s rule, the one that made the institute and every serious foundation after it, was to pick the best people, give them money and independence, and leave them alone. He did not interfere with the science.
  • Results came quickly enough to justify him. When a cerebrospinal meningitis epidemic killed thousands of New Yorkers, Flexner developed a serum that sharply cut the death rate. Nobel laureates followed in later decades.
  • Flexner came to symbolise the institute, and his high-minded standards set its tone. Today it is Rockefeller University.

Chapter 24 · pages 481–c.500

The Millionaires’ Special

A train of rich Northerners touring black colleges in the South launched the General Education Board, the first of the giant Rockefeller foundations, and with it a new kind of philanthropy: wholesale, expert-run, and on a colossal scale.

The train

  • In April 1901 a specially chartered train carried a party of wealthy Northerners on a tour of Southern schools; the press pejoratively tagged it “The Millionaires’ Special”. It was the brainchild of the department-store magnate Robert C. Ogden, an associate of John Wanamaker, who coupled evangelical faith with a retailer’s flair for promotion.
  • Junior made the trip. They stopped at the celebrated showcases of black education, Hampton Institute in Virginia and Tuskegee Normal and Industrial Institute in Alabama, whose principal, Booker T. Washington, preached a practical, enterprising gospel of vocational training. Junior called Washington a truly remarkable man and the journey the most instructive experience of his life.
  • The Rockefellers were already committed to black education, from Spelman onwards. The train showed Junior the scale of the problem: the whole South, white and black, was poorly schooled and poor.

The General Education Board

  • Junior and Gates proposed a board to promote education in the South; it was first conceived as a board for black education but was, tellingly, given the neutral name of the General Education Board (GEB), and incorporated by Act of Congress in 1903. It was, in effect, an extension of the Baptist education society with the Baptist trappings pared away.
  • As executive secretary Gates shrewdly chose Wallace Buttrick, a fellow graduate of the Rochester Theological Seminary and ex-Baptist preacher, an amiable, roly-poly man with an easy laugh and a consuming dedication.
  • Rockefeller funded it on a colossal scale: an initial gift of a million dollars, later millions more, and in 1907 a further $32 million, which the board hailed as “the largest sum ever given by a man in the history of the race” for a philanthropic purpose. True to form, he left it to his son and never met with its board.

Working with Southern prejudice

  • The board concluded that black schooling could not advance in the South without white support, and that Southern whites would not support it while their own schools were wretched. So it worked through white Southern officials, and took as its first major mission the creation of public high schools, of which the South had very few and, for black students, almost none.
  • In the end nine-tenths of its money went to white schools or to general purposes, a gradualism that Chernow presents as pragmatic and that critics see as accommodation to segregation.

Farms before schools

  • To raise the money to support schools, the South needed richer farmers. The board backed Seaman Knapp, who had won legendary status by showing, on a demonstration farm at Terrell, Texas, how the boll-weevil plague threatening Texas cotton could be contained through careful cultivation.
  • With board money Knapp’s farm-demonstration agents spread across the South, teaching farmers by example on their own land. The federal government later adopted the idea as the agricultural extension service.

Medical education

  • The board also financed the remaking of American medical schools. In 1910 Abraham Flexner, brother of the Rockefeller Institute’s director, published his report on medical education in the United States and Canada, naming the most notorious diploma mills; it sparked furious debate, and many of them closed.
  • Rockefeller money then endowed full-time clinical professorships, starting at Johns Hopkins, on the model of research-based German medicine: the foundation of modern American medical education.

Chapter 25 · pages c.501–516

The Codger

By 1905 Rockefeller was the most hated man in America and, in private, a genial, joking old man building a house on a hill. The “tainted money” controversy showed that even his gifts could now be held against him.

The nadir

  • By the close of the Tarbell series in 1905 his reputation had reached its lowest point. Standard was “the Octopus” in a thousand cartoons, and Rockefeller a monster; reporters and photographers were constantly caught vaulting the walls at Pocantico.
  • He was pilloried no matter what he did. If he did not tip, he was mocked as a miser; when he did, he was mocked for the size of the tip.
  • Yet in private he was, by the accounts Chernow collects, a relaxed and humorous old man, teasing his golf partners, singing hymns, dispensing homely maxims, and in excellent health.

Tainted money, 1905

  • In March 1905 it became known that Rockefeller had given $100,000 to the Congregationalists’ foreign-missions board. The gift had been solicited by Dr James L. Barton, who met one Sunday with Rockefeller’s aides Starr Murphy and Frederick Gates.
  • The Reverend Washington Gladden of Columbus, Ohio, a leader of the Social Gospel and a scourge of Rockefeller, urged the board to refuse what came to be called tainted money, and many ministers took up the cry.
  • The board, which had asked for the gift, kept it. But the argument, played out in pulpits and newspapers across the country, showed how far opinion had moved: in the 1880s Rockefeller’s giving had been admired; now even his charity was suspect as conscience money.

The Bible class

  • Junior, meanwhile, taught a men’s Bible class at the Fifth Avenue Baptist Church, where the family worshipped in New York. It swelled enormously as ambitious young men, some from Standard Oil, joined, and the press made it a target: every earnest talk the young heir gave on thrift, competition or Christian stewardship was reported and ridiculed.
  • His father’s own faith went back to Cleveland, where Deacon Alexander Sked, a Scottish florist who knew the whole Bible by heart, had immersed him in the baptismal basin and made him a member.

Kykuit

  • Rockefeller decided to build a new house at the highest point of Pocantico, a jagged ridge called Kykuit Hill (pronounced kye-cut, from the Dutch word for lookout), five hundred feet up with a peerless view of the Hudson and the distant Catskills.
  • To restore Cettie’s health he wanted it to catch the maximum winter sunshine. He left most of the work to Junior and Abby. Contracts went to Delano and Aldrich as architects (Chester Aldrich was Abby’s distant cousin), Ogden Codman, Jr., the Boston designer who had collaborated with Edith Wharton, for the interiors, and William Welles Bosworth for the gardens.
  • Codman, who shared Wharton’s revolt against the cold, cluttered Victorian rooms of her childhood, wanted to give Kykuit an easy tranquillity. Junior insisted that it was far less elaborate than many houses they had seen, but the grounds, with terraces, fountains and sculpture, set off a far bigger uproar than the house.
  • It became the family seat. Rockefeller, who had lived plainly all his life, now had a Georgian mansion; he spent much of his time in it reclining on a lounge in his bedroom.

Chapter 26 · pages c.517–536

The World’s Richest Fugitive

1905–07: state attorneys general hunted Rockefeller with subpoenas, Washington moved against Standard, Pulitzer’s World hunted his father, and the richest man in the world spent months dodging process servers and hiding in France.

The states close in

  • After Tarbell, suits against Standard multiplied in the states: Ohio, Texas, Kansas, Tennessee and above all Missouri, where the reformer Herbert S. Hadley was elected attorney general in 1905 and set out to prove that two supposedly independent marketers, the Waters-Pierce Company of St Louis and Republic Oil, were secret subsidiaries of Standard.
  • Hadley wanted Rockefeller on the stand in New York and failed. When a process server came to 26 Broadway he was told Mr Rockefeller was at home but could not be seen; the man spent the night dozing on Rockefeller’s stoop lest he slip out early. Rockefeller slipped away, and later blandly denied that he had fled the process server.
  • Other executives were cornered: as Henry Rogers’s car pulled away from the kerb, a process server named M. E. Palemdo sprang from hiding onto its running board, flung the subpoena at the speechless Rogers, flashed his court order and leaped off.
  • The press loved the chase; in one episode a pursuer chased him across the frozen Hudson, hopping from one ice floe to the next.

Washington

  • Rebates had been forbidden by the Elkins Act of 1903, which Roosevelt supported, and in 1906 the Hepburn Act gave the Interstate Commerce Commission power to set railway rates and placed interstate pipelines under its authority. The public naively assumed that rebating had ended.
  • In May 1906 Roosevelt sent Congress the Bureau of Corporations’ report on Standard’s transport arrangements, which showed that it still enjoyed secret rates. In November 1906 the federal government filed suit under the Sherman Act in St Louis to dissolve Standard Oil of New Jersey.

Escape to France

  • In 1906 Rockefeller took refuge abroad. Charles and Bessie Strong were renting the Château des Avenues at the edge of the forest of Compiègne, north-east of Paris, for the summer, and he joined them there; he played golf, drove through the forest, and read each letter from home for news of the suits. For a man who had fled to Europe for a peaceful interlude, the letters must have made grim reading.

The hunt for Big Bill

  • Joseph Pulitzer, publisher of the World, who had inveighed against Standard Oil as the most pitiless trust, offered a reward for information about Rockefeller’s father, setting off a nationwide manhunt; nobody even knew whether the old man was alive.
  • When his first reporter burned out, Pulitzer assigned another, A. B. Macdonald of the St. Louis Post-Dispatch. A druggist in Madison, Wisconsin, told him that for years a friend, a travelling doctor, had looked like the pictures of Rockefeller in Tarbell’s series. The trail led to Freeport, Illinois.
  • There Macdonald found the widow of Dr William Levingston, an eye-and-ear specialist whose obituary notice was dated 11 May 1906. Shown photographs, neighbours recognised him. Margaret Allen Levingston said that some things would be kept forever, now that her husband was dead.
  • Bill is always said to have died on 11 May 1906, but Chernow notes that references to his estate appear in John’s papers in January 1906, suggesting that he died earlier and that the family kept the death quiet. He was entombed in a plain box in an unmarked grave.
  • Bill’s old protégé Charles Johnston had kept their frontier life together a deeply buried secret until the press forced him to come clean. When the story came out, Rockefeller said nothing at all.

Judge Landis

  • Standard of Indiana was prosecuted in Chicago for taking rebates from the Chicago and Alton Railroad on oil shipped from Whiting to East St Louis after rebates were outlawed. The judge was Kenesaw Mountain Landis, forty-one, newly appointed, with prematurely white hair and a flair for publicity.
  • To justify an eye-popping fine, Landis asked for the parent company’s capitalisation and its earnings between 1903 and 1905, and subpoenaed Rockefeller himself to testify in July 1907. On the stand the old man seemed vague and genial; his mind wandered, apparently, while Landis tapped his spectacles on the desk in frustration.
  • In August 1907 Landis imposed the maximum penalty, $20,000 for each of 1,462 carloads, $29,240,000 in all, the largest fine in American history. Rockefeller affected indifference, but, Chernow writes, his poker face concealed deep rage.
  • In July 1908 a federal appeals court not only revoked the fine but severely reprimanded Landis for basing it on the assets of the parent company, which was not the defendant. The fine was never paid; the headlines had done their work.

The panic of 1907

  • That autumn a financial panic struck New York. Rockefeller put large sums at the banks’ disposal, but J. P. Morgan, who organised the rescue, got the credit. James Stillman observed that Morgan took the pose of saviour of his country and assumed all the credit.
  • Rockefeller resented it, and saw in the panic evidence that Roosevelt’s war on business had frightened the markets.

The Archbold letters

  • In the 1908 campaign William Randolph Hearst, backing his own Independence Party, began reading aloud in speeches from letters between John D. Archbold and politicians. A nineteen-year-old office boy at 26 Broadway, Charles Stump, and a colleague had copied them from Archbold’s files for Hearst’s newspaper.
  • They showed Standard paying Senator Joseph B. Foraker of Ohio, who was on its payroll, and other politicians, and Congressman Joseph C. Sibley acting as Archbold’s go-between with the White House. Foraker’s career ended. The letters destroyed what was left of Standard’s pose of injured innocence.

The federal case

  • The government’s case was run by Frank B. Kellogg, a Minnesota lawyer (Junior relayed rumours that U.S. Steel had pushed him at Standard to deflect heat from itself). In November 1908 Kellogg grilled Rockefeller in New York.
  • Rockefeller was, in Chernow’s telling, a superb witness for himself: courteous, patient and unable to recall arrangements he had personally designed. He insisted that competition had been fierce and that Standard had simply been better.
  • In November 1909 a federal circuit court in St Louis ruled that Standard Oil of New Jersey was an illegal combination and ordered it to divest its subsidiaries. Taft praised Kellogg’s “complete victory”. Standard appealed.

15 May 1911

  • At 4 p.m. on 15 May 1911, Chief Justice Edward White told a sleepy courtroom that he also had to announce the decision in the Standard Oil case. The Court upheld the break-up and gave Standard six months to dissolve itself.
  • It also adopted the “rule of reason” that White had long advanced in vain: the Sherman Act outlawed only unreasonable restraints of trade, not every combination. That doctrine has governed American antitrust law ever since.
  • The news reached Rockefeller on a golf course. He did not seem particularly perturbed. “Father Lennon,” he asked the priest he was playing with, “have you some money?” When the priest said no, Rockefeller told him to buy Standard Oil.

The break-up made him the richest man in history, and the fortune went into the most ambitious philanthropy yet: a campaign against hookworm across the South and the world, and the Rockefeller Foundation, “to promote the well-being of mankind throughout the world”.

Richer than ever

  • Once the thirty-four successor companies traded separately, investors could at last see what they owned. Their shares soared, and Rockefeller, who held about a quarter of each, saw his net worth reach a lifetime peak of about $900 million in 1913, more than $13 billion in 1996 dollars.
  • Chernow says this made him something just short of history’s first billionaire. (The book’s own jacket calls him the first billionaire outright.) Retirement had not slowed the fortune; it had multiplied it.

Hookworm

  • The idea came from Charles Wardell Stiles, a zoologist on the federal payroll whom most people dismissed as a dreamer. He was convinced that many Southerners thought to be lazy, or to suffer from malaria, were in fact infected with hookworm, a parasite that entered through bare feet and caused anaemia and listlessness, and that a victim could be cured with fifty cents’ worth of salts and thymol, which pried the worms loose from the intestine wall.
  • Walter Hines Page, the North Carolina-born editor and later ambassador, brought him to Rockefeller’s attention. In 1909 Rockefeller gave $1 million to create the Rockefeller Sanitary Commission for the Eradication of Hookworm Disease, deliberately not based in the North but run through Southern state health officials.
  • Its executive secretary was Wickliffe Rose, forty-seven, a Tennessee philosophy professor and clergyman’s son, gentle and decorous, who ran an operation of military scope: county dispensaries, travelling doctors, school inspections, sanitation drives. Many doctors were sceptical; William Osler went so far as to deny hookworm existed in America.
  • Harvard’s president Charles W. Eliot called it the most effective campaign against a widespread disabling disease ever conducted. The new Rockefeller Foundation then asked Rose to take it abroad, and he extended the fight to fifty-two countries on six continents, treating millions of people. Its International Health Board went on to yellow fever and malaria, its most spectacular success coming against yellow fever.

A charter for the Foundation

  • Gates had long warned that the fortune was rolling up like an avalanche and would crush the family unless it was given away faster than it grew. His answer was a set of permanent, general-purpose corporate philanthropies, one of them “to promote the well-being of mankind throughout the world”.
  • From 1910 Rockefeller sought a federal charter from Congress, promising public oversight: government officials would sit on the board. The timing was hopeless: it came just as the government was suing Standard. Critics saw the vague purpose as a gauzy curtain behind which the evil wizard of Standard Oil could work; President Taft opposed it as a bill to incorporate John D. Rockefeller. After three years it died.
  • In 1913 the New York legislature granted the charter instead, without the oversight Rockefeller had once promised Congress so fervently. He endowed it with about $100 million within a year, one of the largest gifts ever made.
  • At the first meeting Rockefeller became just one of nine trustees. Two were family members, Senior and Junior; the rest were his philanthropic staff, among them Gates, Starr Murphy, Wickliffe Rose, Simon Flexner and Jerome Greene of the Rockefeller Institute, and Harry Pratt Judson of the University of Chicago.
  • The year 1913 also brought the federal income tax, and with it a new incentive to give. Even at a top rate of 6%, the foundation offered both an act of conscience and an answer to the problem of a fortune too large for any family to hold safely.

Chapter 29 · pages c.580–590

Massacre

Ludlow, 1914: the family’s worst moment happened at a Colorado coal company it barely thought about, and it fell on the son. It made him, and it gave the family its first public-relations man.

Colorado Fuel and Iron

  • In 1902 Rockefeller had bought a large stake in the Colorado Fuel and Iron Company, a sprawling coal and steel concern in southern Colorado, as an investment; Junior sat on its board as his father’s proxy.
  • To run it Gates nominated his own uncle, LaMont Montgomery Bowers, bald and well-tailored, with a long business résumé and a hatred of unions. When an explosion at a CFI mine killed seventy-nine men in 1910, Bowers blamed careless miners.
  • The mining camps were company towns: company houses, company stores, company doctors, company-paid sheriffs. Against union organisers Bowers and the company’s president, Jesse Welborn, resorted to terror, fielding spies, detectives and armed guards.

The strike

  • In September 1913 the United Mine Workers called a strike for union recognition, an eight-hour day, and the right to trade outside company stores. Evicted from company houses, the miners and their families spent a bitter winter in tent colonies, the largest at Ludlow. Mother Jones came to rally them.
  • President Wilson intervened, asking Welborn for a full and frank statement; Welborn retailed vicious gossip about Mother Jones instead.
  • In April 1914 Junior testified before a House committee in Washington and defended the managers and the open shop as a matter of principle, on his managers’ word that outside agitators were to blame. His father gave him ten thousand shares of CFI as a reward for his testimony.

20 April 1914

  • Before the month was out, on 20 April, militiamen and company guards attacked the tent colony at Ludlow with rifles and machine guns and set the tents on fire. The arsonists did not know that two women and eleven children were huddled for safety in a dirt bunker under one tent; they suffocated.
  • About twenty people died that day. Ten days of open war followed in the coalfields, with miners burning mines and killing guards, until President Wilson sent federal troops.
  • The name Rockefeller became attached to dead children. Upton Sinclair sent Junior a “solemn warning” and led silent pickets in mourning outside 26 Broadway. In July anarchists building a bomb, widely thought to be meant for Pocantico, blew themselves up on the top floor of a Lexington Avenue tenement.

Junior’s education

  • Junior had believed his managers and been sincerely, completely wrong. In a second round of hearings in May 1915 the Commission on Industrial Relations, chaired by the labour lawyer Frank Walsh, released subpoenaed correspondence showing how closely Junior had in fact backed the managers’ hard line, and grilled him mercilessly.
  • His salvation was a providential personage, William Lyon Mackenzie King, a Canadian labour expert and former minister of labour (later Canada’s long-serving prime minister), whom the Rockefeller Foundation hired to study industrial relations. King exerted a tremendous influence on Junior and taught him that he could not simply delegate responsibility.
  • In September 1915 Junior went to Colorado with King, bought two-dollar suits of denim overalls from a company store at Lee’s suggestion, went down the mines, and visited miners’ homes. He then announced an employee-representation plan: elected employee representatives, grievance machinery, and improvements in housing and schools. It was a company union rather than an independent one; Mother Jones called it a sham and a fraud. But Junior and King introduced such plans at Standard Oil of New Jersey and elsewhere, and they became a model across American industry.
  • Senior, unlike his son, never acknowledged the legitimacy of organised labour.

Ivy Lee

  • The crisis also produced Ivy Lee, a publicist whom the family hired in 1914. Coached by Lee, Rockefeller eschewed loaded words like “trust”, and the family learned to explain itself.
  • Critics called Lee a paid liar, and Upton Sinclair memorably branded him “Poison Ivy”. But within a decade he had helped turn the most hated man in America into a harmless old gentleman handing out dimes.

Chapter 30 · pages 591–c.606

Introvert and Extrovert

The Ludlow years were also years of private loss and farce: Cettie’s death and a coffin smuggled out of New York to dodge a tax bill, and Edith’s flight to Zurich and Carl Jung, whose terms give the chapter its title.

Cettie

  • The Ludlow saga was intertwined with Cettie’s long decline. An invalid for years, nearly paralysed after what was perhaps a mild stroke, she died on 12 March 1915, forcing Junior to postpone his trip to Colorado until September.
  • She was to be buried in Cleveland’s Lake View Cemetery, beside Eliza. But Ohio’s tax officials, arguing that Rockefeller was still really a resident of Cleveland, had assessed him $1.5 million in taxes on his personal property. Having already paid taxes in New York, he refused to submit to what he called extortion, and feared being served with papers if he set foot in the state.
  • So the burial was delayed for months. When the time came, the undertaker hid her coffin inside a rough, plain, unmarked box and drove it to the Harmon station of the railway, to be taken secretly to Cleveland. Perpetuating the intrigue, only Senior, Alta, Parmalee and Cettie’s sister Lucy, “Aunt Lute”, attended the burial at Lake View.

Edith in Zurich

  • Edith McCormick, after losing two children (Jack in 1901, Editha in 1904), had grown increasingly unstable, with mood swings that widened after the birth of her last child, Mathilde, in April 1905. She had five children in all: John, Fowler, Muriel, Editha and Mathilde.
  • In New York in September 1912, Harold’s cousin Medill McCormick, an editor and co-owner of the Chicago Tribune who had been treated by Jung for alcoholism, introduced Edith to the Swiss psychiatrist. Jung recommended that she come to study with him in Zurich.
  • In 1913 she sailed with a retinue of children, tutors, governesses and servants, and settled into a suite at the fashionable Hotel Baur-au-Lac, where she spent the next eight years. Jung liked her mind; by October 1914 she had graduated from straight analysis with him to analysing patients herself.
  • She financed Jung generously, including the renovation of a posh Zurich mansion for a new Psychological Club, complete with library and restaurant. Sigmund Freud, who had broken with his heretical disciple, told Sándor Ferenczi that Jung was scheming for the Rockefeller money.
  • Jung’s typology of introvert and extrovert gives the chapter its title; the Rockefeller family offered specimens of both.

Money and scandal

  • Edith spent on a scale that appalled her father. She asked him to buy the Villa Turicum, a mansion she and Harold had built at Lake Forest, Illinois, and never occupied, for more than two million dollars; having had more than enough, he refused.
  • Harold, balding and pudgy, was tracked down at the Plaza Hotel by a Polish soprano, Ganna Walska, a voluptuous woman with a hypnotic gaze and ponderous jewellery, who pursued him and his fortune. Their affair ended the marriage; Edith and Harold divorced in 1921, and he married Walska the following year.
  • When Mathilde married her Swiss riding master, a forty-five-year-old widower named Max Oser, in 1923, Edith, who had paid for the expensive riding lessons, felt betrayed. She refused to see Oser, or even her own grandchildren, for many years.
  • It was exactly the outcome that the frugal upbringing of the Rockefeller children, with its account books and shared bicycle, had been designed to prevent.

Chapter 31 · pages c.607–618

Confessional

Widowed and nearly eighty, Rockefeller finally told his side of the story, in private, to a journalist who read him his enemies’ charges line by line: the Inglis interviews on which Chernow’s book leans. The same years brought a Sargent portrait and a giant war chest.

Alone

  • After Cettie’s death Rockefeller gave way to many lonely moments, but he was also liberated from years of tending an invalid. He filled the days with golf, drives, music and company; he loved the church organ, and once exclaimed after listening that he reverenced a man who composed music.
  • He had never answered a critic in public. Now, pressed by his son, he agreed to reminisce behind closed doors. “I have gone into it,” he told the interviewer, “because my son, very conscientious,” wanted it.

The Inglis interviews, 1917–1920

  • William O. Inglis, a New York newspaperman, was engaged to prepare an authorised biography. Starting in 1917, for an hour each morning, Rockefeller fielded his questions, slumped in an easy chair or reclining on a lounge in his bedroom at Kykuit; for six weeks at a time Inglis golfed with him and listened to him recount his past, and he read aloud the works of the critics, above all Tarbell and Lloyd, for the old man’s reply.
  • Rockefeller would stretch out on a lounge, shut his eyes and seem inert while Inglis read; just when Inglis thought he was asleep, he would sit up and contradict a detail. The replies ranged from genial reminiscence to fury (the “poison tongue” of the Prelude).
  • The biography was never published. The transcript, some seventeen hundred pages, stayed in the family archive. Chernow, poring over it, found it the nearest thing to a confession Rockefeller ever made: not of guilt, which he never felt, but of how he saw himself.
  • What he saw was a man who had rescued an industry, paid fair prices to rivals in a sinking ship, and been slandered by the envious. He admitted almost nothing, and on the rare occasions he came close, it was about his own feelings of injustice.

Sargent

  • In 1916 Junior and the family suggested hiring John Singer Sargent, who had studied in Florence and Paris and was the leading portraitist of the age, for five portraits: three of Rockefeller, one of Junior and one more. Sargent was reluctant to do the great man (he was tired of portrait work) but agreed.
  • The second portrait, in Chernow’s description, shows a face thin but not yet gaunt, the eyes pensive, the pose softer and more relaxed than in the first: the dignified elder statesman the family and Ivy Lee wanted the public to see.

The war

  • When the United States entered the First World War in 1917, Rockefeller gave on a heroic scale: $70 million to the war cause in all, including $22 million from the Rockefeller Foundation, for relief, the Red Cross and war work, and he bought Liberty Loan bonds in large amounts.
  • His last major philanthropic commitment came in 1918: $74 million to endow the Laura Spelman Rockefeller Memorial in Cettie’s name, which under Beardsley Ruml became a great patron of the social sciences.
  • He sent some of the Liberty bonds to Junior as a gift, with a note signed “Affectionately, Father”: the beginning of the vast transfers of the fortune described in the next chapter.

Chapter 32 · pages c.619–632

Dynastic Succession

Between 1917 and the early 1920s Rockefeller handed his son about half a billion dollars, and Junior, freed at last from a disguised allowance, turned the fortune into parks, museums, a church and a restored colonial town, while raising six children in his father’s thrifty image.

The transfer

  • For twenty years Junior had worked for his father on a salary that seemed generous but was, Chernow writes, a disguised allowance that kept him in a state of childlike dependence. He owned almost nothing in his own right.
  • From 1917, prompted in part by his age and by wartime taxes (the top estate-tax rate soared to 60%), Rockefeller began giving Junior huge blocks of securities, first Liberty bonds and then shares in the Standard Oil successor companies. Within a few years Junior had received some $450 million and was saddled with managing about half a billion dollars. Rockefeller kept only enough to live on and play the stock market; the rest went to Junior and the foundations.

Six children

  • Junior and Abby had six children: Abby, called Babs (1903); John D. 3rd (1906); Nelson (1908); Laurance (1910); Winthrop (1912); and David (1915). Senior once told his valet, John Yordi, that David was the grandchild who most resembled him.
  • Junior raised them as he had been raised: account books inspected in his study, small allowances, and pocket money earned by chores, such as killing flies at Pocantico or at the family’s summer house, the Eyrie, at Seal Harbor on Mount Desert Island in Maine.
  • In 1911 Junior and Abby bought 10 West Fifty-fourth Street, next door to Senior’s house, and built a nine-storey mansion, one of New York’s largest private residences, which Senior found infuriatingly extravagant. (It was later demolished; the site is now part of the Museum of Modern Art.)
  • The children rebelled in different ways. Babs, the eldest and most scarred, fought her parents, married in 1925 and later divorced; the brothers, as the next chapter shows, became the “Rockefeller Brothers” of mid-century politics and finance.

Junior’s projects

  • Maine. On Mount Desert Island he personally charted fifty-seven miles of auto-free carriage roads and gave land for what became, in 1929, Acadia National Park, the first national park in the East.
  • Wyoming. Alarmed by commercial clutter around Jackson Hole, and encouraged by Horace Albright, superintendent of Yellowstone, he quietly bought thousands of acres in the valley; the land was merged into an expanded Grand Teton National Park in 1950.
  • The Cloisters. He bought the collection of medieval art assembled by the romantic sculptor George Grey Barnard, then a one-man museum in northern Manhattan, and housed it in The Cloisters, in the new Fort Tryon Park he gave the city.
  • Williamsburg. The Reverend Dr William Goodwin, a professor of sacred literature at William and Mary who met Junior at a Phi Beta Kappa banquet in 1924, was a monomaniac on restoring the old colonial capital of Virginia. In 1926 he took Junior and Abby round it; Junior spent tens of millions on Colonial Williamsburg.
  • Riverside Church. For the liberal Baptist preacher Harry Emerson Fosdick he built the great Gothic Riverside Church on Morningside Heights, which the fundamentalist Baptist Bible Union denounced.
  • Senior had funded science, medicine and education; the son added art, landscape and history, and his philanthropy made the family name loved in a way the father’s never was.

Chapter 33 · pages c.633–650

Past, Present, Future

The 1920s and early 1930s: an old man in Florida handing out dimes for the newsreels and hoping to reach a hundred, while his son restored Versailles, built Rockefeller Center in the teeth of the Depression, and the Standard Oil companies raked in money.

The Casements

  • Blessed with his father’s longevity, Rockefeller spent his last two decades on a fixed round: Pocantico, Lakewood and, increasingly, Ormond Beach in Florida, where from 1918 he wintered in a modest house across from the Ormond Beach Hotel, called The Casements after its awning-covered windows.
  • It had no guards or gatehouse. He increasingly warmed to strangers, and when the local newspaper editor and mayor, George N. Rigby, wrote an article called “Ormond the Different” about the town’s respect for his privacy, Rockefeller set out to prove it true.
  • His days ran on a strict timetable of golf, drives, naps, meals, hymns, and dozing in his mother’s old rocking chair. His new physician, an elderly German named Dr Henry N. Moeller, fattened him up a little: his face grew rounder, and he humbly asked for a waiver to eat ice cream. He told Rigby he had tried to do what good he could and would really like to live to be a hundred.

Dimes

  • The shiny souvenir dimes, spontaneous at first, became a managed ritual, undoubtedly Ivy Lee’s idea: dimes for adults, nickels for children, handed out on his rounds to household staff, caddies and visitors, always with a word about thrift.
  • He did not mind mugging for newsreel cameras when celebrities called; the humorist Will Rogers, whose dry, folksy quips were not unlike his own, traded one-liners with him at Ormond Beach in February 1927. A Hearst Movietone photographer, Curt Engelbrecht, pursued him until he agreed to be filmed.
  • The old man handing out dimes became the image of the late Rockefeller: the wizened codger who replaced, in the public mind, the predator of Tarbell’s series. It was carefully produced, and he enjoyed the performance.

1929

  • He still played the stock market with the modest fortune he had kept. After the crash of October 1929 he issued a statement that, believing the fundamentals sound, “my son and I have for some days been purchasing sound common stocks”. When the comedian Eddie Cantor was told the Rockefellers were buying, he responded with the wisecrack: “Sure, who else had any money left?”
  • He had always enjoyed speculating with what he kept; on the eve of the First World War his borrowings had swelled to nearly $10 million, and Junior had reminded him how often he had preached caution.

Junior’s monuments

  • In France after the war, Junior was startled by the deteriorating state of the palace at Versailles, with rusting fences and water dripping from the roof. He gave the French prime minister, Raymond Poincaré, a million dollars to refurbish the Versailles roof and gardens, make emergency repairs at Fontainebleau and restore the great cathedral of Reims, scarred by wartime bombing, and later more.
  • In Manhattan, when the Metropolitan Opera decided to abandon its old quarters and its chairman Otto Kahn looked for a site for an opulent new house, Junior agreed to lease land from Columbia University in midtown, the site of the old Elgin Botanical Garden, at more than three million dollars a year. The crash killed the opera plan, and Junior, left holding an enormous lease, built the office complex of Rockefeller Center on his own, one of the largest private building projects of the Depression.
  • He also broke publicly with the family’s long support for Prohibition, which his parents had championed, calling in 1932 for its repeal because it had failed.

Chapter 34 · pages c.651–c.664

Heirs

The grandchildren: six young people raised on account books and prayer who became, as the “Rockefeller brothers”, a governor and vice-president, a banker, a conservationist, a philanthropist and another governor, with a sister scarred by her upbringing.

A grandfather’s table

  • In his last years Rockefeller, like a pastor receiving his flock, greeted each grandson as “Brother”. Seated at the head of the table, he told tales from his past and mimicked old adversaries, and he played games (only ones where he could dictate the rules) in which the winner got a dime and the losers nickels.
  • When Rockefeller Center rose, the family office moved to the fifty-sixth floor of its new skyscraper; henceforth Room 5600 was the seat of the Rockefeller empire, managing the family’s money, philanthropy and affairs.

Six lives

  • Babs, the only daughter, was the most scarred by the family’s strictures. She rebelled early, married in 1925 and later divorced.
  • John D. 3rd, shy and dutiful like his father, took on the family’s philanthropic mantle; he married Blanchette Hooker at the Riverside Church in 1932 before 2,500 guests, and later chaired the Rockefeller Foundation, Lincoln Center and the Population Council.
  • Nelson, charming and restless, danced well (his brother noted in his diary that he himself was rotten), married his childhood friend Mary Todhunter Clark (“Tod”), thin and aristocratic, without consulting his father, and took his bride to Ormond Beach to meet his grandfather. He became governor of New York and, under Gerald Ford, vice-president.
  • Laurance, born in 1910 and named for his grandmother, took up photography, became a pioneer of venture capital, and a leading conservationist.
  • Winthrop, big and broad-shouldered, was expelled from Yale in his junior year, spent the summer of 1933 as a roustabout in the Texas oil fields for Humble Oil, trained at the Chase National Bank, smoked, drank and philandered, and later became governor of Arkansas.
  • David, the youngest and, Senior thought, the one who most resembled him, became chairman of the Chase Manhattan Bank, heir to the Chase National that his uncle Winthrop Aldrich had built into the world’s largest bank.

What the fortune did to them

  • The grandchildren inherited Senior’s habits of work and duty and his son’s sense of public obligation, but also his wariness: they grew up under guard, pursued by the press, taught that the money was a trust and a danger.
  • The Standard Oil companies, meanwhile, went on making money for all of them: Jersey Standard (later Exxon), Socony (later Mobil), Standard of California (Chevron), Standard of Indiana (Amoco) and the rest, which remained among the largest companies in the world.

Chapter 35 · pages c.665–676

See You in Heaven

The last years and the death, in 1937, of the world’s richest man, who never lost the thrifty habits of the Cleveland clerk; and the ledger of what he left.

The last years

  • The world’s richest man never lost the thrifty habits of the Cleveland clerk. His days were minutely budgeted, and he never tired of preaching economy to everyone around him.
  • He took his farewells with a joke. When Henry Ford visited, Rockefeller said, “Good bye, I’ll see you in heaven,” and Ford replied, “You will if you get in.” The chapter takes its title from the line.
  • He had hoped to reach a hundred. In the early morning of 23 May 1937, at The Casements in Ormond Beach, he lapsed into a coma and died in his sleep, six weeks short of his ninety-eighth birthday. The official cause was sclerotic myocarditis, a hardening and inflammation of the heart muscle.

The funeral

  • The body came north by train, with the family waiting on the platform in identical homburgs. On 25 May the Reverend Harry Emerson Fosdick, Junior’s pastor at Riverside Church, gave a brief, touching eulogy at the funeral at Pocantico while Dr Archer Gibson played the Kykuit pipe organ.
  • He was buried in Cleveland, in the family plot at Lake View Cemetery under the obelisk he had raised, with his wife and his mother, not far from men he had bought out in 1872.

The ledger

  • Having given most of his money to his philanthropies and his son, he left an estate of only $26.4 million, much of it claimed by taxes.
  • He gave away about $530 million in his lifetime, and $450 million of it went directly or indirectly into medicine, making him the greatest lay benefactor of medicine in history. Andrew Carnegie, the other great giver of the age, gave away some $350 million in his entire life.
  • His son then gave away $537 million directly between 1917 and 1960, plus another $540 million through the family’s institutions, and the foundations went on giving long after both.
  • What he founded endured: the University of Chicago, Rockefeller University, the General Education Board’s schools and farm agents, the global campaigns against hookworm and yellow fever, and the model of the modern foundation, run by experts and left alone.

Chernow’s verdict

  • It is too glib, Chernow writes, to call Rockefeller a hypocrite who used piety as a cloak for greed: the voice of his private letters is the voice of his public professions. He held the devout Baptist and the predatory monopolist together without apparent strain.
  • His good and bad sides were not a mask and a face: the same traits (patience, secrecy, self-command, the certainty of being right, the love of order and the ledger) produced both the drawback and the Rockefeller Institute.
  • His business legacy was the modern corporation: managed by committees and statistics, integrated from raw material to retail, and legally organised as a holding company. His philanthropic legacy was its mirror image, the modern foundation.
  • Chernow will not strike a balance. He sets the refiners crushed in 1872, the bought legislators and the tent colony at Ludlow against hookworm, yellow fever, meningitis serum and a great university, and leaves both columns standing, because the man himself never felt the need to total them.

Since 1998

What held up

Titan is a biography, not a forecast, but its closing picture of Rockefeller’s legacy can be checked against what happened after it was published in May 1998.

Held

The break-up reversed in slow motion Ch. 27, 34

The book ends with the Standard Oil successors still separate giants. Within four years of publication they began to recombine: BP bought Amoco (1998) and ARCO (2000), Exxon and Mobil merged in 1999, Chevron took over Texaco in 2001 and Conoco merged with Phillips in 2002. The 1911 decree’s structure did not survive the century, though the economics of 1911, many firms in a world market, did.

Held

The institutions outlived the fortune Ch. 23, 28, 35

The Rockefeller Foundation, Rockefeller University and the University of Chicago remain major institutions; the foundation model Rockefeller and Gates built is still the template for large-scale philanthropy, from Carnegie’s heirs to the Gates Foundation.

Turned

The family and oil Ch. 34

The book shows the heirs living on Standard Oil money. In 2014 the Rockefeller Brothers Fund announced it would divest from fossil fuels, and in 2016 the Rockefeller Family Fund said it would sell its ExxonMobil holdings and criticised the company’s record on climate: the family’s philanthropy turned on the industry that made it.

Note

“History’s first billionaire” Ch. 28

The book’s jacket calls Rockefeller history’s first billionaire; its text is more careful, putting his 1913 peak at about $900 million, “something just short” of a billion. The jacket, not the book, is the source of the popular claim.

Where readers push back

Debates and pushback

Did Standard actually use predatory pricing? McGee 1958

John S. McGee’s study of the case record in the Journal of Law and Economics (1958) argued that Standard grew by buying rivals, not by selling below cost to ruin them, since price wars cost the bigger firm more. Chernow reports local price-cutting (Chess, Carley’s war on Rice’s seventy barrels) as a method; economists still argue about how often it was used and whether it worked.

Was the real weapon the railways? Granitz and Klein 1996

Elizabeth Granitz and Benjamin Klein argued that Standard’s power came from policing a railway cartel: it acted as “evener” for the trunk lines, and was paid in rebates and drawbacks that raised its rivals’ costs. That reading fits the South Improvement scheme and the evener role Chernow describes, and makes the drawback the centre of the story rather than a lapse.

Was consolidation good for customers? Nevins; Tarbell

Kerosene prices fell steeply under Standard, which defenders from Nevins onward treat as the verdict that matters. Critics answer that prices were falling across the industry anyway, that the gains went disproportionately to Standard, and that domestic prices rose after Archbold took charge in 1895. Chernow does not settle it with data.

Market entrepreneur or political one? Folsom 1987; Josephson 1934

Matthew Josephson’s The Robber Barons (1934) made Rockefeller the archetype of the predator; Burton Folsom’s The Myth of the Robber Barons (1987) set him among entrepreneurs who won on cost rather than by state favours. The rebates, the drawback and the Archbold letters complicate Folsom’s line; the Lima gamble and the by-products complicate Josephson’s. Chernow gives the evidence for both.

Was the break-up necessary? Ch. 27

By 1911 Texas, California, Oklahoma, Russia and Sumatra had eroded Standard’s share before any court acted, and the successors were worth more apart. One reading is that the market was already doing the Court’s work; another is that the case’s value lay in the rule of reason and in ending the secrecy, not in the numbers.

Is Chernow too kind? Reviewers, 1998

Some readers found the book fair to a fault, its psychological reading generous to a man who did real harm; others thought it the first account to let him be a person. The Inglis interviews are his own voice, and the book leans on them heavily: a self-portrait of injured innocence, which Chernow tests against the record but also lets speak.

Glossary

Job Day
26 September, the day in 1855 he was hired at Hewitt and Tuttle; celebrated for life.
Ledger A
The red notebook, bought for a dime, recording every cent he earned, spent and gave from 1855.
Refining
Distilling crude oil into kerosene and other products: the stage Rockefeller chose.
Rebate
A secret refund on the published freight rate, won by guaranteeing volume.
Drawback
A payment to Standard on oil shipped by its rivals.
Evener
Standard’s role in sharing oil traffic among the railroads, for a fee.
Waybill
A railway’s record of a shipment: sender, volume, destination.
South Improvement Company
The 1872 railway–refiner scheme of rebates, drawbacks and waybills; it never shipped a barrel.
Cleveland Massacre
The purchase of 22 of Standard’s 26 Cleveland rivals in the weeks of the 1872 crisis.
Pittsburgh Plan
The short-lived National Refiners’ Association cartel of 1872–73.
Immediate shipment
Standard’s 1878 refusal to store Bradford crude except for immediate sale, at its price.
Trust
Stock held by trustees for owners; after 1882, a byword for monopoly.
Liquidating trustees
The eight men, Rockefeller among them, charged with dissolving the trust after 1892, who took years about it.
Holding company
A company whose business is owning other companies: New Jersey’s answer, from 1899.
Sherman Act
The 1890 federal law against combinations in restraint of trade.
Elkins and Hepburn Acts
The 1903 and 1906 laws that tightened the ban on rebates and put pipelines under federal regulation.
Rule of reason
The 1911 doctrine that only unreasonable restraints of trade are illegal.
Lima crude
Sulphur-heavy “skunk oil” from Ohio that nobody could refine until Frasch’s process of 1888.
Tainted money
The 1905 row over whether churches should accept Rockefeller’s gifts.
Scientific philanthropy
Gates’s doctrine: fund institutions that remove causes, run by independent experts.
Employee representation
Junior’s post-Ludlow system of elected workers’ councils: a company union, not an independent one.
Kykuit
The Rockefeller house on the highest point of Pocantico; Dutch for lookout.
The Casements
His winter house at Ormond Beach, Florida, where he died in 1937.
Room 5600
The family office on the fifty-sixth floor at Rockefeller Center.

Using it

Reading it

Some 670 pages of text and about a hundred more of notes, bibliography and index, in thirty-five chapters with a foreword and a prelude.

  1. Chapters 1–5 slowly

    The parents explain the man. Skim nothing about Big Bill.

  2. Chapters 6–13 with a pencil

    The consolidation of 1867–1885 is where the mechanics are. Keep the rebate figure to hand.

  3. Chapter 22 beside Tarbell

    Chernow’s middle third is partly an answer to her. Reading the charge and the reply together is the best way in.

  4. Chapters 22–29 in one go

    Tarbell, the suits, the break-up, the Foundation and Ludlow form one arc: the collapse and the rebuilding of the name.

  5. Finish on the giving

    Chapters 17, 23, 24 and 28 change how the first half reads, which is Chernow’s point.
