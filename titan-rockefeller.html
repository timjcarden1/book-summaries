f. 00 · The book itselfRandom House, 1998

The book itself

Chernow set out to answer a question nobody had answered properly in eighty years: what was actually going on inside the most private American of his era?

Rockefeller was the most written-about businessman in American history and the least known. He gave almost no interviews, testified evasively, destroyed or withheld records, and left behind a public image assembled equally by his enemies and by his own publicists. Two biographical traditions had hardened: the muckrakers' monster, fixed by Ida Tarbell in 1904, and the authorised saint of Allan Nevins's two sympathetic studies in 1940 and 1953.

Chernow's advantage was archival. He worked from the Rockefeller Archive Center at Pocantico, and above all from a long, unpublished set of reminiscences Rockefeller dictated to a friendly newspaperman, William O. Inglis, between 1917 and 1920. Inglis sat with him over hundreds of hours, often reading Tarbell's accusations aloud line by line and recording the old man's replies. It is the closest thing to a Rockefeller confession that exists, and it had been barely used.

What emerges is not a resolution of the two traditions but an argument that both were describing real things. The book's organising idea is that Rockefeller's contradictions were not hypocrisy. He was genuinely devout and genuinely predatory, and the same traits produced both: the patience, the secrecy, the refusal to be provoked, the conviction that he was an instrument of a larger order. He tithed from his first pay packet at sixteen and gave away over half a billion dollars, and he did it with the same temperament that let him strip twenty-two competitors out of Cleveland in six weeks without raising his voice.

How the book moves

Chernow braids three strands rather than telling them in sequence. The business story runs from Cleveland in 1863 to the Supreme Court in 1911. The family story runs alongside it and is often the more disturbing of the two: a con-artist bigamist father, a rigidly pious mother, an obsessively controlled household, four children raised in deliberate ignorance of their own wealth, and an heir who spent his life trying to launder a name. The philanthropic story starts as a childhood habit and ends as the invention of the modern foundation.

The book is long, roughly 774 pages, and the density is in the middle third. If you are listening to it, the two stretches that repay the most attention are the 1872 to 1882 consolidation and the 1900 to 1914 collapse of the public reputation, because those two are where the mechanics are actually explained.

What the book is not

It is not a business history of the oil industry. Technology, geology and the producers' side get sketched rather than analysed. It is not an economic assessment either: Chernow states the case that Standard cut prices and rationalised a chaotic industry, and the case that it did so by methods that were coercive and often secret, and he does not adjudicate between them with any economic apparatus. It is a biography, and its centre of gravity is character.

f. 01 · Devil Bill and the making of a Baptist1839 – 1855

Devil Bill and the making of a Baptist

The two halves of Rockefeller's character were installed before he was sixteen, one by each parent, and they never merged.

John Davison Rockefeller was born on 8 July 1839 in Richford, New York, a hardscrabble settlement in the state's southern tier. His father, William Avery Rockefeller, was a large, genial, physically impressive man who sold patent medicines, traded horses, lent money at hard rates, and disappeared for months at a time. He advertised himself as a botanic physician and, later, as a specialist in cancer cures, charging as much as twenty-five dollars for treatments that did nothing.

Bill Rockefeller was also, from 1855, a bigamist. While still married to Eliza he married a much younger woman, Margaret Allen, in Ontario under the name Dr. William Levingston, and maintained that second household for the rest of his life. He died in 1906 in the Dakotas as Levingston. His famous son, then the richest man in the world, spent decades quietly containing the story while newspapers hunted for it.

Eliza Davison Rockefeller was the counterweight. Left with the children and often with no money, she ran the house on strict Baptist discipline, unbending thrift and the phrase her son repeated all his life: willful waste makes woeful want. She whipped him with a birch switch and once, when he told her mid-beating that he was innocent of the charge, she finished anyway on the grounds that it would do for next time. Chernow's reading is that Rockefeller learned from her the endurance and the moral scaffolding, and learned from his father the negotiating, the concealment and the ease with which a smiling man can take another man's money.

"I cheat my boys every chance I get. I want to make 'em sharp."

William Avery Rockefeller, as quoted by a neighbour

Moving, always moving

The family moved constantly, staying a step ahead of gossip and creditors: Richford, then Moravia, then Owego, and in 1853 to Strongsville outside Cleveland. The boy went to Central High School in Cleveland, then spent about three months at Folsom's Commercial College learning bookkeeping, penmanship and commercial law. That short course, not the high school, was the education that mattered.

He was already recognisably himself: slow, deliberate, unusually quiet, indifferent to sport, precise about money, and religious in a way that his contemporaries found more like a habit of order than an enthusiasm. He joined the Erie Street Baptist Mission Church and stayed with it for decades, sweeping floors, serving as a trustee and eventually rescuing it from its mortgage. He tithed from his first wages and never stopped.

The pattern Chernow draws out

The son organised his entire life as a refutation of the father: no drinking, no gambling, no showmanship, no debt he could not cover, no promises he did not keep, one wife, one address at a time, everything recorded. And yet the father's gifts came through intact. Rockefeller's talent for the poker face in negotiation, his taste for operating through concealed ownership, and his ability to feel morally comfortable while doing ruthless things are, in this reading, Bill's inheritance wearing Eliza's clothes.

f. 02 · Job Day and Ledger A1855 – 1863

Job Day and Ledger A

He found work at sixteen after six weeks of walking the Cleveland waterfront, and treated the date as more important than his own birthday for the next eighty years.

In the late summer of 1855 Rockefeller made a list of the substantial firms in Cleveland and called on each one, then called again, then a third time. He was turned away for six weeks. On 26 September the commission merchants Hewitt & Tuttle put him to work as an assistant bookkeeper. No salary was discussed. Three months later they handed him fifty dollars.

He loved the work in a way that is hard to overstate. The ledgers of a commission house recorded every transaction of a small trading empire, and he treated them as both a moral and a technical instrument. He checked every freight bill line by line and found errors that others waved through. He learned, in his own words, to have great respect for figures and facts no matter whose they were. He would later say he was trained in the school of the ledger and never left it.

He also kept his own. Ledger A, a small account book recording every cent he earned and spent from 1855, became the family relic. He read from it aloud to his children and grandchildren, showed it to visitors, and in old age still spoke about it with visible emotion. From the first pay packet it recorded charitable giving, and by the time he was twenty he was giving away roughly six percent of his income, rising past ten.

"All my future seemed to hinge on that day, and I often tremble when I ask myself the question: what if I had not got the job?"

On Job Day, 26 September 1855

Clark & Rockefeller

In 1859, aged nineteen, he went into business with Maurice Clark as produce commission merchants. He had saved about eight hundred dollars. His father lent him the balance of his share at ten percent interest, characteristically charging his son a commercial rate and, equally characteristically, occasionally demanding the principal back at awkward moments to test him.

The Civil War was the making of the firm. Government demand for provisions, salt and grain ran through Cleveland, and Clark & Rockefeller cleared it. Rockefeller did not serve. He paid for a substitute, as thousands of prosperous Northerners legally did, and justified it afterwards on the grounds that he was supporting his mother and siblings and that his business was itself war work. Chernow treats it as one of the episodes where the self-justification is a little too smooth.

Two miles of the same story were unfolding in western Pennsylvania. On 27 August 1859 Edwin Drake struck oil at sixty-nine and a half feet outside Titusville. Within three years the valley was a forest of derricks, the price of crude had collapsed from around sixteen dollars a barrel to pennies, and a chaotic new business had appeared that needed exactly what Rockefeller had: capital, transport connections and an accountant's temperament.

f. 03 · The refiner1863 – 1870

The refiner

He never wanted to drill for oil. He wanted the one stage of the business where scale, chemistry and freight rates decided the outcome.

In 1863 Rockefeller and Clark backed Samuel Andrews, an English mechanic who understood how to distil kerosene cleanly, and built the Excelsior Works on Kingsbury Run in Cleveland, sited deliberately beside a railway line. Within two years refining was earning more than the produce business, and Rockefeller wanted to plough everything back into it. The Clark brothers wanted to slow down.

On 2 February 1865 the partners agreed to auction the refinery between themselves. The bidding climbed past fifty thousand dollars, then seventy. Rockefeller bid $72,500 and Maurice Clark put down his hand. He was twenty-five years old, and he had, in a single afternoon, taken sole control of the largest refinery in Cleveland. He called it the day that determined his career.

Flagler, and the discovery of leverage

The second decisive arrival was Henry Flagler in 1867, bringing with him the capital of Stephen Harkness. Flagler was a natural negotiator with no scruples about pressing an advantage, and he supplied what Rockefeller lacked in temperament. The two men worked at facing desks, walked to and from the office together, and drafted contracts jointly. Flagler's insight was that a refiner shipping enough volume was not a customer of the railroads but a counterparty, and that guaranteed, regular, full trainloads were worth paying for.

That insight only worked because of where Cleveland sat. Refiners in the Pennsylvania oil regions and in Pittsburgh were effectively captive to the Pennsylvania Railroad. Cleveland had two competing eastern trunk connections plus a water route down Lake Erie and along the Erie Canal in the shipping season. Rockefeller could, and did, play them against one another.

Why he refused to drill

Producing crude was a lottery. A well came in or it did not, and when too many came in the price collapsed. Between 1859 and 1861 crude fell from around sixteen dollars a barrel to about ten cents. Rockefeller watched producers get rich and then ruined on the same acre, and concluded that the money was in the narrow, capital-intensive, chemically demanding middle of the chain. He stayed out of production for more than twenty years and entered only in 1885, when he could do it on his own terms.

Integration as cost war

What Rockefeller did with the Cleveland works between 1865 and 1870 is the part of the story that reads most like an operating manual. He bought the timber tracts, built the kilns to dry the staves so the wood would ship lighter, and made his own barrels for under a dollar when competitors paid two and a half. He bought his own tank cars, his own warehouses, his own boats. He built a plant to recover the sulphuric acid used in treatment instead of dumping it. He hired his own plumbers and bought pipe by the wagonload.

He also refused to waste anything that came out of a barrel. The residues that competitors poured into the Cumberland and the Allegheny became paraffin wax, petroleum jelly, lubricating oils, naphtha, benzene, paint and glue. Every one of those was a small business, and collectively they meant Standard's real cost of a gallon of kerosene was lower than the number a rival could calculate from his own books.

The famous anecdote sits here. Inspecting a canning line, he asked how many drops of solder were used to seal each tin. Forty. He asked them to try thirty-eight. The cans leaked. Thirty-nine held, and the saving on that one change, at Standard's volumes, ran into thousands of dollars a year for decades. Chernow's point is not the thrift itself but the fact that the richest man in the world was still asking that question at that level of detail.

f. 04 · Standard Oil and the Cleveland Massacre1870 – 1872

Standard Oil and the Cleveland Massacre

In six weeks in early 1872 Standard Oil absorbed twenty-two of the twenty-six refineries in its own city. It is the single most consequential episode in the book, and the one that made Rockefeller permanently hated.

On 10 January 1870 the partnership became the Standard Oil Company of Ohio, capitalised at one million dollars in ten thousand shares. Rockefeller took 2,667 of them, the largest single block; the rest went to William Rockefeller, Flagler, Andrews, Harkness and Oliver Jennings. The name was chosen to signal a standard, uniform, non-explosive quality of kerosene at a time when bad refining regularly burned families to death.

Standard was already the largest refiner in the world and still only about a tenth of American capacity. The industry had roughly three times the refining capacity it needed. Prices were collapsing, everyone was losing money, and Rockefeller drew the conclusion he would hold for the rest of his life: an industry with this much overcapacity cannot be fixed by competition, and someone has to consolidate it.

He never used the word monopoly. He called it cooperation, and he described what followed as an act of rescue.

The South Improvement Company

The instrument was a dormant Pennsylvania charter with extraordinarily loose powers. In late 1871 Standard, a small group of allied refiners, and the three trunk railroads, the Pennsylvania, the Erie and the New York Central, signed an agreement that would have restructured the entire industry. Freight rates would roughly double for everyone. Members would receive rebates that put them back where they started or better. And, critically, members would receive drawbacks: a payment on every barrel shipped by a non-member.

It also required the railroads to hand over full waybill information on rivals' shipments, meaning Standard would know what every competitor shipped, where, and to whom.

The scheme collapses, the plan succeeds

The South Improvement Company leaked in February 1872 when a railway clerk published the new rate schedule. The oil regions erupted. Three thousand men packed a hall in Titusville, the producers formed a Petroleum Producers' Union and embargoed shipments to Standard, effigies were burned, Standard's cars were shunted and its property attacked. The railroads capitulated within weeks. Pennsylvania revoked the charter on 2 April 1872. The South Improvement Company never shipped a single barrel of oil.

It did not matter. In the same six or seven weeks, while the fight was still going on, Rockefeller went to every refiner in Cleveland in turn. He did not need the scheme to work; he only needed his competitors to believe it might. He showed them Standard's books, which were far better than theirs. He offered an appraisal, a choice of cash or Standard stock, and the observation that he could survive what was coming and they could not.

Twenty-two of twenty-six Cleveland refiners sold. Standard's capital went from one million to two and a half and then three and a half million dollars. Its share of American refining jumped from about a tenth to about a quarter. Rockefeller was thirty-two.

"The Standard Oil Company will take care of you. You will get your money in ten years and be much better off than you are now."

Rockefeller to a Cleveland refiner, as recalled in the Massacre negotiations
f. 05 · The conquest1872 – 1879

The conquest

Seven years to go from a quarter of American refining to nine tenths of it, mostly by absorbing the men who had beaten him in public.

Cleveland was the template and the rest of the country was the rollout. Between 1874 and 1875 Rockefeller quietly brought in the leading refiners of the three cities that mattered: Charles Lockhart in Pittsburgh, William Warden in Philadelphia, and Charles Pratt with Henry Rogers in New York. These were not weak men being crushed. They were the strongest independents in the country, and the offer was partnership in a combination that would end the price war they were all losing.

The arrangement was kept secret for years. Acquired firms went on trading under their own names, with their old proprietors apparently in charge, while Standard held the stock through nominees. Customers who thought they were buying from a competitor were buying from Standard. Chernow treats this concealment, more than the rebates, as the thing that poisoned the company's reputation permanently: it turned a defensible commercial strategy into something that looked and functioned like a conspiracy.

Breaking the Pennsylvania Railroad, 1877

The last serious challenger to Standard's transport position was Tom Scott's Pennsylvania Railroad, which in 1876 pushed its subsidiary, the Empire Transportation Company, into refining and pipelines. Standard's response was total. It cancelled shipments on the Pennsylvania, cut prices in the markets Empire served, shut its own Pittsburgh refineries to make the point, and shifted traffic to the Erie and the New York Central.

The railroad fought for months and then the great strike of July 1877 tore through its system, burning rolling stock at Pittsburgh. Weakened and bleeding cash, the Pennsylvania sold Empire's refineries, pipelines and boats to Standard in October for around three and a half million dollars. The largest corporation in America had lost a fight with an Ohio refiner who was thirty-eight years old.

The pipeline war

The other threat was technological. In 1879 the Tidewater Pipe Company completed the first long-distance oil pipeline, a hundred and nine miles over the Allegheny mountains to a rail connection at Williamsport, proving that oil could be pumped across a mountain range and bypass the railroads entirely. It was a genuine engineering triumph and a mortal danger to Standard's freight-based advantage.

Standard's answer was not to fight the technology but to adopt it faster and at greater scale, building four trunk lines to the seaboard while simultaneously buying up Tidewater stock, litigating, and pressuring its bankers and customers. By 1883 Tidewater had accepted an agreement giving it a fixed minority share of the trade. Standard's pipeline network, later organised as the National Transit Company, became the cheapest long-haul transport in America and the foundation of the next thirty years.

By the end of the 1870s Standard controlled roughly ninety percent of American refining, most of the pipelines, and the terms on which oil moved. Rockefeller was forty.

f. 06 · The Trust1879 – 1899

The Trust

Standard Oil kept outgrowing the legal forms available to it, so its lawyer invented a new one, and gave the English language a word for the thing everyone would spend the next thirty years trying to destroy.

By 1880 Standard Oil had a structural problem that had nothing to do with its critics. An Ohio corporation could not legally own stock in corporations chartered by other states. Standard had acquired dozens of companies across a dozen states, and the only way to hold them had been to park the shares in the personal names of trusted individuals, held on informal trust. It worked, but it depended on the honesty and continued life of specific men, and it left the company's real structure invisible even to its own shareholders.

Samuel C. T. Dodd, Standard's general counsel, produced the solution on 2 January 1882. Forty-one shareholders conveyed the stock of every constituent company to nine trustees, who held it for their benefit and issued them trust certificates in exchange: seven hundred thousand certificates at a par of one hundred dollars, seventy million dollars in all. Rockefeller held roughly a quarter of them. Dodd, notably, refused to own any Standard stock so that his advice would never be suspect, and he turned down a seat as a trustee.

The word trust had meant a legal relationship of confidence. Within a decade it meant a monopoly, and the laws written to break these arrangements were called antitrust laws. Standard's own device named its executioner.

26 Broadway

The Trust also produced something more interesting than a legal device: a working model of professional corporate management, more than a decade before anyone else had one. Standard moved into 26 Broadway, and the building became shorthand for the company in the way that a street name later stood for a whole industry.

Rockefeller ran it through committees. Each functional area, domestic trade, export, manufacturing, transportation, lubricants, cases and cans, had a standing committee drawing on managers from across the constituent companies. The committees had no formal power to command. They gathered comparable statistics from every plant, circulated the comparisons, and argued. Decisions were reached by discussion until consensus, and Rockefeller almost never overruled anyone. He asked questions, and he waited.

The system's real weapon was information. Standard could tell a refinery superintendent in Baltimore exactly what a barrel cost to produce in Cleveland, and did. It built a statistical department that knew the daily output of nearly every well and refinery in the country, its own and everyone else's. Chernow calls the group of executives it produced the most formidable management team in American business, and notes that half of them had arrived as defeated competitors.

The Lima gamble

In 1885 an enormous field opened at Lima in northwestern Ohio, and the crude was almost useless. It carried so much sulphur that the kerosene stank of rotten eggs and the residue corroded equipment. Nobody could refine it, the price fell to fifteen cents a barrel, and the entire Standard board wanted nothing to do with it.

Rockefeller wanted all of it. He argued that the chemistry problem would be solved and the field would not wait. When the executive committee refused to commit the company's money, he offered to put up three million dollars of his own and let them buy in later if it worked. They gave way. Standard bought oil and land, and stockpiled forty million barrels of crude nobody wanted.

The chemist Herman Frasch found the answer in 1888, using copper oxides to strip out the sulphur. Standard suddenly owned an enormous cheap field, and built the Whiting refinery outside Chicago to process it. It also, for the first time, became a major producer of crude in its own right, ending twenty years of deliberate abstinence. By 1891 Standard was producing a quarter of American crude.

Chernow uses Lima to complicate the caricature. The man remembered for grinding down competitors was also capable of betting his own money against his entire board's judgment on an unsolved chemistry problem, and waiting three years to be proved right.

Ohio dissolves the Trust, and nothing changes

In 1890 Congress passed the Sherman Antitrust Act. In 1892, on a suit by the Ohio attorney general David Watson, the Ohio Supreme Court ordered the Standard Oil Trust dissolved. Standard complied in form. The trust certificates were exchanged for proportional interests in the twenty constituent companies, so that a man who had held one percent of the Trust now held one percent of each of the twenty. The same nine men still controlled all of them.

The result was less accountable rather than more. Then in 1899 New Jersey's permissive new corporation law allowed Standard to do openly what it had been doing covertly: Standard Oil of New Jersey became the holding company for the entire system, with its capital raised from ten to a hundred and ten million dollars. Rockefeller remained its president in name until 1911, though he had withdrawn from daily management around 1896 and John Archbold had been running the company for years.

f. 07 · Enemies1881 – 1904

Enemies

He answered nothing for thirty years, on principle, and by the time he understood what that silence had cost him it was far too late to speak.

Rockefeller's policy toward criticism was absolute and consistent: never respond, never explain, never sue, never grant an interview. He believed that answering an accusation dignified it, that the record would vindicate him eventually, and that a man who was right did not need to argue. It was, Chernow says, the single most catastrophic business decision of his life. Into a thirty-year vacuum his enemies poured a portrait that has never been dislodged.

The first serious attack came from Henry Demarest Lloyd, whose 1881 Atlantic Monthly article The Story of a Great Monopoly sold out seven printings, and whose 1894 book Wealth Against Commonwealth made Standard the emblem of everything wrong with industrial capitalism. Lloyd never named Rockefeller in the book's title or index but the target was unmistakable.

The most damaging was Ida Tarbell. Her father Franklin had been a Titusville tank builder and independent oil man broken in the aftermath of 1872; a family friend who had borrowed to compete killed himself. She spent five years on the research, working from court records, government investigations, and the testimony George Rice had spent two decades accumulating. Nineteen instalments ran in McClure's from November 1902 to October 1904. It is meticulous, largely accurate on the facts, and devastating.

Chernow's assessment is careful. He judges Tarbell's account of the business methods essentially sound and her portrait of the man unfair: she made him a cold hypocrite, dwelt on his physical appearance, and dismissed his philanthropy as conscience money. The most quoted line about Rockefeller's face, that it was the face of a man with no scruples and a certain moldy hunger, is hers. Rockefeller's only recorded response was to call her Miss Tarbarrel and refuse to discuss it. Privately, decades later with Inglis, he went through her charges line by line and was still furious.

"Not a word! Not a word about that misguided woman."

Rockefeller, when asked about Ida Tarbell

Politics, and the letters that surfaced

Standard's involvement in politics was extensive and, for a long time, deniable. In 1884 Henry B. Payne, father of Standard's treasurer, won an Ohio Senate seat under circumstances so obviously purchased that the Senate investigated. Standard money moved through state legislatures wherever oil legislation was pending.

It became undeniable in 1908, when William Randolph Hearst read aloud at a rally a set of letters from John Archbold to senators and congressmen, obtained by bribing a Standard office boy, discussing payments and legislative favours in language nobody could explain away. Standard's cultivated air of injured innocence did not survive them.

f. 08 · The world closes in1885 – 1911

The world closes in

While America argued about the monopoly, four things were dismantling it that had nothing to do with the law: Russian oil, Asian oil, Texan oil, and the electric light bulb.

Standard's dominance was always national, never global, and its export trade was two thirds of its business. From the 1880s onward the export position came under sustained attack. The Nobel brothers and then the Rothschilds developed the Baku fields on the Caspian, closer to European and Asian markets than Pennsylvania was. Marcus Samuel, a London shell merchant, built bulk tankers that could pass through the Suez Canal and undercut Standard's five-gallon tins across Asia; his company took its name from the shells. Royal Dutch developed Sumatra. In 1907 Shell and Royal Dutch merged under Henri Deterding into the only rival with genuine global reach.

At home, the geography changed. Spindletop blew in at Beaumont, Texas, in January 1901, at a rate that dwarfed anything Pennsylvania had produced, and Standard was not there. Gulf Oil, the Texas Company and Sun grew up outside its control, protected by Texan hostility to the trust. California and then Oklahoma did the same. By 1911 Standard's share of refining had fallen to around two thirds without a court lifting a finger.

And the core product was dying. Edison's incandescent lamp, first demonstrated in 1879 and spreading through cities from the late 1880s, was aimed precisely at the market Standard existed to serve. Rockefeller's fortune had been built on lighting the world with kerosene, and electricity was going to take that away.

What saved it was the waste product. Gasoline had been a dangerous nuisance, dumped in creeks and burned off, until the internal combustion engine turned it into the most valuable thing in the barrel. American refinery output of gasoline passed kerosene at almost exactly the moment the Supreme Court broke the company up. Standard's successors inherited a demand curve that was about to go vertical.

f. 09 · The reckoning1906 – 1911

The reckoning

The government won the biggest antitrust case in history and made Rockefeller considerably richer within eighteen months.

Theodore Roosevelt created the Bureau of Corporations in 1903, and its 1906 report on Standard Oil's transportation practices supplied the documentary spine of the case. On 15 November 1906 the federal government filed suit in the Circuit Court for the Eastern District of Missouri, alleging that Standard Oil of New Jersey and sixty-five subsidiaries were an illegal combination in restraint of trade under the Sherman Act.

Meanwhile, in Chicago, Judge Kenesaw Mountain Landis fined Standard Oil of Indiana $29,240,000 in August 1907 for taking rebates on 1,462 carloads of oil, the largest fine in American history to that point and more than the company's capitalisation. Rockefeller received the news on a golf course, played on, and had a lawyer note that the judge would be dead a long time before Standard paid it. The fine was thrown out on appeal the following year. The headline was never thrown out.

Rockefeller himself testified in the government's case, and Chernow's account of it is one of the book's best set pieces. He was a superb witness: courteous, foggy, forgetful in exactly the right places, unable to recall the details of arrangements he had personally designed. He charmed the room and conceded nothing.

15 May 1911

The Supreme Court, Chief Justice Edward Douglass White writing, affirmed the lower court and ordered Standard Oil of New Jersey to divest its subsidiaries within six months. The decision also introduced the rule of reason: not every restraint of trade violates the Sherman Act, only unreasonable ones. That doctrine, which sounded like a loss for the trustbusters, has governed American antitrust ever since.

Standard was divided into thirty-four companies. Because every shareholder received a proportional interest in each successor, Rockefeller now owned roughly a quarter of thirty-four separate oil companies instead of a quarter of one. The market immediately noticed something the government had not: as independent entities, no longer obliged to hide their earnings inside a single opaque parent, the pieces were worth far more than the whole. Within a year the successor shares had roughly doubled.

"Father Lennon, have you any money? Buy Standard Oil."

Rockefeller, on the golf course at Pocantico, on being told of the Court's decision
f. 10 · The private man1864 – 1915

The private man

A household of extraordinary warmth and extraordinary control, run on the assumption that the money was a danger to everyone in it.

Rockefeller married Laura Celestia Spelman on 8 September 1864. She came from a Cleveland abolitionist family whose house had been a stop on the Underground Railroad, had been valedictorian of her class, and was if anything stricter in her piety than he was. For thirty years she was his closest adviser; he showed her contracts and discussed strategy with her, and by his own account never made a major decision without her.

They had five children. Bessie, born 1866; Alice, born 1869, who died before her first birthday; Alta in 1871; Edith in 1872; and John Davison Jr. in 1874. Laura's unmarried sister Lucy, called Aunt Lute, lived with them and shared the raising of the children.

Raising children against money

The regime was deliberate and, by any modern standard, strange. The children were not told they were rich. They wore hand-me-downs, John Jr. wearing his sisters' dresses as a small boy. There was one bicycle among four children. They kept individual account books and were required to record every penny, exactly as their father had at sixteen, and to show them.

They earned money on a published schedule: cents for killing flies, sharpening pencils, practising music, weeding, abstaining from sweets. Alcohol, tobacco, dancing and the theatre were forbidden. The house was full of games, singing and affection, and it was also a closed system designed to inoculate its inhabitants against the world their father had built.

The results were mixed. John Jr. emerged conscientious to the point of paralysis, suffered a breakdown in 1904, and spent his life on the moral rehabilitation of the family name. Edith went the other way entirely: married into International Harvester, moved to Zurich, was analysed by Carl Jung and then practised as an analyst herself, and spent on a scale that shocked her father. Bessie married a philosopher and lived in poor health abroad, dying in 1906. Alta, partly deaf, stayed closest to home.

Cettie declined into invalidism after 1900, spending her last fifteen years largely confined, and died on 12 March 1915. Rockefeller could not attend her funeral in Cleveland immediately because of an outstanding Ohio tax matter that risked his arrest, a detail Chernow uses to sum up the cost of the life he had chosen.

The body

In the early 1890s, at the peak of his power, Rockefeller collapsed. Digestive trouble, exhaustion and depression forced him out of the office for long stretches; he lived for a period on milk and crackers and relied on the homeopath Hamilton Biggar, who prescribed golf and rest as much as medicine. Then in 1901, at sixty-one, generalised alopecia took every hair on his body, eyebrows and eyelashes included. He wore skullcaps at first, then a rotating set of wigs of graduated lengths so that his hair appeared to grow between them. The photographs from these years, of a bald, skeletal, unreadable old man, are the ones that fixed his image as a ghoul at precisely the moment Tarbell was writing.

He then lived another thirty-six years, better than he had for the previous ten.

f. 11 · The giving1855 – 1937

The giving

He invented the modern foundation for the same reason he invented the trust: the existing structures could not hold what he had built.

The giving was not a late conversion. Ledger A records charitable donations from his first wages in 1855, when he was sixteen and earning fifty cents a day. By his twenties he was tithing above ten percent, mostly to Baptist causes, black churches and schools in the South, and individual hardship cases. He paid off the mortgage on his own struggling mission church. He funded Spelman Seminary, later Spelman College, named for his wife's family.

By the 1880s the system was breaking down. Requests arrived by the thousand every week, personally addressed, and he tried to read them. He was, in the phrase Chernow quotes, being hunted like a wild animal by supplicants. The money was compounding faster than any human being could give it away one letter at a time.

Gates

The answer arrived in 1891 in the form of Frederick Taylor Gates, a Baptist minister who had impressed him during the founding of the University of Chicago. Gates became the chief of staff of the fortune, running both the philanthropy and, increasingly, the investments. He it was who bought the distressed Mesabi iron ore ranges in Minnesota, assembled a fleet of ore ships, and then sold the whole thing to J. P. Morgan's US Steel in 1901 at an enormous profit, a transaction that has almost nothing to do with oil and made Rockefeller a great deal of money.

Gates's central argument, delivered with force, was that the fortune had become a physical danger to the family.

"Your fortune is rolling up, rolling up like an avalanche! You must distribute it faster than it grows! If you do not, it will crush you and your children and your children's children."

Frederick T. Gates to Rockefeller

Together they articulated what Gates called scientific philanthropy, and its central rule was wholesale, not retail. Do not relieve the symptom; fund the institution that eliminates the cause. Do not give to a hospital; build the research institute that makes the disease treatable. Do not endow a college out of sentiment; find the man who can build a university and give him the means. And do not run it yourself: hire the best available expert, give them the money and independence, and then leave them alone.

That last principle was genuinely novel, and it is the direct ancestor of every large foundation since.

What it built

1889 · $35m and more
The University of Chicago

Built from nothing under William Rainey Harper into a first-rank research university in about fifteen years. Rockefeller called it the best investment he ever made, and deliberately kept his name off it and his hands out of its academic affairs.

1901
Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research

America's first institution dedicated purely to medical research, under Simon Flexner. Produced a meningitis serum within a decade, and later the work of Landsteiner, Carrel, Rous, and Avery's identification of DNA as the carrier of heredity. Now Rockefeller University. Prompted partly by the death of a grandchild from scarlet fever.

1903
General Education Board

Aimed at American schooling, particularly in the impoverished South, funding high schools, teacher training and black colleges. Its record is genuinely mixed: it accommodated segregation and steered black education toward vocational training, a compromise it was criticised for at the time and since.

1909
Sanitary Commission for the Eradication of Hookworm

Under Wickliffe Rose. Identified and treated hookworm across eleven Southern states, transforming the health and productivity of a region that had been written off as constitutionally lazy. The model for the Foundation's later global public health work, including yellow fever.

1913
The Rockefeller Foundation

Chartered in New York after Congress spent three years refusing a federal charter on the grounds that it was an attempt to launder the reputation of a convicted monopolist. Endowed with over $180 million and instructed simply to promote the well-being of mankind throughout the world.

1918
Laura Spelman Rockefeller Memorial

Created in his wife's memory, $74 million, and the most important early funder of the social sciences in America before it was folded into the Foundation.

f. 12 · Ludlow and the public face1913 – 1915

Ludlow and the public face

The worst moment in the family's history happened at a coal company in Colorado that Rockefeller had barely thought about, and it fell on his son.

The Rockefellers held a controlling interest in the Colorado Fuel and Iron Company, a coal and steel operation fourteen hundred miles from New York, run by managers they never questioned. In September 1913 the miners struck for union recognition, an eight-hour day, and the right to shop somewhere other than the company store. They were evicted from company housing and spent the winter in tent colonies on the plains.

On 20 April 1914 the Colorado National Guard and company guards attacked the largest camp at Ludlow. In the fighting and the fire that followed, roughly twenty people died, including two women and eleven children who suffocated in a pit dug beneath a tent. A ten-day guerrilla war followed across the coalfields before federal troops arrived.

John D. Jr. had assured a congressional committee, on the basis of what his managers told him, that the men were not on strike and that outside agitators were responsible. He was, in Chernow's account, sincere and completely wrong, and his testimony after Ludlow was a public humiliation. The name Rockefeller became attached to dead children.

Chernow treats Ludlow as the hinge of the second half of the story. It broke John Jr. out of his father's shadow and set the direction of his life: labour relations, public accountability, and philanthropy as the family's business. It also produced, in Ivy Lee, the machinery that within a decade had converted the most hated man in America into a benign old gentleman handing out dimes on a golf course.

f. 13 · The long ending1911 – 1937

The long ending

He outlived the trust, the muckrakers, the war, the boom and most of the crash, and turned the last quarter century into a public performance of harmless old age.

Rockefeller was seventy-one when the Supreme Court broke up his company and he lived another twenty-six years. He spent them at Pocantico, Lakewood in New Jersey, and increasingly at Ormond Beach in Florida, on an unvarying schedule of golf, drives, music and naps. He transferred hundreds of millions to John Jr. and to the foundations, so that the enormous fortune drained steadily away by design.

The dimes became the defining image. What began as an unforced habit was industrialised under Ivy Lee: pre-counted rolls carried by an aide, dimes for adults, nickels for children, distributed to caddies, reporters, passers-by and visiting dignitaries, always with a small homily about thrift. It worked. Newspapers that had called him the greatest criminal of the age ran affectionate features about his golf handicap.

Between 1917 and 1920 he dictated his recollections to William Inglis, who read Tarbell's accusations to him one at a time. It is the fullest defence he ever offered, and he never allowed it to be published. It sat in the archive until Chernow used it.

He made no adjustment for the 1929 crash beyond a single public statement that he and his son were buying common stocks, which briefly lifted the market and was widely mocked. He watched his contemporaries die, kept his diet, and pursued an openly stated goal of living to one hundred.

He died at Ormond Beach on 23 May 1937, seven weeks short of his ninety-eighth birthday. He was buried at Lake View Cemetery in Cleveland, beside Cettie and his mother, a few hundred yards from the graves of some of the men he had bought out in 1872.

f. 14 · Chernow's verdictThe argument of the book

Chernow's verdict

The book's thesis is that the contradiction is not a puzzle to be solved. It is the man.

Two biographical traditions had competed for eighty years. Ida Tarbell's Rockefeller is a cold, pious hypocrite whose charity is a laundering operation. Allan Nevins's authorised Rockefeller, written with family cooperation in 1940 and revised in 1953, is a misunderstood organiser who rescued a chaotic industry and was libelled for it. Chernow's position is that both accounts describe real behaviour and both fail because they assume a person has to be one thing.

His Rockefeller is genuinely religious and genuinely predatory, and the same underlying traits produce both. The patience that let him wait three years for Frasch to solve the sulphur problem is the patience that let him wait out a competitor's bankruptcy. The secrecy that concealed Standard's ownership of a dozen apparently independent firms is the secrecy with which he gave anonymously for decades. The refusal to answer Tarbell is the same refusal to justify himself to anyone, which in a devout man reads as humility and in a monopolist reads as contempt.

The five arguments the book makes

  1. He was an organiser before he was a monopolist. The industry in 1870 had three times the refining capacity it needed and was destroying itself. Consolidation of some kind was coming. The question the book leaves open is how much of what he did was necessary to achieve it.
  2. The methods matter more than the outcome. Kerosene did get cheaper and better, and Standard did carry it to the whole world. But rebates, drawbacks, secret ownership, waybill intelligence and predatory local pricing were not incidental to that achievement, they were the mechanism, and Chernow does not let the result excuse them.
  3. The silence was the fatal error. Thirty years of refusing to answer left the entire public record to his enemies, and no amount of later philanthropy or public relations recovered it.
  4. He invented two things that outlived the company. Modern professional corporate management, built at 26 Broadway on comparable statistics and functional committees, and the modern foundation, built with Gates on the principle of funding institutions rather than relieving symptoms.
  5. The state arrived late and was largely irrelevant to the outcome. By 1911 Texas, California, Russia, Sumatra and the internal combustion engine had already changed the industry beyond Standard's control. The Court's decision made its owner richer and its successors dominant for another eighty years.

The last line

What Chernow will not do is total the ledger. The hookworm campaign, the yellow fever work, the meningitis serum, a university built from nothing, and the model that most large philanthropy still follows are set against the wrecked refiners, the bought legislators, the sabotaged Buffalo plant and the tent colony at Ludlow. The book presents both columns in full and declines to strike a balance, on the grounds that the man himself never felt the need to.

"I believe the power to make money is a gift from God, to be developed and used to the best of our ability for the good of mankind. Having been endowed with the gift I possess, I believe it is my duty to make money and still more money, and to use the money I make for the good of my fellow man according to the dictates of my conscience."
