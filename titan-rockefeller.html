Titan

The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr.

“All my future seemed to hinge on that day, and I often tremble when I ask myself the question: what if I had not got the job?”

Rockefeller, remembering 26 September 1855

Ron Chernow · 1998

The whole book in one breath

A sixteen-year-old bookkeeper who tithed from his first wage became, in seventeen years, the master of nine-tenths of American oil refining, by consolidation, secret railroad deals and a management machine nobody else had. The state spent thirty years trying to break what he built and finally did in 1911, which roughly tripled his fortune. He then gave away more money than anyone before him, through a new kind of institution he invented for the purpose. Chernow’s argument is that the saint and the monopolist were not a mask and a face. The same patience, secrecy and certainty of being right produced both, and the book refuses to strike a balance between them because Rockefeller never felt the need to.

The book itself

The most written-about businessman in America, and the least known

Rockefeller gave almost no interviews, testified evasively, and left a public image made half by his enemies and half by his publicists. Two traditions had hardened: the muckrakers’ monster, fixed by Ida Tarbell in 1904, and the misunderstood organiser of Allan Nevins’s authorised studies of 1940 and 1953. Chernow’s advantage was archival. Above all he used the reminiscences Rockefeller dictated to the journalist William O. Inglis between 1917 and 1920, when Inglis read Tarbell’s charges aloud line by line and wrote down the old man’s replies. It is the nearest thing to a Rockefeller confession, and it had barely been used.

What comes out is not a verdict between the two traditions but an argument that both described real things. The devotion and the predation were not a mask and a face; the same temperament produced both.

  • Three strands, braided. The business story runs from Cleveland in 1863 to the Supreme Court in 1911. The family story runs alongside it and is often the more disturbing. The giving starts as a boy’s habit and ends as the invention of the modern foundation.
  • Where the density is. If you listen rather than read, the stretches that repay attention are the consolidation of 1872–82 and the collapse of his reputation in 1900–14. That is where the mechanics are explained.
  • What it is not. Not a business history of oil (technology and the producers are sketched), and not an economic assessment: Chernow states the case that Standard cut prices and rationalised a chaotic industry, and the case that it did so by coercive and secret means, without economic apparatus to settle it. Its centre is character.

If you remember nothing else

Twelve things to keep

Character

Two parents, two halves

His mother gave him the discipline and the faith; his father gave him the poker face and the habit of concealment. They never merged.

Strategy

Stay out of the lottery

Drilling was boom and bust. He took the narrow, capital-hungry middle of the chain, refining, where scale and freight decided everything.

Cost

Own every input, waste nothing

Barrels, tank cars, acid, pipe and every by-product in the barrel. A rival could not see Standard’s true cost from his own books.

Leverage

Volume is a weapon

A shipper with enough traffic is a counterparty, not a customer. Cleveland’s three routes let him auction his trainloads.

The line he crossed

The drawback

A rebate is a volume discount. A payment on every barrel a rival shipped is not defensible on any theory, and it turned opinion for good.

Consolidation

Buy the losers, keep the best

He called it cooperation. Most of Standard’s senior men arrived as competitors who were bought, not beaten.

Secrecy

Hidden ownership poisoned everything

Firms kept their old names while Standard owned them. Concealment, more than rebates, made a strategy look like a conspiracy.

Organisation

He invented modern management

Committees, comparable cost statistics and decision by consensus at 26 Broadway, a decade before anyone else.

Reputation

Silence was the fatal error

Thirty years of never answering handed the whole public record to his enemies.

The market

The monopoly was eroding anyway

Texas, California, Russia, Sumatra and the electric light cut Standard’s share before any court did.

1911

The break-up made him richer

Thirty-four transparent companies were worth far more to the market than one opaque one.

Giving

Wholesale, not retail

Fund the institution that removes the cause, hire the best expert, and leave them alone: the model of the modern foundation.

Who’s who

Cast of characters

Chernow keeps about two dozen people in play. These carry most of the action. Notice the middle group: nearly every senior Standard man arrived as a rival who was bought rather than beaten.

Inside the family

Father

William Avery Rockefeller

“Devil Bill.” Pedlar of patent cures, absent for months, from 1855 a bigamist living as Dr William Levingston. Taught his son to bargain hard and trust no one.

Mother

Eliza Davison Rockefeller

Devout, thrifty, severe, and often left alone with the children. The source of the discipline, the faith and the tolerance for silence.

Wife

Laura “Cettie” Spelman

Married 1864. From an abolitionist family, and more pious than he was. His closest counsel for decades.

Brother

William Rockefeller

Standard’s New York and export man. Sociable where John was not, and drawn into the Amalgamated Copper stock scheme.

Brother

Frank Rockefeller

Sold out, felt cheated, and feuded for life; he moved his two dead children from the family plot rather than have them near John.

Son

John D. Rockefeller, Jr.

The only son. Scrupulous to the point of illness, and spent his life turning the fortune into institutions and the name into respectability.

Partner

Henry M. Flagler

Joined 1867. The negotiator behind the railroad rebate strategy; later built Florida’s east-coast railway and Palm Beach.

Partner

Samuel Andrews

The mechanic who actually knew how to refine. Bought out in 1878 for a million dollars, and bitter about it afterwards.

Capital

Stephen V. Harkness

The silent money behind Flagler, whose investment in 1867 made the expansion possible.

Rival to ally

Oliver H. Payne

Cleveland’s largest competitor. Sold in 1872, took stock, became Standard’s treasurer and very rich.

Rival to ally

Brooklyn’s leading refiner, brought secretly inside in 1874 with his Astral Oil brand.

Enforcer

Brilliant, charming and ruthless; the executive later exposed in the Amalgamated Copper affair.

A young Titusville refiner who ran Standard from the late 1890s. His letters to politicians, published in 1908, were the scandal that stuck.

The life at a glance

Ninety-seven years, seventeen of them decisive

Everything that made Standard Oil unbeatable happened between 1865 and 1882, when he was 25 to 42. He then spent fifty-five more years as its owner, its defendant and its donor.

1839–1855

Devil Bill and the Making of a Baptist

The two halves of his character were installed before he was sixteen, one by each parent.

  • Born 8 July 1839 in Richford, in the southern tier of New York. His father, William Avery Rockefeller, was big, genial and seldom home: he sold patent medicines, traded horses, lent money at hard rates, and later posed as a cancer specialist.
  • From 1855 “Devil Bill” was also a bigamist, keeping a second wife in Ontario as Dr William Levingston until he died in 1906. His famous son spent decades quietly containing the story while newspapers hunted for it.
  • His mother, Eliza Davison, ran the house on strict Baptist discipline and hard thrift. Her son repeated her line all his life: wilful waste makes woeful want.
  • Chernow’s reading: from his mother he learned endurance and a moral scaffolding; from his father, negotiation, concealment, and how easily a smiling man takes another man’s money. His father is said to have boasted that he cheated his boys whenever he could, to make them sharp.
  • The family moved often, a step ahead of gossip and creditors, reaching Strongsville near Cleveland in 1853. A few months at a commercial college, learning bookkeeping, mattered more than high school.
  • He organised his life as a refutation of his father: no drink, no gambling, no show, no debt he could not cover, one wife, everything recorded. Yet the poker face, the concealed ownership and the ease with ruthlessness were, in this reading, Bill’s gifts in Eliza’s clothes.

1855–1863

Job Day and Ledger A

He treated the day he found work as more important than his birthday for the next eighty years.

  • In the summer of 1855 he listed Cleveland’s substantial firms and called on each, again and again, for six weeks. On 26 September Hewitt & Tuttle took him on as an assistant bookkeeper. Nobody mentioned pay; after three months they gave him fifty dollars.
  • He loved the ledgers as both a moral and a technical instrument, checked every freight bill line by line, and caught errors others waved through.
  • He kept his own: Ledger A, every cent earned and spent from 1855. It recorded charitable giving from the first wage, and in old age he still read from it with visible emotion.
  • In 1859, aged nineteen, he formed Clark & Rockefeller, produce merchants. His father lent him the balance of his stake at ten per cent, and sometimes demanded it back at awkward moments to test him.
  • The Civil War made the firm. He paid for a substitute, as many prosperous Northerners legally did; Chernow finds his later justification a little too smooth.
  • That August, Edwin Drake struck oil at Titusville, Pennsylvania. Within three years the valley was a forest of derricks and a chaotic new business needed exactly what he had: capital, connections and an accountant’s temperament.

1863–1870

The Refiner

He never wanted to drill. He wanted the one stage where scale, chemistry and freight rates decided the outcome.

  • In 1863 he and Clark backed Samuel Andrews, an English mechanic who could distil kerosene cleanly, and built the Excelsior Works beside a railway line in Cleveland.
  • When the Clarks wanted to slow down, the partners auctioned the business between themselves on 2 February 1865. He won at $72,500, aged twenty-five, and called it the day that determined his career.
  • Henry Flagler arrived in 1867 with Stephen Harkness’s capital. His insight was that a refiner shipping enough volume was the railroads’ counterparty, not their customer.
  • That only worked because of where Cleveland sat (see the first figure below). Drilling he left alone for twenty years: in the first years of the industry the price of crude swung from about sixteen dollars a barrel to less than fifty cents (see the second).
  • He integrated in every direction that cut cost: timber, kilns and his own barrels at under a dollar where rivals paid about two and a half; tank cars, warehouses, boats; a plant to recover the sulphuric acid he used.
  • Nothing in the barrel was wasted. Residues rivals poured into rivers became wax, petroleum jelly, lubricants, naphtha and more, so his real cost of kerosene was lower than any rival could calculate.
  • The best-known anecdote: he asked how many drops of solder sealed each can. Forty. Try thirty-eight: they leaked. Thirty-nine held, and the saving ran to thousands of dollars a year.

1870–1872

Standard Oil and the Cleveland Massacre

In about six weeks of 1872 Standard absorbed twenty-two of the twenty-six refineries in its own city.

  • On 10 January 1870 the partnership became the Standard Oil Company of Ohio, with a million dollars of capital; Rockefeller held the largest block. The name promised a uniform, safe kerosene when bad refining still burned families to death.
  • Standard was already the world’s largest refiner, yet held only about a tenth of American capacity. The industry could refine about three times the oil it needed, and everyone was losing money. His conclusion, held for life: overcapacity this bad cannot be cured by competition. He never said monopoly. He said cooperation, and called it a rescue.
  • The instrument was the South Improvement Company, a dormant Pennsylvania charter. With a few allied refiners and the three trunk railroads, it would have doubled freight rates for everyone, rebated them to members, paid members a drawback on every barrel a non-member shipped, and handed them rivals’ waybills (see the figure below).
  • The scheme leaked in February 1872. The oil regions erupted, producers embargoed Standard, and Pennsylvania revoked the charter that April. It never shipped a barrel.
  • It did not matter. While the fight raged he visited every Cleveland refiner, showed them Standard’s books, and offered an appraisal in cash or stock, with the observation that he could survive what was coming and they could not. Standard’s share of American refining jumped from about a tenth to about a quarter. He was thirty-two.
  • What each seller chose decided how history remembers it (see the second figure).

1872–1879

The Conquest

Seven years from a quarter of American refining to about nine-tenths, mostly by absorbing the men who had fought him in public.

  • In 1874–75 he quietly brought in the leading refiners of the cities that mattered: Charles Lockhart in Pittsburgh, William Warden in Philadelphia, Charles Pratt with Henry Rogers in New York. They were the strongest independents, offered partnership in a combination that would end a price war they were all losing.
  • The arrangement was secret for years. Acquired firms kept their names and apparent owners while Standard held the stock through nominees, so customers who thought they had a choice did not. Chernow sees this, more than the rebates, as what poisoned the company’s name.
  • In 1876–77 Tom Scott’s Pennsylvania Railroad pushed its Empire Transportation Company into refining. Standard pulled its traffic, cut prices in Empire’s markets and shut its own Pittsburgh plants to make the point. Weakened further by the great strike of July 1877, the railroad sold Empire to Standard that October.
  • In 1879 the Tidewater Pipe Company pumped oil over the Alleghenies to a railhead, about 110 miles, proving oil could bypass the railroads. Standard adopted the technology faster and bigger, bought into Tidewater, and by 1883 had pinned it to a fixed minority of the trade.
  • By the end of the 1870s Standard controlled about 90% of American refining, most of the pipelines, and the terms on which oil moved. He was forty.

1879–1899

The Trust

Standard kept outgrowing the legal forms available to it, so its lawyer invented one and gave the language a word.

  • An Ohio company could not own shares in companies chartered elsewhere, so the empire’s stock sat in the names of trusted individuals. On 2 January 1882 Samuel Dodd’s solution conveyed it all to nine trustees, who issued trust certificates worth $70 million to the shareholders. Rockefeller held about a quarter.
  • “Trust” had meant a relationship of confidence. Within a decade it meant monopoly, and the laws to break such things were called antitrust. Standard’s own device named its executioner.
  • At 26 Broadway Rockefeller built modern management: standing committees for each function, comparable cost statistics from every plant, a department that knew the output of nearly every well and refinery in the country, and decisions reached by discussion. He rarely overruled anyone. He asked questions, and waited.
  • The Lima gamble, 1885. A vast Ohio field produced crude so sulphurous nobody could refine it. His board refused to commit; he offered three million dollars of his own. Herman Frasch cracked the chemistry in 1888, the Whiting refinery followed, and Standard became a major producer of crude for the first time.
  • In 1892 Ohio’s supreme court ordered the Trust dissolved. Certificates became proportional shares in twenty companies, controlled by the same men. In 1899 New Jersey’s new corporation law let Standard Oil of New Jersey hold the lot openly, with $110 million of capital. Rockefeller had stepped back from daily work around 1896; John Archbold ran it.

1881–1908

Enemies

He answered nothing for thirty years, on principle, and by the time he saw what the silence cost it was too late.

  • His rule never changed: never respond, never explain, never sue, never give an interview. Answering dignified an attack; the record would vindicate him. Chernow calls it the most catastrophic business decision of his life.
  • Henry Demarest Lloyd made the first serious case in the Atlantic in 1881, and made Standard the emblem of industrial wrongdoing in Wealth Against Commonwealth in 1894.
  • Ida Tarbell did the damage. Her father’s business had suffered in the 1872 oil war. She worked from court records, government reports and George Rice’s twenty years of evidence, and ran nineteen instalments in McClure’s from November 1902. Chernow judges her account of the methods essentially sound and her portrait of the man unfair: cold, hypocritical, his giving dismissed as conscience money.
  • In public he said nothing. Privately, with Inglis years later, he went through her charges one by one and was still furious.
  • Standard’s politics were extensive and, for a long time, deniable. In 1908 William Randolph Hearst read out letters from Archbold to senators and congressmen, stolen by an office boy, discussing payments and favours. The air of injured innocence did not survive them.

1885–1911

The World Closes In

While America argued about the monopoly, four things were dismantling it that had nothing to do with the law.

  • Russian oil. The Nobels and then the Rothschilds developed Baku, nearer Europe and Asia than Pennsylvania was. Standard’s export trade was about two-thirds of its business.
  • Asian oil and the tanker. Marcus Samuel’s bulk tankers went through the Suez Canal and undercut Standard’s tins across Asia; his company took its name from the shells his family traded. Royal Dutch developed Sumatra, and in 1907 the two merged into the one rival with global reach.
  • Texas. Spindletop blew in at Beaumont in January 1901, and Standard was not there. Gulf, the Texas Company and Sun grew up outside its control; California and Oklahoma followed. By 1911 Standard’s share of refining was down to about two-thirds with no court involved.
  • The light bulb. Edison’s lamp went after exactly the market Standard existed to serve. What saved the fortune was a waste product: gasoline, dumped for decades, became the most valuable thing in the barrel once the car arrived, overtaking kerosene around the time of the break-up.

1906–1911

The Reckoning

The government won the largest antitrust case in history and made Rockefeller far richer within two years.

  • Roosevelt’s Bureau of Corporations reported on Standard’s transport practices in 1906, and in November the federal government sued Standard Oil of New Jersey and its subsidiaries under the Sherman Act.
  • In August 1907 Judge Landis fined Standard of Indiana $29.24 million for taking rebates on 1,462 carloads of oil, the largest fine in American history to that date. It was thrown out on appeal. The headline was not.
  • Rockefeller testified in the government case and was, in Chernow’s telling, a superb witness: courteous, foggy, unable to recall arrangements he had personally designed.
  • On 15 May 1911 the Supreme Court, Chief Justice White writing, ordered the break-up. It also announced the “rule of reason”: only unreasonable restraints of trade break the law. That doctrine has governed American antitrust ever since.
  • Standard became thirty-four companies, and every shareholder got a proportional stake in each. Freed from one opaque parent, the pieces were worth far more to the market. Told the news on a golf course, the story goes, he advised the priest he was playing with to buy Standard Oil.

The family

The Private Man

A household of great warmth and total control, run on the assumption that the money was a danger to everyone in it.

  • He married Laura Celestia Spelman on 8 September 1864. She came from an abolitionist family whose house had sheltered people escaping slavery, and she was his closest adviser for thirty years.
  • Five children: Bessie (1866), Alice (1869, died in infancy), Alta (1871), Edith (1872) and John Jr. (1874).
  • The children were not told they were rich. They wore hand-me-downs, shared one bicycle among four, kept account books like his, and earned cents on a published schedule for chores and for going without sweets.
  • The results were mixed. John Jr. broke down in 1904 and spent his life on the family’s rehabilitation. Edith married into International Harvester, moved to Zurich, was analysed by Carl Jung and then practised analysis herself, spending on a scale that shocked her father.
  • In the early 1890s, at the height of his power, his health collapsed and he withdrew from daily work. In 1901 alopecia took every hair on his body; the bald, gaunt photographs of those years fixed his image as a ghoul just as Tarbell was writing. He lived thirty-six more years, healthier than he had been for the previous ten.
  • Cettie declined into invalidism and died on 12 March 1915.

1855–1937

The Giving

He invented the modern foundation for the reason he invented the trust: the old structures could not hold what he had built.

  • The giving was not a late conversion. Ledger A records it from his first wage, and by his twenties he gave more than a tenth, mostly to Baptist causes, black churches and schools, and people in need. He funded what became Spelman College, named for his wife’s family.
  • By the 1880s thousands of begging letters arrived each week, and the fortune compounded faster than anyone could give it away one letter at a time.
  • In 1891 Frederick Gates, a Baptist minister, became chief of staff of the fortune. He warned that it was rolling up like an avalanche and would crush the family unless it was given away faster than it grew. He also made money: the Mesabi iron ranges he assembled were sold to J. P. Morgan’s US Steel in 1901.
  • Their doctrine was wholesale, not retail. Fund the institution that removes the cause, not the relief of the symptom. Find the best expert, give them money and independence, and leave them alone. That last principle was new, and every large foundation since descends from it.
  • What it built: the University of Chicago (from 1889); the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research (1901), now Rockefeller University; the General Education Board (1902–03); the Sanitary Commission that fought hookworm across the South (1909); the Rockefeller Foundation (1913), chartered in New York after Congress refused; and the Laura Spelman Rockefeller Memorial (1918).
  • Chernow’s judgement: neither the laundering operation critics claimed nor the simple piety he claimed. In cold terms it was the most productive use of capital in his life. The hookworm and yellow-fever campaigns alone changed more lives than Standard Oil did.

1913–1915

Ludlow and the Public Face

The family’s worst moment happened at a Colorado coal company it barely thought about, and it fell on the son.

  • The Rockefellers controlled Colorado Fuel and Iron, run by managers they never questioned. In September 1913 its miners struck for recognition, an eight-hour day and freedom from the company store, and spent the winter in tent colonies.
  • On 20 April 1914 the militia and company guards attacked the camp at Ludlow. About twenty people died, among them two women and eleven children who suffocated in a pit under a burning tent. Ten days of fighting followed before federal troops arrived.
  • John Jr. had told Congress, on his managers’ word, that outside agitators were to blame. He was sincere and completely wrong, and the name Rockefeller became attached to dead children.
  • It made him. He went to Colorado with the labour expert William Lyon Mackenzie King, later Canada’s prime minister, went down the mines, and adopted a plan for grievance machinery and worker representation, through a company union rather than an independent one.
  • It also produced Ivy Lee, who within a decade had turned the most hated man in America into a harmless old gentleman handing out dimes.

1911–1937

The Long Ending

He outlived the trust, the muckrakers and most of his enemies, and made his last quarter-century a performance of harmless old age.

  • He was seventy-one at the break-up and lived another twenty-six years: Pocantico, Lakewood and, increasingly, Ormond Beach in Florida, on a fixed routine of golf, drives, music and naps.
  • He passed hundreds of millions to John Jr. and to the foundations, so the fortune drained away by design.
  • The dimes, spontaneous at first, became a managed ritual under Ivy Lee: dimes for adults, nickels for children, always with a word about thrift. Papers that had called him the greatest criminal of the age wrote fondly about his golf.
  • From 1917 to 1920 he gave Inglis his side of the story. He never let it be published; it waited in the archive for Chernow.
  • He died at Ormond Beach on 23 May 1937, seven weeks short of ninety-eight, and was buried in Cleveland near his wife and mother, not far from men he had bought out in 1872.

The argument

Chernow’s Verdict

The contradiction is not a puzzle to be solved. It is the man.

  • He was an organiser before he was a monopolist. An industry with three times the capacity it needed was destroying itself, and consolidation of some kind was coming. How much of what he did was necessary is left open.
  • The methods matter more than the outcome. Kerosene did get cheaper and better. But rebates, drawbacks, secret ownership, waybill intelligence and local price-cutting were the mechanism, not incidents, and the result does not excuse them.
  • The silence was the fatal error. No later giving or public relations recovered the record he let his enemies write.
  • Two inventions outlived the company: professional corporate management and the modern foundation.
  • The state arrived late. By 1911 new fields and the engine had already changed the industry, and the Court made the owner richer and the successors dominant for decades.
  • Chernow will not total the ledger. Hookworm, yellow fever, a meningitis serum and a university set against wrecked refiners, bought legislators and the tent colony at Ludlow: he shows both columns and declines to strike a balance, because the man never felt the need to.

Where readers push back

Arguments the book leaves open

Did Standard actually use predatory pricing?

John S. McGee’s 1958 study of the case record (Journal of Law and Economics) argued that Standard grew by buying rivals, not by selling below cost to ruin them, since price wars cost the bigger firm more. Chernow reports local price-cutting as a method; economists still argue about how often it was used and whether it worked.

Was consolidation good for customers?

Kerosene prices fell steeply under Standard, which defenders from Nevins onward treat as the verdict that matters. Critics answer that prices were falling across the industry anyway and that the gains went disproportionately to Standard. Chernow does not settle it with data.

Market entrepreneur or political one?

Burton Folsom’s The Myth of the Robber Barons (1987) sets Rockefeller among the entrepreneurs who won on cost rather than by state favours. The rebate and drawback deals, and the Archbold letters, complicate that line; Chernow gives the evidence for both readings.

Is Chernow too kind?

Some reviewers found the book fair to a fault, its psychological reading generous to a man who did real harm; others thought it the first account that let him be a person. The Inglis interviews are his own voice, and the book leans on them heavily.

Words that do a lot of work

Glossary

Refining
Distilling crude oil into kerosene and other products: the stage Rockefeller chose.
Rebate
A secret refund on the published freight rate, won by guaranteeing volume.
Drawback
A payment to Standard on oil shipped by its rivals.
Evener
Standard’s role in sharing oil traffic among the railroads, for a fee.
Waybill
A railway’s record of a shipment: sender, volume, destination.
Trust
Stock held by trustees for owners; after 1882, a byword for monopoly.
Holding company
A company whose business is owning other companies: New Jersey’s answer, from 1899.
Sherman Act
The 1890 federal law against combinations in restraint of trade.
Rule of reason
The 1911 doctrine that only unreasonable restraints of trade are illegal.
Lima crude
Sulphur-heavy Ohio oil that nobody could refine until Frasch’s process of 1888.
Scientific philanthropy
Gates’s doctrine: fund institutions that remove causes, run by independent experts.

Using it

Reading it

  1. Read chapters 1–5 slowly

    The parents explain the man. Skim nothing about Devil Bill.

  2. Then 1870–1882 with a pencil

    The consolidation is where the mechanics are. Keep the rebate figure above to hand.

  3. Read Tarbell’s chapters alongside

    Chernow’s middle third is partly an answer to her. Reading the charge and the reply together is the best way in.

  4. Then 1900–1915 in one go

    Tarbell, the suit, the break-up and Ludlow form one arc: the collapse and the rebuilding of the name.

  5. Finish on the giving

    It changes how the first half reads, which is Chernow’s point.

Read alongside

  • Ida Tarbell, The History of the Standard Oil Company (1904): the case for the prosecution, still worth reading.
  • Allan Nevins, Study in Power (1953): the case for the defence.
  • Daniel Yergin, The Prize (1991): the whole oil century, with Standard as its opening act.
  • Ron Chernow, The House of Morgan (1990): the financier who bought the Mesabi ranges from Rockefeller.
  • Man, Economy, and State, summarised on this site: Rothbard’s argument that a monopoly without state privilege cannot hold.
