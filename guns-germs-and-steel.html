Guns, Germs, and Steel

The Fates of Human Societies

“Why is it that you white people developed so much cargo and brought it to New Guinea, but we black people had little cargo of our own?”

Yali, a New Guinean politician, to Diamond in 1972. The question the book answers.

Jared Diamond · 1997 · Pulitzer Prize 1998

The whole book in one breath

Why did Eurasians conquer the Americas, Australia and much of Africa, and not the other way round? Diamond’s answer is that history followed different courses for different peoples because of differences in their environments, not in the peoples themselves. The Fertile Crescent and the rest of Eurasia began with the richest set of wild plants and animals that could be domesticated. Eurasia’s east–west axis let crops, animals and ideas spread along similar latitudes. Farming meant food surpluses, dense populations and settled societies with specialists, states, writing and technology. Crowded living beside livestock bred epidemic diseases, to which Eurasians slowly built immunity. When the continents collided, the proximate weapons were guns, germs and steel, but the ultimate causes lay thousands of years back, in geography and biology.

The author

A physiologist who became a geographer in New Guinea

Jared Diamond (born 1937) says his background had led him into several of the book’s disciplines before Yali ever asked his question: his mother was a teacher and linguist, his father a physician specialising in the genetics of childhood diseases. He was a “fanatical bird-watcher” by seven, trained mainly in languages, history and writing, nearly quit science to become a linguist, and took a Ph.D. in physiology in 1961. Living in Europe from 1958 to 1962, among friends traumatised by its twentieth-century history, made him think about how chains of causes work in history.

He then split his research between molecular physiology (he worked at the UCLA medical school, “the University of California Medical Center, where I work”) and the evolution and biogeography of birds, which took him to South America, southern Africa, Indonesia, Australia and above all New Guinea, where he spent 33 years in the field with people who used stone tools within living memory and collected bird names in nearly 100 of its languages. Evolutionary biology, he notes, is itself a historical science that cannot rely on laboratory experiments, which is the method he brings to human history.

Guns, Germs, and Steel (1997) grew from a chapter of his The Third Chimpanzee (1991) called “Accidental Conquerors”. It won the 1998 Pulitzer Prize for general nonfiction, became one of the most widely read works of “big history”, and was followed by Collapse (2005), The World Until Yesterday (2012) and Upheaval (2019). He later became a professor of geography at UCLA.

The book is deliberately anti-racist in purpose. Diamond’s stated reason for writing it is that until there is a convincing explanation of history’s lopsided outcomes, “most people will continue to suspect that the racist biological explanation is correct after all.”

The argument

Proximate causes, ultimate causes

Diamond’s one-sentence summary: “History followed different courses for different peoples because of differences among peoples’ environments, not because of biological differences among peoples themselves.” The argument works backwards through a chain of causes, drawn in the diagram above.

  • The proximate causes of European conquest, seen at Cajamarca in 1532: steel weapons and armour, guns, horses, ocean-going ships, centralised states, writing, and above all the germs Europeans carried.
  • Food production is the hinge. Farming and herding fed more people per acre, let them settle and store surpluses, and so fed kings, soldiers, priests, scribes and inventors. Livestock added transport, war animals and the crowd diseases that evolved from their germs.
  • The ultimate causes are why food production came earlier and richer to some places: which wild plants and animals were available to domesticate (the Fertile Crescent had 32 of the world’s 56 largest-seeded grasses; Eurasia had 13 of the 14 domesticated big mammals), and how easily crops, animals and ideas could spread (Eurasia’s east–west axis versus the north–south axes of Africa and the Americas).
  • Size and connection then compound the lead: bigger, better-connected regions have more inventors and more competing societies, and technology feeds on itself.
  • Not the people. Wherever peoples gained access to better crops, animals or tools (New Guineans and the sweet potato, Plains Indians and the horse, the Navajo and sheep) they adopted them fast. Within any continent, some societies are innovative and some conservative; over large areas the difference averages out.

If you remember nothing else

Twelve ideas that carry the book

Prologue

Environment, not biology

History diverged because continents offered different raw materials, not because their peoples differed in ability. Diamond even argues New Guineans are, on average, the smarter.

Ch. 1

No clear head start in 11,000 BC

Everyone was a hunter-gatherer at the end of the Ice Age, and an observer then could have made a case for any continent. The divergence came after.

Ch. 2

Environment shapes society

Maori and Moriori shared Polynesian ancestry. Different islands made one people farmers and warriors and the other peaceful hunter-gatherers, whom the first slaughtered in 1835.

Ch. 3

The proximate causes

At Cajamarca 168 Spaniards beat 80,000 Incas with steel, horses, ships, a centralised state and writing, after smallpox had already split the empire.

Ch. 4–6

Farming powers everything else

Food surpluses support dense populations and specialists. Nobody chose farming with foresight; it crept in as wild foods dwindled and wild cereals spread.

Ch. 7–8

Starter kits differed

The Fertile Crescent had easy, productive, self-pollinating cereals and pulses. New Guinea and the eastern United States had poor ones, and dropped them when better crops arrived.

Ch. 9

The Anna Karenina principle

Only 14 of 148 big wild herbivores passed every test of diet, growth, breeding, temper, nerves and social structure. Thirteen of them were Eurasian.

Ch. 10

Axes matter

Crops spread along a latitude, sharing day length and climate. Eurasia runs east–west; the Americas and Africa run north–south, and diffusion stalled.

Ch. 11

Germs did most of the conquering

Crowd diseases evolved from the germs of herd animals and need dense populations. The Americas had neither, so the exchange of germs ran one way.

Ch. 12–13

Ideas spread more than they are invented

Writing was invented only a few times. Invention is usually the mother of necessity, and big, connected regions with many societies innovate fastest.

Ch. 14

Bigger societies need kleptocrats

Bands, tribes, chiefdoms and states follow population size. Strangers must be kept from killing each other, so power centralises, and elites take their cut.

Epilogue

Europe, not China, because of fragmentation

Unified China could halt its treasure fleets with one court decision; divided Europe let Columbus try five patrons. The Fertile Crescent wrecked its fragile environment.

Prologue

Yali’s Question

Why did history unfold so differently on different continents? Not because of the peoples, Diamond says, but because of their environments; and the proximate weapons of conquest need ultimate explanations.

The question on the beach

  • In July 1972 Diamond, a biologist studying bird evolution, was walking along a beach in New Guinea when he fell into step with Yali, a charismatic local politician preparing people for self-government (Papua New Guinea was then still administered by Australia under a UN mandate). For an hour Yali quizzed him: about his birds, how much he was paid, how Yali’s ancestors had reached New Guinea, and how whites had colonised it within the last 200 years.
  • Two centuries earlier all New Guineans had been “living in the Stone Age”. Whites arrived with steel axes, matches, medicines, clothing, soft drinks and umbrellas, all known locally as “cargo”, and even the least able white “master” lived far better than Yali. Yet both men knew “perfectly well that New Guineans are on the average at least as smart as Europeans.” So Yali asked: “Why is it that you white people developed so much cargo and brought it to New Guinea, but we black people had little cargo of our own?”
  • Diamond had no answer then; historians still disagree, and most no longer ask. “This book, written twenty-five years later, attempts to answer Yali.”

Pushing the question back

  • The question widens: why do peoples of Eurasian origin dominate the world in wealth and power, while Aboriginal Australians, Native Americans and southern Africans were decimated, subjugated or exterminated? Why wasn’t it the other way round? The outcomes were lopsided: not 51 per cent of the Americas conquered by Europeans and 49 per cent of Europe by Native Americans.
  • By AD 1500 the differences were already stark: metal-equipped states across Eurasia and North Africa, stone-tool empires among the Aztecs and Incas, small iron-using states in parts of Africa, and farming tribes or hunter-gatherer bands with stone tools everywhere else. The mass production of bronze tools, just beginning in the Andes before 1500, was established in parts of Eurasia over 4,000 years earlier; the Tasmanians’ stone tools in 1642 were simpler than those of Upper Palaeolithic Europe tens of thousands of years before.
  • At the end of the last Ice Age, around 11,000 BC, all peoples everywhere were hunter-gatherers. So the real question is: “why did human development proceed at such different rates on different continents?” (see the figure).
  • It matters now: colonial legacies in Africa, guerrilla wars pitting indigenous people against conquerors’ descendants from Mexico to New Caledonia, native peoples outnumbered after genocide and disease, and the impending loss of most of the world’s 6,000 languages.

Three objections to asking

  • Explaining is not justifying. Psychologists study murderers and physicians study disease to break the chain of causes, not to excuse it.
  • It is not Eurocentric. Most of the book is about non-Europeans and their interactions with each other, and most basic elements of western civilisation were developed elsewhere and imported.
  • “Civilisation” is not assumed to be better. Industrial citizens get better medicine, lower homicide risk and longer lives but much less support from friends and extended family; “the so-called blessings of civilization are mixed.”

The answers Diamond rejects

  • Race. From 1500 Europeans assumed innate differences, then recast them in Darwinian and then genetic terms. The “seemingly compelling” Australian argument (whites built an industrial democracy within a century on a continent Aborigines had held for 40,000 years) fails, as Chapter 15 shows. Racist explanations are “not just loathsome, but also wrong”: IQ studies cannot separate genes from upbringing, and formerly Stone Age peoples master industrial technology when given the chance.
  • Diamond goes further. From 33 years with New Guineans, he finds them on average more intelligent and alert than Westerners. In New Guinea, death came from murder, war, accidents and hunger, which selected for wits; in dense European societies, death came from epidemics, which selected for body chemistry (people of blood group B or O resist smallpox better than group A). And Western children sit in front of a television that is on seven hours a day in the average American home. “The usual racist assumption has to be turned on its head.”
  • Cold climate. Northern Europeans contributed nothing of fundamental importance to Eurasian civilisation until the last thousand years; they were lucky to receive farming, wheels, writing and metal from warmer lands. In the Americas, writing arose only in Mexico south of the Tropic of Cancer, the oldest pottery comes from near the equator, and the most advanced society was the tropical Classic Maya.
  • River-valley irrigation. Complex irrigation followed political centralisation, after a considerable lag. Farming and villages in the Fertile Crescent began in the hills, and the Nile valley was a backwater for about 3,000 years after that.
  • Guns, germs and steel themselves. These are “on the right track” but only proximate. Why did Europeans, not Africans or Native Americans, get them? Africa, where humans evolved longest, is the biggest puzzle.
  • Arnold Toynbee’s twelve-volume A Study of History examined 23 civilisations, 22 literate and 19 Eurasian, and never asked Yali’s question, because its roots lie in prehistory. Until there is a convincing answer, “most people will continue to suspect that the racist biological explanation is correct after all. That seems to me the strongest argument for writing this book.”

Why now, and why him

  • Environmental explanations are old but out of favour, caricatured as “environmental determinism”. What is new is evidence from genetics, molecular biology and biogeography of crops, animals and germs, epidemiology, linguistics and archaeology on every continent. The subject is history, but the approach is that of historical sciences such as evolutionary biology and geology.
  • Diamond’s mother was a teacher and linguist and his father a physician specialising in the genetics of childhood diseases; he was a fanatical bird-watcher by seven, trained mainly in languages and history, took a Ph.D. in physiology in 1961 (nearly quitting to become a linguist), and lived in Europe from 1958 to 1962 among friends traumatised by its twentieth-century history. His fieldwork took him to South America, southern Africa, Indonesia, Australia and above all New Guinea, home to 1,000 of the world’s 6,000 languages; he has collected bird names in nearly 100 of them.
  • The book grew from Chapter 14 of The Third Chimpanzee (1991), “Accidental Conquerors”, on Europeans and Native Americans.

Preface to the paperback: why is world history like an onion?

  • The 1999 paperback adds a preface. The book is “a short history of everybody for the last 13,000 years”. Most world histories concentrate on literate Eurasia, although history before writing (around 3000 BC) is 99.9 per cent of the human story, and by 3000 BC parts of Eurasia and North Africa already had writing, states, cities, metal and draught animals.
  • Cultural explanations like Confucianism stifling Chinese science are not ultimate either: why didn’t Confucianism arise in the west instead? And Confucian China was technologically ahead of western Eurasia until about AD 1400.
  • A reviewer teased that Diamond sees world history as an onion, whose layers must be peeled back. “Yes, world history is indeed such an onion!”

Part One

From Eden to Cajamarca

Where humans stood at the end of the Ice Age, a Polynesian test of how environment shapes societies, and the collision at Cajamarca that makes the question vivid.

Chapter 1

Up to the Starting Line

A whirlwind tour from 7 million years ago to 11,000 BC. By the starting line every continent except Antarctica was peopled, the Americas last; big animals had vanished from Australia and the Americas; and an observer then could have argued for any continent as the future winner.

A note on dates

  • Diamond starts the comparison around 11,000 BC: the beginnings of village life, the first undisputed peopling of the Americas, and the end of the last Ice Age. Throughout the book, dates for the last 15,000 years are calibrated radiocarbon dates, so they look older than familiar ones: the Clovis horizon, usually given as about 9000 BC, appears here as about 11,000 BC.

From apes to the Great Leap Forward

  • Our closest relatives, gorillas and the two chimpanzees, live only in Africa, where the human line split off about 7 million years ago (estimates run from 5 to 9). Our ancestors stood upright by about 4 million years ago and began growing larger bodies and brains around 2.5 million, through Australopithecus africanus, Homo habilis and Homo erectus (by about 1.7 million years ago, near our size but with barely half our brain). Crude stone tools became common around 2.5 million years ago.
  • Homo erectus was the first to leave Africa: Java man, usually dated to about a million years ago, perhaps 1.8 million. Unquestioned evidence of humans in Europe begins about half a million years ago. Diamond warns of a recurring pattern: every claim of “the earliest X” provokes claims of an earlier one, and consensus takes decades.
  • By half a million years ago skulls were rounder and are classed as early Homo sapiens, but their tools were crude; their one confident addition is fire. Neanderthals (Europe and western Asia, 130,000 to 40,000 years ago) had brains slightly larger than ours and buried their dead and cared for their sick, but their tools lacked standard shapes. Africans of 100,000 years ago had more modern skeletons but the same crude tools; they hunted only easy prey and could not even catch fish.
  • Around 50,000 years ago came what Diamond calls the Great Leap Forward: standardised stone tools and ostrich-shell beads in East Africa, then the Near East, and about 40,000 years ago the Cro-Magnons in south-western Europe, with bone fishhooks and needles, multipiece weapons (harpoons, spear-throwers, bows), rope, sewn clothing, houses, careful burials, and the painted bulls and horses of Lascaux. Diamond’s own guess at the trigger, argued in The Third Chimpanzee, is the perfected voice box and modern language.
  • Where it happened is unresolved: a single origin (African skulls of 100,000 years ago, early mitochondrial DNA studies) or regional continuity (Chinese and Indonesian skulls said to resemble modern Chinese and Aboriginal Australians). The case for replacement is strongest in Europe, where within a few thousand years of the Cro-Magnons’ arrival there were no Neanderthals, with “little or no evidence of hybridization”.

Australia, New Guinea and the first mass extinction

  • Australia and New Guinea, then one continent, were settled between 40,000 and 30,000 years ago. Even with Ice Age seas hundreds of feet lower, reaching it meant crossing at least eight deep-water channels, one at least 50 miles wide, to a land always invisible from Timor or Tanimbar: by far the earliest evidence of watercraft anywhere. New Britain, New Ireland and Buka (a 100-mile crossing to an unseen island) were settled by about 35,000 years ago, so it was no fluke.
  • Australia/New Guinea once had giant kangaroos, cow-sized diprotodonts, a marsupial “leopard”, a 400-pound flightless bird, a one-ton lizard, a giant python and land crocodiles. Today its largest mammal is a 100-pound kangaroo. Carefully dug sites show no trace of the giants in the last 35,000 years.
  • Animals that evolved without humans are fatally tame, as Galápagos and Antarctic wildlife still are; the dodo, New Zealand’s moas, Madagascar’s giant lemurs and Hawaii’s flightless geese all vanished soon after humans arrived. Africa’s and Eurasia’s big mammals survived because they co-evolved with slowly improving hunters. Critics of the “overkill” hypothesis point to the lack of kill sites and blame drought. Diamond cannot see why giants that survived millions of years of droughts should die together, in every habitat from desert to rain forest, just when humans came.
  • Whatever the cause, the result mattered: Australians and New Guineans were left with not a single large animal to domesticate.

The Americas, last of all

  • Neanderthals got no farther north than northern Germany and Kiev; modern humans with needles and warm houses reached Siberia by about 20,000 years ago (possibly finishing off the woolly mammoth and rhinoceros). Only then could people cross the Bering land bridge, up to a thousand miles wide, which flooded again after about 14,000 BC.
  • The oldest unquestioned remains in the Americas are in Alaska around 12,000 BC. Once an ice-free corridor opened near modern Edmonton, Clovis hunters, with their big stone spearpoints, blanketed all 48 lower states and Mexico in the centuries before 11,000 BC, and soon reached Amazonia and Patagonia. The arithmetic is easy (see the figure).
  • About 15,000 years ago the American West looked like the Serengeti, with elephants, horses, lions, cheetahs, camels and giant ground sloths. Most vanished; the Shasta ground sloth and Harrington’s mountain goat disappeared within a century or two of 11,100 BC, the date Clovis hunters reached the Grand Canyon, and mammoth skeletons have been found with Clovis points between their ribs. The climate alternative faces the question: why did animals that survived 22 previous Ice Age endings die with the 23rd?
  • Pre-Clovis claims come every year. Pedra Furada in Brazil (in the book, “Pedro Furada”) offered charcoal dated to 35,000 years ago and rock-fall “tools”; Meadowcroft in Pennsylvania has dates of about 16,000 years ago but with plants and animals of a mild climate; Monte Verde in Chile, dated to at least 15,000 years ago, “now seems convincing to many”. Diamond doubts them all: hundreds of sites show human layers resting on older layers with animals but no people, while Australia, with a tenth as many archaeologists as the United States, has over a hundred unequivocal pre-Clovis sites. Either way, the Americas have the shortest human prehistory of the five habitable continents.
  • The last islands filled later: Crete, Cyprus, Corsica and Sardinia between about 8500 and 4000 BC; the Caribbean from about 4000 BC; the High Arctic about 2000 BC; Polynesia and Micronesia between 1200 BC and AD 1000; Madagascar between AD 300 and 800; Iceland in the ninth century.

Who had the head start?

  • Imagine an archaeologist touring the world in 11,000 BC trying to predict which continent would develop guns, germs and steel first (see the figure). Each had a case; nobody would have picked Eurasia for the reasons that actually operated. A head start in time counts for little: the New World filled in under 1,000 years, and New Zealand’s Maori found every worthwhile stone source within about a century and killed every moa within a few centuries.
  • “With hindsight, of course, we know that Eurasia was the one.” The rest of the book looks for the real reasons.

Chapter 2

A Natural Experiment of History

Polynesia is a laboratory: one ancestral people spread over islands of every kind, and within a few thousand years produced everything from hunter-gatherer bands to proto-empires. Environment shaped them, and the Moriori paid the price.

The fate of the Moriori, 1835

  • On 19 November 1835 a ship carrying 500 Maori armed with guns, clubs and axes reached the Chatham Islands, 500 miles east of New Zealand; on 5 December another 400 arrived. The Moriori still outnumbered them two to one, but they had a tradition of settling disputes peacefully, and their council decided to offer “peace, friendship, and a division of resources”.
  • Before they could, the Maori attacked, killing hundreds, eating many, and enslaving the rest, most of whom they killed over the next few years. A survivor: “[The Maori] commenced to kill us like sheep.” A conqueror: “We took possession … in accordance with our customs and we caught all the people. Not one escaped.”
  • What makes this “grimly illuminating” is that both peoples were Polynesians who had diverged less than a thousand years earlier. The Maori descend from farmers who settled New Zealand around AD 1000; some of them then settled the Chathams and became the Moriori. The two lost contact for perhaps 500 years, until an Australian sealing ship told New Zealand of islands whose people “do not understand how to fight, and have no weapons”.

Why the two diverged

  • Maori tropical crops could not grow in the cold Chathams, so the settlers reverted to hunting seals, shellfish, seabirds and fish, with clubs and bare hands. Without surpluses they could feed no craft specialists, armies, bureaucrats or chiefs. The islands could support only about 2,000 people and there was nowhere else to go, so the Moriori renounced war and limited numbers by castrating some male infants.
  • Northern New Zealand, the largest island group in Polynesia, suited Polynesian farming. The Maori there grew to more than 100,000, lived in dense populations locked in ferocious wars, fed specialists, chiefs and part-time soldiers, and built ceremonial houses and “prodigious numbers of forts”.

Polynesia as a natural experiment

  • Laboratory scientists could split a rat colony among cages and come back generations later. Historians need “natural experiments” instead. Around 1200 BC seafaring farmers from the Bismarck Archipelago began settling the Pacific; by AD 500 nearly every habitable island was reached, the last around or soon after AD 1000. All descended from one founding people with the same culture, language, tools, crops and animals.
  • The islands differed in six ways (see the figure): climate, geology, marine resources, area, terrain fragmentation and isolation. Rainfall ranged from the world’s highest (New Zealand’s Fjordland, Kauai’s Alakai Swamp) to a tenth of that; land from the 100 acres of Anuta to the 103,000 square miles of New Zealand, a fragment of Gondwanaland with iron, coal, gold and jade.
  • Food production depended on the environment. Polynesians brought only the pig, chicken and dog, and isolated islands lost some: New Zealand kept only dogs, Easter and Tikopia only chickens (Easter Islanders built chicken houses for intensive poultry farming). All crops were tropical imports, so the cold south reverted to foraging; atolls and raised limestone islands like Henderson and Rennell had thin soil and little water; Tonga and Easter farmed intensively on dry land; Kauai, Oahu and Molokai built irrigated taro fields yielding up to 24 tons an acre, the highest in Polynesia, and Hawaii alone farmed milkfish and mullet in big fishponds.
  • So densities ranged enormously (see the figure), and so did the size of political units, from a few dozen people to 40,000. Rugged Marquesan valleys each formed separate polities of a few thousand people who communicated by sea; Tonga’s compact islands let one line of chiefs unite the archipelago.

From egalitarian bands to proto-empires

  • The larger and denser the political unit, the more complex its economy, society, politics and material culture (see the figure). Surpluses fed non-producers (chiefs, priests, bureaucrats, warriors) who in turn mobilised labour for irrigation and fishponds.
  • On the Chathams and atolls, chiefs lived in ordinary huts and caught their own food, decisions were made by general discussion, and land belonged to the community. In Hawaii the chiefly class formed eight ranked lineages that married only among themselves, sometimes siblings or half-siblings; commoners prostrated themselves before high chiefs; and chiefs controlled the land and conscripted labour through appointed bureaucrats.
  • Tonga, when Europeans arrived in the eighteenth century, was already an inter-archipelago empire: its chiefs had united the islands, traded regularly with Fiji and Samoa, settled and raided Fiji, and conquered islands up to 500 miles away with navies of canoes holding up to 150 men. Hawaii, “discovered” by Europeans in 1778, had unified each island; Kamehameha I then used European guns and ships to take Maui and Oahu, and Kauai’s chief finally negotiated.
  • Material culture followed raw materials and scale. Henderson’s islanders, with no stone but limestone, made adzes from giant clamshells; the Maori worked jade. Specialists on the big islands made prestige goods like Hawaiian feather capes of tens of thousands of feathers. Monumental building (Easter’s statues, Tongan chiefs’ tombs, Marquesan platforms, Hawaiian and Society Island temples) was evolving in the direction of the pyramids; Easter Island’s 7,000 people raised 30-ton statues by muscle alone.

What Polynesia shows, and what it cannot

  • Polynesia is only a small slice of the world’s range. No island but New Zealand had significant metal, so there was no metallurgy; there were only two proto-empires, one (Hawaii) formed after Europeans came; and there was no writing, except perhaps Easter Island’s mysterious script, which may postdate contact. The oldest Polynesian societies had only 3,200 years to develop, against at least 13,000 on the continents.
  • Given a few more millennia, Diamond speculates, Tonga and Hawaii might have been full empires with their own writing, and the Maori might have added copper and iron to their jade. Polynesia proves that environmental diversification can happen; the question is what drove it on the continents.

Chapter 3

Collision at Cajamarca

On 16 November 1532, 168 Spaniards captured the Inca emperor in the middle of his army of 80,000. The reasons (steel, horses, germs, ships, centralised states and writing) are the proximate causes of Europe’s conquests. Explaining them is the rest of the book.

The greatest collision

  • The biggest population shift of modern times was Europe’s colonisation of the Americas. After the first settlement from Asia, the only well-attested contacts were between hunter-gatherers across the Bering Strait and a voyage that carried the sweet potato from South America to Polynesia. The Norse held Greenland in tiny numbers from AD 986 to about 1500 with no visible effect. The collision began abruptly in 1492.
  • Its most dramatic moment came at Cajamarca in the Peruvian highlands. Atahuallpa, absolute ruler of the largest and most advanced state in the New World, stood amid millions of subjects and an army of 80,000 fresh from a civil war victory. Francisco Pizarro, serving Charles V (Charles I of Spain), had 168 men, 1,000 miles from the nearest Spaniards in Panama. Pizarro captured him within minutes, held him eight months while extracting history’s largest ransom, a room 22 feet by 17 filled with gold to over 8 feet high, and then executed him.
  • Because the Incas revered Atahuallpa as a sun-god and obeyed him even in captivity, his capture let Pizarro explore unmolested and send for reinforcements.

The eyewitnesses

  • Diamond weaves together accounts by six of Pizarro’s companions, including his brothers Hernando and Pedro. They tortured Indians for news; saw Atahuallpa’s camp “like a very beautiful city”; and watched campfires on the hillside “like the sky brightly studded with stars”. Hernando told the men there were 40,000 Indians, “telling a lie just to encourage us, for there were actually more than 80,000.”
  • Pizarro hid his cavalry (under Hernando Pizarro and Hernando de Soto) and infantry around the square, with Pedro de Candia and a small gun in a fort. At noon Atahuallpa came, preceded by 2,000 men sweeping the road, carried on a silver-covered litter by eighty lords in blue livery, wearing a collar of large emeralds. The Spaniards waited in hiding: “Many of us urinated without noticing it, out of sheer terror.”
  • Friar Vicente de Valverde offered Atahuallpa the Bible; he threw it five or six paces away, and the friar cried, “Come out! Come out, Christians! … I absolve you!” Guns, trumpets and horses hung with rattles threw the unarmed crowd into panic; people suffocated in heaps; the litter-bearers were killed and replaced until horsemen heaved the litter over. The army outside never moved. “Six or seven thousand Indians lay dead”; “not one Indian raised a weapon against a Spaniard.” Pizarro then lectured his prisoner on the “bestial and diabolical life” God had delivered him from.

80,000soldiers with Atahuallpa, 500 times as many

Why did Pizarro capture Atahuallpa?

  • Steel and horses. The Spaniards had steel swords, lances and daggers, steel armour and helmets, a dozen hard-to-load harquebuses (a minor factor), and horses. The Incas had stone, bronze or wooden clubs, maces and hand axes, slings and quilted armour. Clubs rarely killed an armoured man; steel went straight through quilting.
  • This was no fluke of novelty, native allies or the Incas mistaking Spaniards for the god Viracocha. On the road to Cuzco, 80, 30, 110 and 40 horsemen won four battles against thousands (see the figure), and the Incas’ two great rebellions within six years, against men and horses they now knew, failed too. By 1536 they had learned to ambush cavalry in narrow passes, but in the open, 26 horsemen routed Emperor Manco’s best troops at Cuzco, and at Lima a cavalry charge killed the general Quizo Yupanqui and all his commanders at once.
  • Horses, domesticated around 4000 BC north of the Black Sea, stayed decisive for 6,000 years, until the First World War. Only Native Americans who mastered horses and rifles themselves (the Plains Indians who beat Custer at the Little Big Horn in 1876, the Araucanians of Chile, the Pampas Indians of Argentina) held out, until army campaigns of the 1870s and 1880s.
  • By the 1700s guns had taken over: in 1808 a British sailor aptly named Charlie Savage upset Fiji’s whole balance of power with muskets, shooting so many villagers at Kasavu that survivors sheltered behind piles of bodies.

Why was Atahuallpa at Cajamarca? Germs

  • He was there because he had just won a civil war. Smallpox, arriving with Spanish settlers in Panama and Colombia and spreading overland, killed the emperor Huayna Capac and most of his court around 1526, then his heir Ninan Cuyuchi, setting Atahuallpa against his half-brother Huascar. Without the epidemic, the Spaniards would have faced a united empire.
  • Smallpox, measles, influenza, typhus and plague decimated peoples everywhere Europeans went, often ahead of them: they killed the Aztec emperor Cuitlahuac after the first Spanish attack failed in 1520; an estimated 95 per cent of pre-Columbian Native Americans; the Mississippian chiefdoms before any European settled on the Mississippi; South Africa’s San in 1713; Aboriginal Australians soon after Sydney was founded in 1788; and Fijians in 1806, from a few sailors wrecked on the Argo. The traffic ran the other way only in the tropics, where malaria and yellow fever were Europe’s main obstacle.

Why was Pizarro there, and not Atahuallpa in Spain?

  • Ships and a centralised state carried Pizarro from Spain to Panama and then to Peru; Spain’s political organisation financed, built, staffed and equipped them. The Incas’ centralisation hurt them, because by seizing its godlike head Pizarro seized its whole chain of command.
  • Writing spread the news of Columbus and Cortés and the sailing directions that sent Spaniards pouring west. Captain Cristóbal de Mena’s account of Cajamarca was printed in Seville in April 1534, nine months after Atahuallpa’s execution, became a best-seller and drew more colonists.

Why did Atahuallpa walk into the trap?

  • His information came by word of mouth, from an envoy who spent two days with the Spaniards at their most disorganised and told him they were no fighting men; with 200 Indians he could tie them all up. Although Spain began conquering Panama, 600 miles from the Inca border, in 1510, no word of the Spaniards seems to have reached the Incas before Pizarro’s first landing in 1527.
  • After his capture he paid the ransom believing they would then leave, not grasping that this was the spearhead of permanent conquest. His general Chalcuchima let Hernando Pizarro lure him in; Montezuma admitted Cortés to Tenochtitlan.
  • Pizarro himself was illiterate, but he belonged to a literate tradition that knew of distant civilisations and thousands of years of history, and he modelled his ambush on Cortés’s. “Literacy made the Spaniards heirs to a huge body of knowledge about human behavior and history.”

Part Two

The Rise and Spread of Food Production

The core of the book: why farming began where and when it did, what the wild plants and animals allowed, and why it spread fast in some directions and not others.

Chapter 4

Farmer Power

Food production is the root of the conquerors’ advantages: more people per acre, settled villages, stored surpluses that feed kings, soldiers and inventors, animals for transport and war, and the germs that came from them.

“Damn the ship that brought you from Switzerland”

  • In the summer of 1956 the teenage Diamond worked in south-western Montana for Fred Hirschy, a Swiss immigrant who arrived in the 1890s, when the local Native American hunter-gatherers were still there. The hands were hard-cursing whites and one Blackfoot, Levi, polite, gentle and sober, until one Sunday he staggered in drunk, cursing: “Damn you, Fred Hirschy, and damn the ship that brought you from Switzerland!” Levi’s tribe of famous hunters and warriors had been robbed of its land by immigrant farmers. How did farmers beat warriors?
  • For almost all of the 7 million years since our ancestors split from the apes, everyone hunted and gathered. Food production began only within the last 11,000 years, at different times in different places, independently in some (ancient China) and borrowed in others (ancient Egypt), never at all in Aboriginal Australia. Diamond predicted that within a decade the last hunter-gatherer bands would be gone.

More calories, more people

  • Most wild species are useless as food: indigestible (bark), poisonous (monarch butterflies, death caps), low in value (jellyfish), tedious (tiny nuts), hard to gather (most insect larvae) or dangerous (rhinos). Most land biomass is wood and leaves. Farming makes edible species 90 per cent instead of 0.1 per cent of the biomass on an acre, so an acre feeds typically 10 to 100 times more farmers and herders than hunter-gatherers. Brute numbers were the farmers’ first military advantage.
  • Livestock add food in four ways (see the figure). The ox-drawn plough mattered hugely: the first central European farmers, the Linearbandkeramik culture of slightly before 5000 BC, were confined to light soils they could work with digging sticks for over a thousand years, and the tough upland sods of the Great Plains waited for nineteenth-century Europeans and their draught animals.
  • Settling down shortens the birth interval. A nomadic mother can carry only one child, so hunter-gatherers space births about four years apart through breastfeeding, abstinence, infanticide and abortion; farm peoples space them about two years apart.

Surpluses feed specialists

  • Storage is pointless if you move on, so full-time specialists first appear in sedentary societies. Once food can be stockpiled, an elite can control others’ produce, tax, and do politics full time: moderate farming societies form chiefdoms, and kingdoms appear only among large ones. Rich-environment foragers on the Pacific Northwest coast and coastal Ecuador became sedentary with nascent chiefdoms but went no further.
  • Taxed surpluses feed professional soldiers. The Maori won stunning victories but could not keep an army permanently in the field and were worn down by 18,000 full-time British troops. Surpluses also feed priests who justify conquest, metalworkers who make swords and guns, and scribes who remember more than anyone can.

Fibre, transport, war and germs

  • Crops and animals gave fibre (cotton, flax for linen, hemp; wool from sheep, goats, llamas and alpacas; silk), bone, leather, and containers: the bottle gourd was one of the Americas’ earliest crops.
  • Big mammals were the main land transport until railways. Horses, donkeys, yaks, reindeer and both camels were ridden; those five and the llama carried packs; cattle and horses pulled wagons; reindeer and dogs pulled sleds.
  • Horses were “the jeeps and Sherman tanks of ancient warfare”. Ridden bareback around 4000 BC, they may explain the spread of Indo-European languages from Ukraine, which replaced every earlier western European language except Basque; chariots, invented around 1800 BC, revolutionised war from the Mediterranean to China, and in 1674 BC let the Hyksos conquer horseless Egypt; with saddles and stirrups came the Huns and finally the Mongols of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Only trucks and tanks in the First World War replaced them.
  • Germs from domestic animals (smallpox, measles, flu) first struck the herders, who evolved resistance; meeting unexposed peoples, they killed up to 99 per cent. They were decisive against Native Americans, Australians, South Africans and Pacific islanders.
  • So domestication “ultimately explains why empires, literacy, and steel weapons developed earliest in Eurasia and later, or not at all, on other continents.” Why food production arose where it did is the subject of the next six chapters.

Chapter 5

History’s Haves and Have-Nots

Food production arose independently in only five certain places and four possible ones, at very different dates. Elsewhere it arrived by borrowing or by invasion, and some of today’s richest farmland never got it until Europeans came.

The puzzles

  • It is no surprise that no food production arose in the Arctic (only reindeer herding in Eurasia’s) or in deserts far from water. What needs explaining is its absence, until modern times, from some of the world’s best farmland: California and the Pacific states, the Argentine pampas, south-western and south-eastern Australia, and much of South Africa’s Cape. In 4000 BC the rest of the United States, England and much of France, Indonesia and all of Africa below the equator still lacked it.
  • Meanwhile the earliest centres were in lands now rather dry or degraded: Iraq and Iran, Mexico, the Andes, parts of China, the Sahel. Why did it start there? Why independently in some places and imported in others? Why did dates vary by thousands of years? And why did local foragers adopt farming in some places (the US Southwest) but get replaced by incoming farmers in others (Indonesia, most of Africa below the equator)?

How we know where and when

  • Domesticated forms differ from wild ones: smaller cattle and sheep, bigger chickens and apples, thinner and smoother pea coats, goat horns twisted like corkscrews rather than curved like scimitars. Finding them at a dated site proves food production.
  • Radiocarbon dating measures the decay of carbon 14 (about one part in a million of atmospheric carbon), which halves every 5,700 years; beyond about 40,000 years too little is left. Until the 1980s it needed grams of material, so archaeologists dated “associated” charcoal, which worms and rodents can mix across millennia. Accelerator mass spectrometry now dates single seeds, and has moved the start of farming in the Americas from as early as 7000 BC (indirect dates of the 1960s–70s) to no earlier than 3500 BC.
  • Calibration corrects for changes in atmospheric carbon 14 using tree rings: uncalibrated dates between about 1000 and 6000 BC are really a few centuries to a thousand years older, and an apparent 9000 BC is really about 11,000 BC. All the book’s dates for the last 15,000 years are calibrated.
  • To locate domestication, look where the wild ancestor grows and where the domestic form appears first. India grows 80 per cent of the world’s chickpeas, but wild chickpeas occur only in south-eastern Turkey, where domestic ones appear around 8000 BC, over 5,000 years before India. Emmer wheat appears in the Fertile Crescent around 8500 BC, Greece around 6500, Germany around 5000. Some animals were domesticated more than once: India’s humped zebu and western cattle descend from wild subspecies that diverged hundreds of thousands of years ago.

Where food production began

  • Five areas certainly invented food production on their own: Southwest Asia (the Fertile Crescent), China (perhaps separately on the Yellow and Yangtze Rivers), Mesoamerica, the Andes (perhaps with Amazonia) and the eastern United States. Four more are candidates: the Sahel (cattle may have come first, perhaps from the Fertile Crescent), tropical West Africa, Ethiopia and New Guinea, whose early crops are unidentified.
  • Southwest Asia has the earliest definite dates, plants around 8500 BC and animals around 8000 BC, and by far the most radiocarbon dates. China is nearly as early; the eastern United States about 6,000 years later (see the figure).
  • In other areas, founder crops from elsewhere let people settle, after which they domesticated a few local species: western and central Europe (the poppy, whose wild form grows only on western Mediterranean coasts, and probably oats) after Southwest Asian crops arrived between 6000 and 3500 BC; the Indus Valley (humped cattle, sesame) after wheat and barley arrived via Iran in the seventh millennium BC; Egypt (the sycamore fig and a vegetable called chufa) after the sixth millennium; perhaps Ethiopia, whose coffee has spread worldwide.

Borrowing or replacement?

  • In some places local foragers simply added foreign crops and animals and gradually dropped wild foods: Egypt, probably Europe’s Atlantic coast, the Khoi of the Cape (who became herders of sheep and cattle but not farmers), and the Native Americans of the US Southwest, who took up Mexican crops.
  • Elsewhere farmers arrived with their own crops and replaced the foragers. That is certain in modern cases written up by literate Europeans: California, the Pacific Northwest, the pampas, Australia and Siberia, where Europeans domesticated no local species except, in Australia, the macadamia nut; and the Cape, where they also displaced Khoi herders.
  • Prehistoric replacements are inferred from skeletons, pottery and language. The clearest are the Austronesian expansion from South China into the Philippines and Indonesia (Chapter 17) and the Bantu expansion across Africa below the equator (Chapter 19). South-eastern and central Europe probably saw “old Greeks and Germans” replaced by new ones, though the skeletal evidence is weaker there.
  • So the peoples with a head start in food production got a head start toward guns, germs and steel, and history became “a long series of collisions between the haves and the have-nots”.

Chapter 6

To Farm or Not to Farm

Nobody chose farming with foresight. It evolved piecemeal from countless decisions about how to spend a day, and five factors tipped the balance toward it after about 8500 BC in the Fertile Crescent.

Farming was not obviously better

  • Why did food production begin in the Fertile Crescent’s Mediterranean habitats around 8500 BC, 3,000 years later in similar south-western Europe, and never independently in the Mediterranean climates of California, south-western Australia and the Cape? And why not in 18,500 or 28,500 BC?
  • Hobbes’s “nasty, brutish, and short” is wrong about foragers. Only affluent First World citizens, who don’t raise their own food, gain from agriculture in work, comfort and lifespan. Peasants may work longer hours than hunter-gatherers, and the first farmers in many areas were smaller, less well nourished, sicker and shorter-lived than the foragers they replaced.
  • Some foragers saw farming and refused it for thousands of years: Aborigines of north-eastern Australia trading with Torres Strait farmers, Californians trading with Colorado River farmers, Khoi herders west of the Fish River trading with Bantu farmers east of it. Others waited: northern Germany’s coastal people adopted farming 1,300 years after the Linearbandkeramik culture brought it inland only 125 miles to the south.

Blurred lines

  • Food production was neither discovered nor invented; the first farmers had never seen farming and could not aim at it. It “evolved as a by-product of decisions made without awareness of their consequences”.
  • Nomad and farmer are not opposites. Foragers of the Pacific Northwest coast (and perhaps south-eastern Australia) settled without farming; those of Palestine, coastal Peru and Japan settled first and farmed much later. Conversely, New Guinea’s Lakes Plains nomads plant bananas and papayas and come back months later; Apaches farmed in summer and foraged in winter; herders move seasonally.
  • Foragers manage land too. New Guineans tend wild sago and pandanus; Aboriginal Australians burned the landscape to promote seed plants and replanted the tops of the wild yams they dug. To count as farmers they needed only to carry the tops home and plant them at camp.

Decisions about a day’s work

  • An incipient farmer wakes and weighs hoeing a garden (much food, months away), gathering shellfish (a little, today) or hunting deer (maybe a lot, probably nothing). Foragers rank foods by payoff and fall back to less preferred ones. They seek calories, protein, fat, salt and sweetness with the least effort and risk; the first gardens, nearly 11,000 years ago, may have been an insurance larder.
  • Prestige and culture also count: men may prefer bagging one giraffe a month to gathering twice the food in nuts; fish are delicacies or taboo; farmers despise foragers as primitive, foragers despise farmers as ignorant, and herders despise both, as nineteenth-century cattlemen, sheepmen and farmers despised each other in the American West.
  • Once farming existed nearby, foragers could choose consciously, and they took it whole, in parts, or not at all. South-eastern Europe adopted cereals, pulses and livestock as a package by about 6000 BC, and central Europe before 5000 BC, perhaps because foraging there was poor; south-western Europe took sheep first and cereals later; Japan, rich in seafood, adopted farming very slowly. Systems changed piecemeal too, and could even go backwards (see the figure).

Five factors that tipped the balance

  • Wild foods declined. Big mammals vanished in the Americas and some in Eurasia and Africa. On islands the sequence is certain: Polynesians intensified farming only after exterminating moas and decimating seals and birds; Easter Islanders, arriving around AD 500 with chickens, made them a major food only once wild birds and porpoises were gone. In the Fertile Crescent, wild gazelles declined.
  • Domesticable wild plants spread. Climate change at the end of the Pleistocene greatly expanded wild cereal stands in the Fertile Crescent, from which huge crops could be harvested fast.
  • Technology accumulated. After 11,000 BC Fertile Crescent foragers developed flint-bladed sickles, baskets, mortars, pestles and grinding slabs, roasting to stop grain sprouting, and plastered underground storage pits: all invented for wild cereals, all prerequisites for farming them.
  • Population and food production drove each other. Densities were rising anyway through the late Pleistocene; food production fed more people, and settling shortened birth spacing, creating more mouths. This “autocatalytic” loop explains the paradox that farmers ate worse than foragers: population rose slightly faster than food.
  • Farmers outnumbered foragers. At frontiers, denser farming populations, with their technology, germs and soldiers, displaced or killed foragers, and foragers who farmed outbred those who didn’t.
  • Those four first factors explain the timing: in 18,500 or 28,500 BC game was still plentiful, wild cereals scarce, the tools missing and densities too low to prize calories per acre.

Who survived as foragers

  • Foragers either were displaced or survived by farming. Where they were numerous or protected by geography they had time to adopt it (perhaps the US Southwest, the western Mediterranean, Atlantic Europe, parts of Japan); in Indonesia, tropical South-East Asia, most of Africa below the equator and probably parts of Europe they were replaced in prehistory, and in Australia and much of the western United States in modern times.
  • Only strong barriers let foragers persist in farmable land until modern times: California, cut off by deserts from Arizona’s farmers; the Cape, whose Mediterranean climate did not suit the Bantu’s equatorial crops; and Australia, separated by narrow seas from Indonesia and New Guinea. The last twentieth-century foragers survived only in deserts and the Arctic.

Chapter 7

How to Make an Almond

Early farmers made crops without meaning to. By picking the plants they liked, and by growing them in gardens, they selected for traits they could see (size, taste) and many they could not (non-shattering seeds, quick germination, self-pollination). Some plants, like oaks, defeated them for good reasons.

Domestication from the plant’s point of view

  • Wild almonds are deadly: a few dozen contain enough cyanide to kill you. Corn looks nothing like its wild ancestor. How did anyone domesticate them? Domestication means growing a plant and thereby changing it genetically, consciously or not, to make it more useful. Today it is a science (UC Davis has a whole Department of Pomology for apples), but farmers 10,000 years ago had no model and no idea of the result. Timing varied hugely: peas by 8000 BC, olives around 4000 BC, strawberries only in the Middle Ages, pecans in 1846, and oaks never.
  • Plants must hitchhike, so many wrap seeds in tasty fruit that signals ripeness: strawberries stay green and sour until the seeds are ready, then turn red for thrushes. Acorns suit squirrels, mangos bats, some sedges ants; an African melon grows mostly on aardwolf latrines. Seeds survive our guts too, so human latrines, spittoons and rubbish dumps were “the first agricultural research laboratories”, stocked with seeds of the plants people preferred.

Traits the gatherers could see

  • Size. Nobody fights mosquitoes for lousy little berries. Cultivated peas became ten times heavier than wild ones; supermarket apples are about three inches across, wild ones one (see the figure for corn).
  • Taste. Seeds evolve to be bitter so animals spit them out. Most wild almonds contain amygdalin, which breaks down into cyanide, but a mutation in a single gene switches it off. Birds eat all the sweet mutants in the wild; farmers’ children nibbling around the village would have found them (European peasants still prize the rare sweet-acorned oak). Wild almonds appear at Greek sites by 8000 BC and domestic ones in the eastern Mediterranean by 3000 BC; almonds were left in Tutankhamun’s tomb around 1325 BC. Lima beans, watermelons, potatoes, eggplants and cabbages also had bitter or poisonous ancestors.
  • Flesh, oil and fibre. Wild squashes are nearly all seed; bananas were selected to be all flesh and no seed, reversing the fruit’s whole purpose. Olives (cultivated since about 4000 BC), sesame, mustard, poppies and flax were chosen for oil; cotton was chosen for long lint independently in the Americas and the Old World; flax and hemp for long straight stems. Flax, domesticated by about 7000 BC, gave linen, Europe’s chief textile until the Industrial Revolution.

Traits nobody could see

  • Seed dispersal. Wild pea pods explode to scatter their seeds; a single-gene mutant that does not pop dies in the wild but is the only pod left for a human to pick. Wild wheat and barley stalks shatter to drop their seeds; the non-shattering mutant is fatal in nature but waits conveniently on the stalk. Harvesting and sowing reversed natural selection “by 180 degrees”. Selection for non-shattering wheat and barley, over 10,000 years ago, was apparently the first major human “improvement” of any plant and marks the start of agriculture in the Fertile Crescent. Lentils, flax and poppies went the same way.
  • Germination. In unpredictable climates, wild annuals hedge their bets with thick seed coats that stagger sprouting over years. Tilled, watered gardens reward seeds that sprout at once, so mutants without inhibitors took over wheat, barley, peas, flax and sunflowers, without anyone noticing.
  • Reproduction. A good mutant is lost if it interbreeds with normal plants, a problem for the self-incompatible hermaphrodites and separate-sexed species that make up most wild plants. Farmers ended up keeping mutants that breed true: fruit without pollination (seedless bananas, grapes, oranges, pineapples), self-fertilising plums, peaches, apples, apricots and cherries, hermaphrodite grapes.
  • Competition in gardens. As the Industrial Revolution’s soot favoured dark moths, dense sowing in rich soil favoured big, fast-growing seeds over the small ones that did well on dry hillsides.
  • So selection pushed plants in opposite directions: big seeds (sunflowers) or none (bananas), leaves (lettuce), seeds (wheat) or fruit (squash). Beets were grown for leaves in Babylonian times (like chard), then for roots, and in the eighteenth century for sugar. Wild cabbage became cabbage and kale (leaves), kohlrabi (stems), brussels sprouts (buds), and cauliflower and broccoli (flower shoots).

The Fertile Crescent’s sequence

  • The first crops (wheat, barley and peas, about 10,000 years ago) were ideal: edible and high-yielding in the wild, easily sown, harvestable within months, storable, mostly self-pollinating, and a few mutations from their wild form. Later stages were harder (see the figure).
  • Cereals are fast-growing, carbohydrate-rich, yield up to a ton of food per hectare, supply over half of all human calories today, and include five of the world’s twelve leading crops (wheat, corn, rice, barley, sorghum). Pulses, often 25 per cent protein (soybeans 38), make up their deficit. A local cereal-plus-pulse pairing launched farming in many regions (see the figure).

Old World and New World farming

  • Much of the Old World came to broadcast seed in handfuls over single-crop fields and, with cattle and horses, to plough. In the New World no animal could pull a plough, so fields were worked with digging sticks and hoes, seeds were planted one by one, and gardens mixed many crops.
  • Roots and tubers, negligible in the ancient Fertile Crescent and China, were staples elsewhere: manioc and sweet potato in tropical South America, potato and oca in the Andes, African yams, and Indo-Pacific yams and taro, with bananas and breadfruit, in South-East Asia and New Guinea.

Why oaks were never domesticated

  • Acorns, rich in starch and oil, were a staple in California and the eastern United States and a famine food for European peasants. Oaks have three strikes against them: an acorn may take a decade or more to become a productive tree (wheat takes months, an almond three or four years); billions of squirrels plant acorns everywhere, so humans could never control selection (beech and hickory likewise); and bitterness in oaks seems to be governed by many genes, not one, so a sweet tree’s offspring stay bitter, whereas half a sweet almond’s offspring are sweet.
  • Thrushes did the same to strawberries and raspberries, spreading the small berries they liked into every Roman garden, until nets and greenhouses finally let humans win.
  • Darwin opened On the Origin of Species not with Galápagos birds but with farmers breeding gooseberries: “the art has been simple, and … has been followed almost unconsciously.”

Chapter 8

Apples or Indians

Did farming lag in some regions because of the people or the plants? Comparing the Fertile Crescent with New Guinea and the eastern United States, Diamond finds the answer in the plants: people knew their floras intimately and grabbed better crops the moment they arrived.

The puzzle of the missing crops

  • Of some 200,000 wild flowering plants, only a few thousand are eaten and a few hundred domesticated. Just twelve species supply over 80 per cent of the world’s crop tonnage: wheat, corn, rice, barley, sorghum, soybean, potato, manioc, sweet potato, sugarcane, sugar beet and banana. All were domesticated thousands of years ago, and modern science has added no major food plant, which suggests ancient peoples found all the good ones.
  • Yet some failures look odd. Sorghum grows wild into southern Africa but was not grown there until Bantu farmers brought a whole package 2,000 years ago. Flax grows wild in western Europe and North Africa, einkorn in the southern Balkans, and olive, grape, fig and date far beyond the eastern Mediterranean, yet none was domesticated outside the Fertile Crescent until it arrived as a crop. Some 40 other olive species and many North American apples and grapes were never domesticated.
  • The flaw in the question: nomads do not settle down for one good plant. They farm only when enough domesticable plants and animals together make farming beat foraging. So the question is about whole floras and faunas.

Why the Fertile Crescent was so lucky

  • It is the best-studied region: for most of its crops the wild ancestor, its range, the genetic changes (often single genes), and the time and place of domestication are known.
  • A Mediterranean climate of mild wet winters and long hot dry summers favours annuals: herbs that die each summer after putting their energy into big dormant seeds rather than wood. Cereals and pulses make up six of the world’s twelve major crops.
  • Rich wild stands. Botanists harvesting wild cereals today get up to nearly a ton of seed per hectare, 50 calories of food for every calorie of work; some foragers settled in villages on wild grain before farming. Wheat and barley needed only a couple of mutations, and their wild ancestors look so like them that there was never doubt. Corn’s ancestor teosinte, by contrast, looks so different that its parentage was long disputed; it yielded less, hid its seeds in rock-hard cases, and needed drastic changes and thousands of years to reach modern cob sizes.
  • Selfers. Many of its plants usually self-pollinate, so good mutants breed true, yet occasionally cross, making new varieties: one such hybrid became bread wheat, the modern world’s most valuable crop. All eight founder crops were selfers, and the wheats carried 8–14 per cent protein, far more than rice or corn.
  • Western Eurasia’s Mediterranean zone beat the other four in the world (California, Chile, south-western Australia and the Cape) in five ways: it is by far the largest and so the most diverse; its climate varies most, favouring annuals; it holds the most large-seeded grasses (see the figure); it spans altitudes from the Dead Sea to 18,000-foot peaks near Tehran within short distances, so foragers could harvest up a mountainside as seeds ripened and farmers could move hillside cereals into damp valley bottoms; and it had wild goats, sheep, pigs and cattle, four of the world’s five most important domestic mammals, domesticated in slightly different parts of the region and soon shared.
  • Wild food was also relatively poor there (few big rivers, short coastlines, gazelle herds overhunted), so farming quickly won. As late as 9000 BC people had no crops or livestock; by 6000 BC some societies depended almost entirely on them. Mesoamerica, with only the turkey and dog and the difficult corn, may have begun domestication only around 3500 BC, and villages appeared only around 1500 BC.

Did people know their plants?

  • Ethnobiology finds traditional peoples “walking encyclopedias of natural history”, with names for a thousand or more species. Starving in the jungle, Diamond warned his Fore companions that mushrooms might be poisonous; they told him to shut up, explained that only Americans could be so stupid, and named 29 edible species, including the delicious tree-growing tanti.
  • At Tell Abu Hureyra on the Euphrates, where foragers lived year-round between 10,000 and 9000 BC, Gordon Hillman, Susan Colledge and David Harris analysed over 700 samples of charred seeds (over 500 each) and found 157 gathered species, all edible, easily detoxified, or useful for dyes and medicine, and none of the most toxic local weeds.
  • In the Jordan Valley, Ofer Bar-Yosef and Mordechai Kislev listed the 23 most palatable large-seeded wild grasses; the first farmers had chosen the two best. Emmer had the biggest seeds and barley the second biggest; barley was among the four most abundant and evolved domestic traits quickly; emmer was efficient to gather and threshed free of its husk.

New Guinea: independent, but limited

  • People have lived in New Guinea for at least 40,000 years. Foraging is poor there (the biggest land animals are a 100-pound cassowary and a 50-pound kangaroo), and every highlander today farms; highland hunters who run out of garden food starve despite knowing every wild plant. Farming began around 7000 BC, independently, and New Guineans domesticated sugarcane (whose tonnage today nearly equals wheat’s and corn’s combined), Australimusa bananas, the nut tree Canarium indicum, giant swamp taro, and perhaps breadfruit, yams and taro.
  • Its limits: no cereals (none of the 56 big-seeded grasses grows there); no domesticable large mammals (pigs, chickens and dogs came from South-East Asia within the last several thousand years); taro is barely 1 per cent protein, so highland children have swollen bellies, people eat mice, spiders and frogs, and protein starvation was probably the ultimate reason cannibalism was widespread; and its root crops did poorly at altitude.
  • When the South American sweet potato arrived, probably via Spanish Philippines, it grew higher, faster and yielded more, and set off a highland population explosion. New Guineans adopted it “exuberantly”. The limits “had nothing to do with New Guinea peoples, and everything with the New Guinea biota and environment.”

The eastern United States: nutritious but weak

  • Its four founder crops came in 2500–1500 BC, 6,000 years after wheat and barley: a squash (for containers and seeds), sunflower, sumpweed and goosefoot. For 2,000 years they were minor supplements to game, waterbirds, fish, shellfish and nuts; farming became a major part of the diet only in 500–200 BC, after knotweed, maygrass and little barley were added.
  • Nutritionally they were superb (see the figure), but goosefoot, knotweed, little barley and maygrass had seeds a tenth the size of wheat’s, and sumpweed, a wind-pollinated relative of ragweed, causes hay fever, smells, and irritates the skin. The region had no local pulse, fibre crop, fruit or nut tree, and no domestic animal but the dog.
  • Mexican corn arrived around AD 200 but stayed minor until a variety suited to short northern summers appeared around AD 900; beans came around AD 1100. Then the Mississippian chiefdoms boomed, the largest towns and most complex societies north of Mexico, too late to prepare for Europeans. The Hopewell florescence (about 200 BC to AD 400) came nearly 9,000 years after village life in the Fertile Crescent. Sumpweed vanished before 1492; only sunflower and the eastern squash (our acorn and summer squashes) survive as crops. The Native Americans themselves “passed judgment” on their crops by dropping them.
  • Even twentieth-century breeders have done little with North American wild plants beyond pecans, blueberries, and hybrids that improved Eurasian fruits.

Caveats, and the answer to the title

  • The Fertile Crescent’s lead followed from its wild species, not its people; “I am unaware of anyone’s even seriously suggesting” otherwise. Where farming never arose (California, Australia, the pampas, western Europe), Blumler’s grasses and next chapter’s mammals show the wild ancestors were even scarcer. Nor was foraging there especially rich: the Americas and Australia had lost their big mammals.
  • Two caveats. Peoples accept better crops only “in the long run and over large areas”; some societies resist, and those that adopt can outbreed and displace them. And poor floras delay farming rather than forbid it: a visitor in 3000 BC would have wrongly written off the eastern United States. South-eastern Aborigines, with winter villages, fish traps and long canals, might within a few thousand years have farmed fish, yams and small-seeded grasses had Europeans not arrived in 1788.
  • So why no North American apples? Apples need grafting and were among the last Eurasian fruit trees domesticated, around classical Greek times, 8,000 years after farming began. At the same pace Native Americans would have domesticated them around AD 5500. The failure lay “neither with the people nor with the apples” but with the whole suite of American wild species.

Chapter 9

Zebras, Unhappy Marriages, and the Anna Karenina Principle

Only 14 of the world’s 148 big wild herbivores were ever domesticated, and 13 of them lived in Eurasia. The reason is not the peoples but the animals: to be domesticable, a species must pass every one of six tests, and failing any one rules it out.

The principle

  • “Domesticable animals are all alike; every undomesticable animal is undomesticable in its own way.” Tolstoy’s opening line to Anna Karenina says a happy marriage must succeed on many counts (sex, money, children, religion, in-laws); failure on any one dooms it. Success generally means avoiding many separate causes of failure. So the question “apples or Indians?” becomes “zebras or Africans?”
  • Small domesticates mattered too: chickens (China), ducks and geese, turkeys (Mesoamerica), guinea fowl (Africa), Muscovy ducks (South America); dogs from wolves; rabbits, guinea pigs, a West African giant rat, perhaps the Caribbean hutia; ferrets and cats as hunters; fur foxes, mink, chinchillas and hamsters in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries; honeybees and silkworms. But none pulled a plough or wagon or carried a rider, so the chapter concerns the big ones.

The Ancient Fourteen

  • Counting mammals over 100 pounds, only 14 were domesticated before the twentieth century. The Major Five spread worldwide: sheep, goat, cow, pig and horse. The Minor Nine stayed regional: Arabian and Bactrian camels, llama/alpaca, donkey, reindeer, water buffalo, yak, Bali cattle (from the banteng) and mithan (from the gaur).
  • Elephants were tamed (Hannibal’s, today’s Asian work elephants) but never domesticated, since a domestic animal is selectively bred in captivity and thereby changed. Domesticates changed in size (cows, pigs and sheep got smaller, guinea pigs bigger), in wool and milk, and often in brain and sense organs, which they no longer needed to escape predators. Compare a dachshund with a wolf.
  • Their wild ancestors were lopsidedly Eurasian (see the figure): South America had one (the guanaco, ancestor of the llama and alpaca); North America, Australia and Africa south of the Sahara had none, astonishingly for a continent tourists visit for its mammals. Seven of the thirteen Eurasian ancestors lived in Southwest Asia.

Not the peoples: five kinds of evidence

  • Rapid adoption. When Eurasian livestock reached Africa, diverse peoples took them up: cattle- and sheep-owning Bantu farmers overran most of the continent’s foragers; Khoisan who got cattle and sheep about 2,000 years ago displaced Khoisan hunters; horses turned West Africa into cavalry kingdoms, stopped only by tsetse-borne trypanosomes. Plains Indians, horseless until the late seventeenth century, became famous mounted warriors; Spanish sheep gave the Navajo their woollen blankets; within a decade of European settlement Aboriginal Tasmanians were breeding dogs. Had zebras or buffalo been domesticable, some African tribe would have done it.
  • Pets everywhere. New Guineans keep kangaroos, possums, ospreys and cassowary chicks (which as adults occasionally disembowel villagers); Asians tame eagles; Egyptians, Assyrians and Indians tamed cheetahs; Egyptian paintings show tame gazelles, hartebeest, cranes, giraffes and even hyenas; the Ainu of Japan raised brown bear cubs for ritual killing. Francis Galton: “every wild animal has had its chance of being domesticated”; the rest, “who failed sometimes in only one small particular, are destined to perpetual wildness.”
  • Speed. Every big mammal with a known date was domesticated between about 8000 and 2500 BC (see the figure), and none since. Small mammals kept coming (rabbits in the Middle Ages, lab mice and rats in the twentieth century, hamsters in the 1930s), but for big ones every candidate had been tried.
  • Repeat domestications. Indian humped cattle and humpless western cattle come from wild aurochs subspecies that diverged hundreds of thousands of years ago; North Africans may have domesticated a third. Wolves became dogs in the Americas and probably several parts of Eurasia; pigs were domesticated in China and western Eurasia. The same few suitable species attracted everyone.
  • Modern failures. Scientific projects with eland (at Askaniya-Nova in Ukraine and in England, Kenya, Zimbabwe and South Africa), red deer (the Rowett Institute, Aberdeen), moose (Pechero-Ilych in Russia), musk ox, zebra and bison have yielded nothing ranchers want. Eland would have huge advantages in Africa, yet have not caught on. Zoos control condor breeding by computer, but control is not domestication.

Six ways to fail

  • Diet. Each step up the food chain keeps about 10 per cent: 10,000 pounds of corn make a 1,000-pound cow, but 1,000 pounds of carnivore take 100,000 pounds of corn. So no carnivore was domesticated for food (not because it tastes bad: Diamond vouches for lion burger). Dogs were eaten only where meat was scarce (Aztec Mexico, Polynesia, ancient China), fattened on vegetables and garbage. Finicky herbivores like koalas fail too.
  • Growth rate. Gorillas and elephants eat anything but take some 15 years to grow; Asians find it cheaper to capture and tame wild elephants.
  • Captive breeding. Cheetahs, prized hunters (one Mughal emperor kept a thousand), would not breed in cages, because the female needs days of rough chasing by several brothers; zoos managed the first captive birth only in 1960. Vicuñas, with the finest wool, need long courtship, hate rival males and need separate feeding and sleeping territories; the Incas corralled, sheared and released wild ones, as merchants still must.
  • Nasty disposition. Grizzlies would be perfect meat animals (up to 1,700 pounds, broad diet, fast growth) but the Ainu prudently killed their cubs at one year. African buffalo are Africa’s most dangerous big mammal; hippos kill more people than lions. Among the eight equids, horses and donkeys were domesticated, but onagers, hunted and hybridised by Sumerians, bite and have irascible tempers, and zebras are worse: they bite and don’t let go, injure more American zookeepers than tigers, and duck the lasso. Lord Walter Rothschild drove zebras through London, but nobody rides them.
  • Panic. Nervous flight species batter themselves to death in pens. Gazelles, the Fertile Crescent’s most hunted game for thousands of years, leap nearly 30 feet and run at 50 miles an hour, and were never domesticated.
  • Social structure. Domesticates descend from herd animals with a dominance hierarchy and overlapping home ranges: a horse herd moves in a fixed order behind the top mare, so pack horses follow a human the same way, and young animals imprint on people. Solitary territorial animals cannot be herded (cats and ferrets were kept as solitary hunters). Herds with exclusive territories, species territorial in the breeding season (most deer and antelope, except reindeer), and herds without clear hierarchies (North American bighorn sheep, unlike the Asian mouflon) all fail.

Many are called, but few are chosen

  • Eurasia’s luck rested on three facts: it started with the most candidates, being biggest and most varied (Africa has less rain forest than South-East Asia and no temperate zone beyond 37 degrees); it lost the fewest in the late-Pleistocene extinctions, which struck Australia and the Americas, whose animals met skilled hunters suddenly; and a higher share of its survivors passed all six tests.
  • Diamond closes with Saint Matthew: “Many are called, but few are chosen.”

Chapter 10

Spacious Skies and Tilted Axes

Eurasia runs east–west, so crops and animals could spread along the same latitude, with the same day lengths, seasons and diseases. The Americas and Africa run north–south, across climate zones, deserts and tropics, so farming, and later wheels and writing, spread slowly or not at all.

Measuring the spread

  • The Americas stretch 9,000 miles north to south but at most 3,000 east to west, narrowing to 40 miles at Panama; Africa too runs mainly north–south; Eurasia runs east–west. Only five to nine areas invented food production, so everywhere else depended on its spread: from Southwest Asia to Europe, Egypt, Ethiopia, Central Asia and the Indus; from the Sahel and West Africa to the east and south; from China to South-East Asia, Korea and Japan; from Mesoamerica north.
  • Some suitable lands never received it in prehistory: California from the US Southwest, Australia from New Guinea and Indonesia, South Africa’s Cape from Natal. Where it did spread, east–west was fast and north–south slow (see the figure). Neither Andean domestic mammal, the llama/alpaca or the guinea pig, ever reached Mesoamerica, though manioc, sweet potatoes and peanuts did.
  • Preemptive domestication is a subtler sign. If a crop spreads fast, nobody bothers to domesticate its wild ancestor again, or its close relatives. Most Southwest Asian crops show a single origin (all cultivated peas share the same recessive non-popping gene), and none of their wild relatives, such as the tasty wild pea Pisum fulvum, was ever domesticated. In the Americas, lima beans, common beans and chili peppers were domesticated at least twice (Mesoamerica and South America), and the squash Cucurbita pepo and goosefoot twice (Mesoamerica and the eastern United States); different species of cotton, chilis, squashes, amaranths and chenopods were domesticated north and south. Mesoamerican cotton, Gossypium hirsutum, is 95 per cent of the world’s cotton today, yet South Americans grew G. barbadense.

The Fertile Crescent package spreads

  • Soon after farming arose there, somewhat before 8000 BC, a wave spread outward (see the figure), as mapped by Daniel Zohary and Maria Hopf. Not every piece travelled (Egypt was too warm for einkorn) and pieces came at different times (sheep before cereals in south-western Europe), but the same package launched farming everywhere, followed by wheels, writing, metalworking, milking, fruit trees, beer and wine.
  • These were not local domestications. Of the eight founders only barley grows wild in Egypt, yet the Nile valley’s similar conditions let the package fuel Egyptian civilisation: “The sphinx and pyramids were built by people fed on crops originally native to the Fertile Crescent.” Even wild flax (Britain to the Caspian) and barley (east to Tibet) were not redomesticated: cultivated varieties share single chromosome arrangements and mutations.

Why latitude matters

  • Places at the same latitude share day length and its seasonal changes exactly, and tend to share temperatures, rainfall, habitats and diseases. Portugal, northern Iran and Japan, each 4,000 miles from the next, are more alike than any is to a place 1,000 miles due south. Rain forest lies within about 10 degrees of the equator, Mediterranean scrub (chaparral, maquis) between about 30 and 40 degrees.
  • Plants are genetically programmed to germinate, grow and flower on local seasonal cues. Mexican corn planted in Canada would try to sprout in March under ten feet of snow; reprogrammed to sprout in June, it would still grow at a leisurely five-month pace and die in the autumn frost, carrying resistance to the wrong diseases. So Fertile Crescent crops thrive in France and Japan but not at the equator.
  • People are the same: northern Europeans settled cool North America, Australia, South Africa and the highlands of Kenya and New Guinea, and died in droves of malaria in tropical lowlands.
  • Eurasia’s axis also let crops flow back toward the Fertile Crescent. Of Roman crops only oats and poppies were native to Italy (see the figure); even Rome’s native apples were grown by grafting, a technique from China. A similar subtropical package from South China (bananas, taro, yams, chickens, pigs, dogs) spread more than 5,000 miles east to Polynesia within 1,600 years.

Africa’s barrier

  • Fertile Crescent crops reached Egypt quickly and the cool Ethiopian highlands, then stopped: 2,000 miles of tropics separated them from South Africa’s ideal Mediterranean climate. Farming south of the Sahara was launched instead by local sorghum and African yams, adapted to warmth, summer rain and steady day length.
  • Livestock were slowed by climate and tsetse-borne trypanosomes. Horses never got south of West Africa’s kingdoms; cattle, sheep and goats halted for 2,000 years at the northern edge of the Serengeti and reached South Africa only in AD 1–200, some 8,000 years after domestication. Bantu farmers and their tropical crops arrived just after, but could not cross the Fish River into the Mediterranean-climate Cape.
  • The result was modern South Africa’s history: Khoisan herders without crops, outnumbered and replaced north-east of the Fish by Bantu farmers; European settlers in 1652 whose Fertile Crescent crops at last thrived at the Cape; the Khoisan decimated by germs and guns; a century of wars, a century of racial oppression, and a search for coexistence “in the former Khoisan lands”.

The Americas’ barrier

  • Highland Mexico to highland Ecuador is 1,200 miles, like the Balkans to Mesopotamia; the Balkans received the whole Fertile Crescent package within 2,000 years. But Central America’s hot lowlands stopped llamas, guinea pigs and potatoes, which would have thrived in cool highland Mexico. Five thousand years after the llama’s domestication, the Olmecs, Maya and Aztecs still had no pack animal and no edible domestic mammal but the dog. Turkeys and sunflowers never went south.
  • Just 700 miles kept Mexican corn, squash and beans from the US Southwest for several thousand years, and chilis and chenopods never got there. Corn reached the eastern US between AD 1 and 200 but mattered only after hardy northern varieties around AD 900, fuelling the Mississippian culture, “a brief flowering ended by European-introduced germs”.
  • Smaller north–south barriers also slowed exchange between the Indus Valley and South India, South China and Peninsular Malaysia, and kept tropical Indonesian and New Guinean crops from Australia’s south-western and south-eastern corners (more than 2,000 miles south of the equator), today its breadbaskets, until European ships brought cool-climate crops.

Beyond latitude

  • Deserts and mountains matter too. The dry southern Plains slowed exchange between the US Southeast and Southwest though they share a latitude; the shift to summer rain delayed farming in India’s Ganges plain; the Central Asian deserts, Tibetan plateau and Himalayas isolated China, which is why its farming was independent. Conversely, a 2,000-mile north–south shift was no barrier where rainfall was similar: to Ethiopia, and for the Bantu from the Great Lakes to Natal. Mesoamerica’s narrowness and lack of highland south of Guatemala throttled exchange as much as latitude.
  • Axes shaped other diffusion. The wheel, invented around 3000 BC in or near Southwest Asia, crossed much of Eurasia within a few centuries, while prehistoric Mexico’s wheels never reached the Andes. The alphabet, developed in the western Fertile Crescent by 1500 BC, reached Carthage and India within about a thousand years; Mesoamerican writing, flourishing for at least 2,000 years, never reached the Andes. The links are indirect, through farming societies: early wheels were on ox carts, early writing served elites fed by peasants.
  • “America the Beautiful” reverses the geography: no amber waves of native grain ever stretched from Atlantic to Pacific, Canada to Patagonia, or Egypt to South Africa, while wheat and barley stretched across Eurasia’s spacious skies. This is not a sign of Eurasian ingenuity: “Around those axes turned the fortunes of history.”

Part Three

From Food to Guns, Germs, and Steel

How food production became the proximate advantages: epidemic diseases, writing, technology and centralised political organisation.

Chapter 11

Lethal Gift of Livestock

The great epidemic killers are crowd diseases that evolved from the germs of domestic herd animals and can survive only in large, dense populations. Eurasia had the animals and the crowds; the Americas had neither. So the exchange of germs was almost entirely one-way, and it did more of the conquering than guns and steel.

From food to germs

  • A naked farmer has no advantage over a naked hunter-gatherer. But ten naked farmers beat one, and farmers are not naked: they “breathe out nastier germs”, own better weapons and technology, and live under centralised governments with literate elites. The next four chapters trace those links.
  • A physician friend of Diamond’s, as a tired young doctor, asked a timid immigrant with a mysterious pneumonia, through his wife as interpreter, about sexual exposures. The man stammered a confession; his wife struck him with a heavy metal bottle and stormed out. He had had repeated intercourse with sheep on the family farm. Few love sheep that way, but we keep a great many (Australia’s 17,085,400 people kept 161,600,000 sheep at a recent census), and smallpox, flu, tuberculosis, malaria, plague, measles and cholera all evolved from animal diseases.
  • Until the Second World War, more war victims died of war-borne microbes than of wounds. The winners were often “merely those bearing the nastiest germs”.

Disease from the germ’s point of view

  • Microbes are selected, like everything else, for spreading their offspring: the number of new victims per patient. Some wait passively: salmonella in eggs, trichinosis worms in undercooked pork, anisakiasis worms in raw fish, and kuru, New Guinea’s “laughing sickness”, spread when babies licked their fingers after playing with the raw brains of kuru victims their mothers were preparing to cook. Others ride insects (mosquitoes for malaria, fleas for plague, lice for typhus, tsetse flies for sleeping sickness) or pass from mother to foetus (syphilis, rubella, AIDS).
  • Others manipulate us. Genital sores inoculate the next host; smallpox lesions spread by contact (even, when US whites sent Native Americans blankets used by smallpox patients); flu, colds and whooping cough make us cough and sneeze; cholera causes massive diarrhoea into water supplies; Korean haemorrhagic fever travels in mouse urine; rabies drives dogs to bite; hookworms and schistosomes burrow through skin. “Symptoms” are the germ’s broadcasting strategies, and killing the host is a by-product: an untreated cholera patient may die from several gallons of diarrhoea a day, but meanwhile spreads the bacterium widely.
  • We fight back with fever (baking the germs), with antibodies (lifelong for measles, mumps, rubella, pertussis and smallpox, the basis of vaccination), and with natural selection, which spreads resistance genes through exposed populations, sometimes at a price: sickle-cell against malaria in Africans, perhaps Tay-Sachs against tuberculosis in Ashkenazi Jews, cystic fibrosis against bacterial diarrhoeas in northern Europeans. Germs counter by changing antigens: new flu strains, malaria, sleeping sickness, and AIDS, which evolves inside a single patient.

Crowd diseases

  • Epidemic diseases spread fast, are acute (you die or recover quickly), leave survivors immune, and live only in humans. So they burn through a population and vanish until a new crop of children is born and an infected outsider arrives. Measles reached the Faeroe Islands in 1781 and died out; in 1846 a carpenter from Denmark brought it back, and within three months almost all 7,782 islanders had had it. Measles dies out in any population under about half a million.
  • Small bands cannot sustain such diseases, so they neither evolve them nor resist them. In 1902 dysentery from the whaler Active killed 51 of the 56 Sadlermiut Eskimos of Southampton Island. Small populations have older kinds of infection: those with animal or soil reservoirs (yellow fever in African monkeys, carried to the Americas by the slave trade), slow chronic ones (leprosy reached about 40 per cent in New Guinea’s isolated Karimui Basin in the 1960s; yaws), and non-immunising parasites like hookworm.
  • Farming multiplied densities 10 to 100 times, settled people amid their own sewage, spread faeces on fields, created snail-infested irrigation water and rat-attracting food stores, and cleared forest for malarial mosquitoes. Cities were worse: until the early twentieth century Europe’s cities needed a constant flow of healthy peasants to replace their dead. Trade routes joined the Old World into one breeding ground (see the figure). In 1991 a plane stopping in Lima delivered dozens of cholera-infected passengers to Los Angeles the same day.

From animals to us

  • Crowd diseases could not exist before crowds, so they had to evolve from somewhere: from the crowd diseases of social animals we domesticated (see the figure). Measles is closest to rinderpest of cattle, which does not infect people; peasants sleeping beside cows for 9,000 years gave it ample chance to jump.
  • Diamond traces four stages. First, occasional infections from animals that do not pass between people: cat-scratch fever, leptospirosis from dogs, psittacosis from chickens and parrots, brucellosis from cattle, tularaemia from skinning wild rabbits. Second, new diseases that spread person to person and then die out: O’nyong-nyong fever in East Africa in 1959 (millions infected), Fort Bragg fever in the US in 1942, kuru, which was exterminating the 20,000-strong Fore until Australian control ended cannibalism around 1959, the English sweats and the Picardy sweats. Third, recent arrivals still establishing themselves: Lassa fever (Nigeria, 1969), Lyme disease (first US cases 1962), AIDS. Fourth, the old established killers, survivors of countless failed jumps.
  • Germs adapt as they jump. Typhus moved from rat fleas to human body lice, and in deloused America found flying squirrels in attics. Myxomatosis, introduced in 1950 to kill Australia’s European rabbits, evolved to kill fewer so it could spread further (see the figure), just as syphilis evolved from a months-long killer in 1495 to a slow disease by 1546.

The Americas: death in bed

  • Cortés landed in 1519 with 600 Spaniards, reached Tenochtitlan and fought his way out losing “only” two-thirds of his force. When he returned, the Aztecs fought street by street, but smallpox had arrived in 1520 with one infected slave from Cuba and killed nearly half of them, including the emperor Cuitlahuac, sparing Spaniards as if to advertise their invincibility. Mexico’s population of about 20 million fell to about 1.6 million by 1618. Pizarro’s luck in Peru came from smallpox around 1526.
  • In North America, germs did everything. When Hernando de Soto marched through the south-east in 1540 he found towns abandoned two years earlier after epidemics spread inland from coastal contacts. When the French reached the lower Mississippi in the late 1600s the big towns he saw were gone, leaving only mound sites. Schoolchildren were once told North America held about one million Indians, a convenient number; the estimate now is about 20 million, with a 95 per cent decline across the New World within a century or two. In 1837 smallpox from a Missouri steamboat cut one Mandan village from 2,000 to fewer than 40 in weeks.
  • Elsewhere, mortality among unexposed peoples ran from 50 to 100 per cent (see the figure): Hispaniola’s Indians fell from around 8 million in 1492 to zero by 1535; measles brought by a Fijian chief from Australia in 1875 killed about a quarter of Fijians, after earlier epidemics since 1791; Hawaii fell from about half a million in 1779 (when Captain Cook brought syphilis, gonorrhoea, tuberculosis and flu) to 84,000 in 1853, when smallpox killed about 10,000 more.

Why not the other way?

  • Over a dozen major Old World diseases took hold in the Americas; perhaps no major killer went the other way, except possibly syphilis, whose origin is disputed. Yet the Americas were densely peopled (Tenochtitlan was among the world’s biggest cities). Dense populations began somewhat later there, and the Andes, Mesoamerica and the Mississippi were never linked by fast trade. (A reported case of tuberculosis in a 1,000-year-old Peruvian mummy could not be distinguished from a related wild-animal germ.)
  • The main reason is simpler: what could American crowd diseases have evolved from? Only five animals were domesticated in the Americas (turkey, llama/alpaca, guinea pig, Muscovy duck and dog), after about 80 per cent of big American mammals vanished 13,000 years ago. Turkeys and Muscovy ducks don’t live in huge flocks or get cuddled; guinea pigs may have given a trypanosome infection like Chagas’ disease. Llamas had four strikes: small herds, limited numbers (never beyond the Andes), no milk drunk, and no living indoors. New Guinea highland mothers, by contrast, sometimes nurse piglets, and peasants keep pigs and cows in their huts.
  • Germs did not always favour Europe. Malaria, cholera and yellow fever made tropical Africa, Asia, Indonesia and New Guinea deadly, which is why Africa and New Guinea were partitioned nearly 400 years after the Americas, and why those diseases, once shipped to the Americas, helped wreck the French Panama Canal and nearly the American one.
  • Europe’s weapons and organisation alone do not explain how so few immigrants supplanted so many natives. That needed “Europe’s sinister gift to other continents”.

Chapter 12

Blueprints and Borrowed Letters

Writing was invented from scratch perhaps only twice (Sumer and Mesoamerica), maybe also in China and Egypt; everyone else copied a blueprint or borrowed the idea. It arose only in stratified farming societies that needed records and could feed scribes, and it failed to reach societies cut off by distance and barriers.

Why writing mattered

  • Nineteenth-century writers saw history as a march from savagery to civilisation, with writing the sharpest mark. Until Islam and European colonialism it was absent from Australia, the Pacific, Africa below the equator, and the whole New World except part of Mesoamerica. Not always decisive (the Incas ruled an empire without it; Rome lost to the Huns), writing still marched with weapons, microbes and states: royal orders, maps, sailing directions, alluring accounts of earlier voyages, and imperial administration.
  • Questions: why did no hunter-gatherers ever write? Why Minoan Crete but not Polynesian Tonga? Why did writing arise in Iraq nearly 4,000 years before Japan or Scandinavia, now far more literate? Why did it spread from the Fertile Crescent to Ethiopia but not from Mexico to the Andes?

Three strategies

  • Scripts differ in the unit each sign stands for (see the figure). No real system is pure: English uses logograms like numerals, $ and %; “syllabic” Linear B had many logograms; “logographic” Egyptian hieroglyphs included syllable signs and a virtual alphabet of consonants.

Inventing writing: Sumer

  • Inventing from nothing was vastly harder than borrowing: the first scribes had to decide how to cut continuous speech into units, ignore variations in pitch, speed and accent, and match symbols to sounds with no model to follow. The two indisputable independent inventions are Sumerian cuneiform, somewhat before 3000 BC, and Mesoamerican writing, before 600 BC; Egypt (3000 BC) and China (by 1300 BC) may also have invented it.
  • In Sumer, farming villages had used clay tokens for accounts (sheep, grain) for thousands of years. In the last centuries before 3000 BC came flat clay tablets, reed styluses replacing pointed scratching tools, and conventions we now take for granted: ruled rows, read left to right and top to bottom. Most early tablets, from Uruk, 200 miles south-east of Baghdad, are pictures of objects and numerals in telegraphic accounting shorthand; signs combined to make new meanings (head plus bread meant “eat”).
  • Perhaps the most important single step in the history of writing was the rebus principle: writing an abstract word with the picture of a drawable word that sounds the same. “Arrow” and “life” were both ti in Sumerian, so the arrow sign meant either, with a silent “determinative” sign to show which. Phonetic signs then spelled grammatical endings and longer words (as if English wrote believe with a bee and a leaf). Sumerian became a mix of logograms, phonetic signs and determinatives, still far from a full syllabary.

Inventing writing: Mesoamerica

  • About a dozen related Mesoamerican scripts are known, mostly only partly deciphered: the earliest is Zapotec, around 600 BC; the best understood is Lowland Maya, whose oldest written date is AD 292. There is no convincing evidence of pre-Norse contact with literate Old World societies, and the signs are entirely different.
  • Yet Maya writing works on the same principles as Sumerian: logograms, rebus writing of abstract words, and consonant-plus-vowel syllable signs (ta, te, ti, to, tu), often derived from pictures of things whose names begin with that syllable (the sign ne looks like a tail, neh). This “testifies to the underlying universality of human creativity”.

Blueprint copying and idea diffusion

  • Few inventions happened because writing is hard to invent and because the first systems spread before others could finish the centuries of experiment needed; they “preempted” further inventions. Borrowing ranges from blueprint copying (adapting a detailed model) to idea diffusion (knowing only that it can be done). Historians argue which Russia’s atomic bomb owed more to: stolen American plans, or Hiroshima’s proof that it worked.
  • Blueprint copying is how linguists write unwritten languages today (Turkey’s Roman alphabet of 1928; Cyrillic scripts for Russia’s tribal languages; missionary alphabets for New Guinea). St Cyril adapted Greek and Hebrew letters for the Slavs in the ninth century; Bishop Ulfilas made a Gothic alphabet in the fourth, from about 20 Greek letters, five Roman, and two runic or invented. Linear B was adapted from Minoan Linear A by about 1400 BC. Borrowers drop letters they don’t need (Finns dropped b, c, f, g, w, x, z), combine letters (th), add marks (ñ, ö), recycle letters (Czech c for ts) or invent new ones (medieval j, u, w).
  • The alphabet arose only once, among Semitic speakers between Syria and Sinai in the second millennium BC, from around 1700 BC. Egyptian hieroglyphs already had 24 signs for the 24 consonants, but Egyptians never dropped the rest. Three innovations made the alphabet: signs only for single consonants; a fixed order with memorable names that begin with their letter (’aleph “ox”, beth “house”, gimel “camel”, daleth “door”), still echoed in alpha, beta, gamma, delta; and vowels, written systematically first by the Greeks in the eighth century BC by recycling five Phoenician consonant letters. Its descendants are shown in the figure.
  • Idea diffusion: around 1810 the Cherokee blacksmith Sequoyah, illiterate and speaking no English, saw that whites’ marks recorded speech. After trying pictures and then word-signs (thousands, still not enough), he realised words were made of recurring syllables, devised 200 signs and cut them to 85. About two dozen were English letters from a spelling book used with new values (D for a, R for e, b for si, h for ni, the numeral 4 for se). The Cherokees soon reached almost 100 per cent literacy and printed books and newspapers. He had reinvented a syllabary 3,500 years after the Minoans.
  • Other idea diffusions: Korea’s han’gul (King Sejong, 1446), with letters grouped into syllable blocks, related shapes for related sounds, and consonants drawn as the mouth positions that make them; Ireland’s ogham (from about the fourth century AD), with letters based on a five-finger hand code. Easter Island’s script, first preserved around 1851, may have been inspired by a Spanish annexation proclamation handed over in 1770.
  • China’s writing (first attested around 1300 BC) is probably independent: no early script is known between the Indus Valley (writing by 2200 BC, 2,600 miles away) and China. Egypt is more suspicious: hieroglyphs appear suddenly and nearly complete around 3000 BC, 800 miles from Sumer, its trading partner, with no trace of earlier experiments despite a climate that preserves them; and new scripts then appear in Iran, Crete and Turkey (proto-Elamite, Cretan pictographs, Hieroglyphic Hittite). Diamond suspects idea diffusion.

Early writing served kings and priests

  • Early scripts were incomplete, ambiguous, complex, or all three. Early cuneiform could only say the equivalent of “John 27 fat sheep”; later it rendered prose with hundreds of signs. Linear B had about 90 syllable signs but dropped word-final consonants and used one sign for l and r, another for p, b and ph, another for g, k and kh: as if “rap”, “lap”, “lab” and “laugh” were spelled alike.
  • Few could write. All surviving Linear B tablets from Knossos and Pylos, identified by handwriting, are the work of about 75 and 40 scribes. About 90 per cent of the earliest Sumerian tablets at Uruk record goods paid in, rations and produce; a third of Knossos’s Linear B tablets are sheep and wool accounts, and Pylos’s are strikingly about flax. The Iliad and Odyssey were composed and passed on by non-literate bards. Early Egyptian, Maya and Chinese writing likewise served propaganda, royal events, astronomy and divination (Shang oracle bones: “If the child is born on a keng day, it will be extremely auspicious”).
  • Ambiguity suited the rulers, who wanted scribes to count sheep owed in taxes, not the masses writing poetry and hatching plots. Lévi-Strauss: early writing’s main function was “to facilitate the enslavement of other human beings.”
  • The alphabet reversed the order. Linear B vanished with Mycenaean Greece around 1200 BC; when writing returned in the eighth century BC it was an alphabet used in homes for poetry and jokes. The first Greek inscription, on an Athenian wine jug of about 740 BC, offers the jug to the nimblest dancer; the next, on a cup, reads: “I am Nestor’s delicious drinking cup. Whoever drinks from this cup swiftly will the desire of fair-crowned Aphrodite seize him.” The earliest Etruscan and Roman inscriptions are also on drinking vessels.

Why writing stopped where it did

  • Every invention and early adoption of writing happened in stratified, centralised societies with full-time bureaucrats fed by peasant surpluses; hunter-gatherers never developed or adopted it. Writing arose independently in the Fertile Crescent, Mexico and probably China because those were the first food producers in their hemispheres, then spread by trade, conquest and religion.
  • But food production was not enough. The Inca Empire (among the world’s largest in 1520), Tonga, Hawaii, all of Africa’s states south of the Sahara before Islam, and the Mississippian chiefdoms had no writing. They started food production later and were too far, or too cut off, from the first centres (see the figure). Distance as the crow flies misleads: the Sahara separated literate North Africa from West Africa, northern Mexico’s deserts separated Mesoamerica from the Mississippi, and the forested, never urbanised Isthmus of Darien separated Mexico from the Andes. Yet the alphabet travelled much further, from the eastern Mediterranean to Ireland, Ethiopia and South-East Asia, within 2,000 years.

Chapter 13

Necessity’s Mother

Invention is usually the mother of necessity, not the other way round; it is cumulative, not heroic; and whether a society adopts it depends on so many factors that, across a continent, receptivity is effectively random. What matters is how many societies there are and how easily ideas can flow between them: so technology raced ahead in big, connected Eurasia.

The Phaistos disk

  • On 3 July 1908 archaeologists at the Minoan palace of Phaistos on Crete found a baked clay disk 6½ inches across, covered on both sides with 241 signs spiralling in five coils, grouped like words. Its 45 distinct signs suggest a syllabary; it is undeciphered, and no other scrap of its script has turned up in 89 years.
  • Dated to about 1700 BC, it is by far the world’s earliest printed document: its signs were punched with at least 45 stamps bearing raised type. Printing did not appear again for 2,500 years in China and 3,100 in Europe. If inventions are that idiosyncratic, can technology’s history be generalised at all?
  • Technology (weapons and transport) is the direct means of conquest, and all the big modern inventions were Eurasian. In 1800 New Guineans and Aboriginal Australians still used stone tools, though New Guinea has some of the world’s richest copper and Australia some of its richest iron. Three explanations are popular: Eurasians are smarter (rejected); rare geniuses (Gutenberg, Watt, Edison, the Wrights) happened to be born in Europe; or some whole societies are conservative.

Invention is the mother of necessity

  • Some inventions do answer a need: the Manhattan Project (1942, three years, $2 billion), Eli Whitney’s cotton gin (1794), Watt’s steam engine for pumping mines (1769). But most major modern breakthroughs, from the aeroplane and car to the light bulb, phonograph and transistor, were inventions in search of a use.
  • Edison’s phonograph (1877) came with a list of ten uses, including recording dying words, books for the blind, announcing the time and teaching spelling; he later said it had no commercial value, then sold it as an office dictating machine and objected when others made jukeboxes. Only after some 20 years did he concede its main use was music.
  • Nikolaus Otto’s gas engine of 1866 was weak, heavy and seven feet tall, at a time when horses had served for nearly 6,000 years and railways worked. Daimler made the first motorcycle in 1885 and truck in 1896; in 1905 cars were unreliable toys for the rich. The First World War convinced armies they needed trucks, and even then the switch in big American cities took 50 years. The US issues about 70,000 patents a year, few of which reach production.
  • Uses shift too: Watt’s mine pump soon powered cotton mills, then, more profitably, locomotives and boats. Gasoline was the waste fraction of nineteenth-century petroleum distillation for lamp oil until internal-combustion engines needed it.

No heroes, only improvers

  • Patent law encourages the “heroic theory of invention”, since inventors must claim novelty. In reality every famous invention had predecessors (see the figure): Watt got his idea repairing a model of Newcomen’s engine; Edison’s bulb of 21 October 1879 improved on bulbs patented between 1841 and 1878; the Wrights followed Lilienthal’s manned gliders and Langley’s unmanned powered plane; Morse followed Henry, Cooke and Wheatstone; Whitney’s gin for short-staple cotton extended gins used on long-staple cotton for thousands of years.
  • Would world history have changed if some genius had not been born? “There has never been any such person.” The Phaistos printer’s tragedy was to invent something his society could not use.
  • Ancient invention was even more cumulative. Nobody noticing burned sand and lime in an Ice Age hearth could foresee glazes (about 4000 BC), free-standing glass objects (about 2500 BC), glass vessels (about 1500 BC) and Roman glass windows (about AD 1). New Guineans still “invent” this way, picking up unfamiliar things in the forest or Diamond’s abandoned camp: tin cans become containers, a pencil becomes an earlobe ornament, broken glass a knife. Petroleum was distilled from asphalt in Mesopotamia by 2000 BC; Greeks made incendiaries; Islamic alchemists distilled fiercer ones against the Crusaders; the Chinese found gunpowder, and an Islamic treatise of about 1100 gives seven recipes, one of 1280 more than 70.

Why a society accepts an invention

  • Economic advantage. Ancient Mexicans made wheeled toys with axles but never vehicles, because without draught animals wheels beat no porter.
  • Prestige. Designer jeans at double the price; Japan’s cumbersome kanji kept over its efficient kana because of its prestige.
  • Vested interests. The QWERTY keyboard of 1873 was “anti-engineering”, designed to slow typists so typewriters would not jam; a 1932 trial of an efficient layout doubled speed and cut effort by 95 per cent, but QWERTY’s users, teachers and makers kept it. Sony licensed the American-invented transistor while US firms protected vacuum-tube sets; British cities, invested in gas, kept gas street lighting into the 1920s.
  • Visibility. In 1340 the earls of Derby and Salisbury saw Arabs use cannon at Tarifa in Spain; England used cannon against the French at Crécy six years later.

Why societies differ, and why it doesn’t matter

  • Historians propose at least 14 factors: long life expectancy; five economic and organisational ones (cheap slave labour, patent law, technical training, investment capital, individualism versus family obligations: a New Guinean who earns money soon has a dozen relatives to feed); four ideological ones (risk-taking, the scientific outlook, tolerance of heretics, religion); and four that cut both ways (war, centralised government, harsh or benign climate, resource abundance or scarcity: why didn’t even rainier New Guinea develop water mills, or deforested China coal?). None is tied to geography.
  • Claims that whole continents are conservative (Aborigines living in the Dreamtime, Africans inward-looking) are pure speculation and circular. On every continent innovative and conservative societies live side by side. In New Guinea’s highlands, first contacted in the 1930s, the Chimbu took up coffee; in 1964 Diamond met an illiterate Chimbu in a grass skirt, born into a stone-tool society, who had bought a $100,000 sawmill and a fleet of trucks. Their Daribi neighbours glanced at the first helicopter and went back to work; now the Chimbu are taking their land. Nigeria’s Ibo and America’s Navajo (weavers with Western dyes, silversmiths, ranchers, truck drivers) are similar. Among Aborigines, the Tasmanians kept the simplest tools while south-eastern fishing groups built canals, weirs and traps.
  • Receptivity also changes over time. Medieval Islam was more literate than Europe, preserved Greek books, invented or improved windmills, tidal mills, trigonometry and lateen sails, and passed China’s paper and gunpowder to Europe; the flow reversed only after about 1500. China until about 1450 out-invented everyone: canal lock gates, cast iron, deep drilling, efficient harnesses, gunpowder, kites, the magnetic compass, movable type, paper, porcelain, printing, the sternpost rudder, the wheelbarrow. Western Europe was the least advanced “civilised” Old World region until the late Middle Ages.
  • With so many independent factors, innovativeness is “essentially a random variable”. Over a whole continent, some share of societies will always be innovative. What matters is how many societies there are, and how easily ideas move among them.

Diffusion, loss and self-acceleration

  • Easy inventions, suggested by common materials, arose many times: plant domestication at least nine times; pottery in Japan about 14,000 years ago, the Fertile Crescent and China by about 10,000, then Amazonia, the Sahel, the US Southeast and Mexico. Hard ones (writing, the alphabet, the water wheel, rotary quern, gearing, compass, windmill, camera obscura) arose once or twice in the Old World and never in the New, and spread by borrowing. The wheel appears around 3400 BC near the Black Sea and within centuries across Eurasia, always in the same odd three-plank design; Mexico’s one-piece wheels were a separate invention. Metallurgy spread from the Andes via Panama to Mesoamerica.
  • Inventions spread by adoption or by conquest: after the Ngapuhi got muskets around 1818, the Musket Wars forced every surviving Maori tribe to acquire them by 1833. Transfer came by trade (transistors to Japan, 1954), espionage (silkworms smuggled to the Middle East, 552), emigration (200,000 Huguenots expelled from France in 1685 spread glass and clothing crafts) and war (Chinese papermakers captured at the Talas River in 751 started paper-making in Samarkand). Porcelain, invented in China around the seventh century and admired in Europe from the fourteenth, was reinvented by idea diffusion only in 1707 by Johann Böttger at Meissen.
  • Isolated societies can lose technology. Japan got guns from two Portuguese on a Chinese ship in 1543 and by 1600 had more and better guns than any country; but guns threatened the samurai’s graceful single combat and grew despised as foreign, so the government restricted, licensed and finally stopped ordering them, until Commodore Perry’s cannon-bristling fleet arrived in 1853. No European country could have done that without being overrun. China gave up ocean-going ships, mechanical clocks and water-driven spinning machines. Tasmanians abandoned bone tools and fishing; Torres Islanders gave up canoes, and Gaua Islanders gave them up and took them back; Polynesians abandoned pottery; Polar Eskimos lost the bow and the kayak; Dorset Eskimos the bow, bow drill and dogs.
  • Technology is autocatalytic. Advances need simpler ones first: iron came only after thousands of years of cold-hammered copper and gold, pottery kilns, and bronze, about 2,000 years of it in both the Fertile Crescent and China, while the New World had only just begun bronze when Europeans came. And new materials recombine (see the figure): Gutenberg’s press (1455) combined six technologies the Phaistos printer lacked, and a mass market to finance it.
  • Two great jumps stand out: the Great Leap Forward, 100,000 to 50,000 years ago, probably genetic; and settled life, from about 13,000 years ago. Nomads can own only what they carry. Fired clay figurines from Czechoslovakia 27,000 years ago, and evidence of weaving there, came long before the first vessels (Japan, 14,000 years ago), baskets (13,000) and woven cloth (9,000); neither craft took off until people settled.

Why Eurasia

  • Three factors (an early start in food production, few barriers to diffusion, and large populations with many competing societies) explain the continents’ technological differences, magnified by autocatalysis. Eurasia was largest, had the two earliest farming centres, an east–west axis and no barrier like Panama’s 40-mile isthmus, the Darien forest or the northern Mexican desert. In the Americas, Mesoamerica’s wheels and the Andes’ llamas, both in place by 3000 BC, never met in 5,000 years, across 1,200 miles, while France and China, 6,000 miles apart, shared wheels and horses.
  • Africa below the Sahara, cut off by desert and its own north–south axis, got pottery and iron in the Sahel as early as western Europe, but pottery reached the southern tip only around AD 1 and metallurgy not at all before it came by European ship. Australia, the smallest, driest and most isolated continent, with no native food production, remained the only continent without metal.
  • “The New Guineans whom I know include potential Edisons,” but they applied their ingenuity to surviving in the jungle without imports, not to inventing phonographs.

Chapter 14

From Egalitarianism to Kleptocracy

Societies grow from bands to tribes to chiefdoms to states as populations grow. Large, dense populations cannot run on kinship and village meetings: they need centralised power, which lets elites take a cut (kleptocracy), justified by religion. Societies merge into bigger ones only under threat or by conquest.

The Fayu

  • In 1979 Diamond flew with missionaries over a swampy New Guinea basin where Indonesian crocodile hunters had met and shot some uncontacted nomads, probably the Fayu. His friend Doug flew in by helicopter during one of the Fayu’s once- or twice-yearly gatherings to exchange brides. For people who normally lived as scattered families, a few dozen together was frightening: a son spotted his father’s killer and rushed him with an axe; both were wrestled down, screaming. The tension lasted days.
  • The Fayu, about 400 hunter-gatherers in four clans over a few hundred square miles, said they had once numbered about 2,000, reduced by killing each other; they had no mechanisms to settle serious disputes peacefully. A missionary couple has since lived among them for a dozen years and persuaded them to renounce violence. After missionaries come teachers, doctors, bureaucrats and soldiers; government and religion have spread together throughout history, the one organising conquest and the other justifying it.
  • At the end of the Ice Age most people lived like the Fayu. In 1500 less than 20 per cent of the world’s land was divided into states; today all but Antarctica is. Government and religion are the fourth set of proximate agents, alongside germs, writing and technology.

Four kinds of society

  • Any classification of a continuum is arbitrary (is a 19-year-old an adolescent? Brahms and Liszt would hate sharing a label), but four categories help (see the figure).
  • Bands (5 to 80 people, mostly relatives) were the organisation we inherited, shared with gorillas and chimpanzees; probably everyone lived in bands until 40,000 years ago and most did 11,000 years ago. Recent bands include many African Pygmies, the San, Aboriginal Australians, the Inuit, and peoples of Tierra del Fuego and the northern forests. They share land, have no specialists but by age and sex, and no laws or police. “Egalitarian” means only that leadership is informal. In New Guinea’s Lakes Plains, bands live on sago, moving on when they have cut the mature palms; malaria, the lack of even stone (obtained by trade) and scarce food keep numbers low.
  • Tribes (hundreds, usually settled, from about 13,000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent) have several clans that exchange marriage partners and own land, and everyone still knows everyone. The 12,000 Fore of 1964, in 65 villages, had no political unity: each hamlet fought and allied with its neighbours. Kin ties do the work of police; New Guineans meeting away from home would discuss relatives at length to find a reason not to kill each other. A big-man has influence but no office, secrets or inheritance, and looks like everyone else. There are no full-time specialists: Faletau, the Solomons’ most famous wood carver, still had to dig his own sweet potatoes. And so no slaves.
  • Chiefdoms (thousands to tens of thousands; from about 5500 BC in the Fertile Crescent and 1000 BC in Mesoamerica and the Andes; gone as independent units by the early twentieth century because they held land states wanted) forced people “for the first time in history” to meet strangers regularly without killing them. The chief holds a hereditary office, a monopoly of force and of information, and is recognisable from afar (a big fan worn on the back on Rennell Island; commoners prostrate themselves in Hawaii). Generalist bureaucrats like Hawaii’s konohiki collect tribute, oversee irrigation and organise labour. Rich foragers could manage chiefdoms too: the Kwakiutl, Nootka and Tlingit lived on salmon and halibut. Chiefs got luxury goods (feather cloaks taking generations to make), which archaeologists spot in rich graves, public buildings and a hierarchy of settlements. The new economic system was redistribution: produce collected by the chief and given back at feasts or over the year. When most of it stayed with the chief’s lineage and craftsmen, it became tribute, the ancestor of taxes.

Kleptocrat or statesman?

  • Chiefdoms introduced the dilemma of every unequal society: they provide services individuals could not buy, and they transfer wealth upward. The difference between a kleptocrat and a statesman is “merely one of degree”: President Mobutu of Zaire kept billions and left no working telephone system; George Washington spent taxes on admired programmes and did not enrich himself, though he was born into wealth far more unequally distributed than in any New Guinea village. Hawaiian history was punctuated by revolts against oppressive chiefs, usually led by younger brothers promising less oppression.
  • Kleptocrats win support in four ways: disarm the people and arm the elite (easier with industrial weapons than with home-made spears); redistribute much of the tribute popularly; keep order (see below); and build an ideology or religion that justifies them, with chiefs claiming divinity or a hotline to the gods, and tribute spent on temples.
  • Order is a real and underrated benefit. Anthropologists who saw no murder in three years in a band of 25 concluded bands were gentle; but such a band could not survive a murder every three years. Iyau women in New Guinea, asked to name their husbands, listed several killed in turn: “My first husband was killed by Elopi raiders. My second husband was killed by a man who wanted me, and who became my third husband. That husband was killed by the brother of my second husband, seeking to avenge his murder.”
  • Institutionalised religion also bonds unrelated people and gives them a motive, beyond kinship, to die for the group, which makes it far better at conquest.

States

  • States (temples of standard design, at least four levels of settlement, pottery styles over tens of thousands of square miles) arose around 3700 BC in Mesopotamia, around 300 BC in Mesoamerica, over 2,000 years ago in the Andes, China and South-East Asia, and over 1,000 years ago in West Africa. Most modern states exceed a million people; China exceeds a billion. They have cities, kings, and more concentrated information: in the Cuban Missile Crisis (which the book dates to 1963; it was 1962) Kennedy confined deliberations to a ten-member committee and final decisions to four men.
  • Specialisation makes collapse catastrophic, as when Roman troops, officials and coins left Britain between AD 407 and 411. Early Mesopotamian states ran the economy through four specialist groups (cereal farmers, herders, fishermen, orchard and garden growers), supplying each and taking its produce, trading wool for metals, and feeding irrigation workers. States used slaves on a larger scale because they had more uses and more captives. Bureaucracy multiplies vertically and horizontally: the small West African state of Maradi had over 130 titled offices. Laws, courts and police formalise conflict; writing arose with the first states. Early kings were often divine: the Aztec and Inca emperors rode in litters, and Japanese has special forms of “you” for the emperor.
  • States break from chiefdoms in being organised by territory, not kinship; in being multi-ethnic; and in recruiting bureaucrats partly for skill. Over 13,000 years the trend has been toward larger units, “1,000 amalgamations for 999 reversals” (the USSR, Yugoslavia and Czechoslovakia split, as did Alexander’s empire; Rome and China fell to “barbarian” and Mongol chiefdoms).
  • States win because of weapons and numbers, centralised command, and willingness to die: “For King and Country”, “Por Dios y España”, and the Aztec yearning for “the flowery death”. Diamond’s New Guinea friends’ accounts of tribal wars contain no hint of patriotism or suicidal charges; raids minimise risk. Fanaticism in war, of the kind that drove Christian and Islamic conquests, was probably unknown before chiefdoms and states arose within the last 6,000 years.

Why states form

  • States arose “pristinely” on every continent except Australia and North America, and Europeans watched many form from chiefdoms in the last three centuries. Aristotle thought states natural (all the societies he knew were states); Rousseau imagined a social contract, but no state has ever formed that way. The “hydraulic theory” (states formed to build big irrigation systems) fails too: in Mesopotamia, North China, Mexico and Madagascar small irrigation preceded states and big irrigation came later; Maya and Andean irrigation stayed small.
  • The best single predictor of complexity is regional population. Intensive food production and complexity drive each other: food production supplies seasonal labour after harvest (for pyramids, fishponds or conquest), stored surpluses for specialists, and settled life; centralised societies in turn organise public works, long-distance trade and specialist exchange. So missionaries and governments contacting nomads always aim to pacify them and settle them in villages.
  • Large populations need centralisation for four reasons (see the figure): conflict among strangers, decision-making, redistribution, and density. And centralised power inevitably lets its holders reward themselves.

Merger by threat or by conquest

  • Competition selects better-governed societies: tribes that settle conflict as badly as the Fayu fall back into bands; well-run ones concentrate force and absorb neighbours. But small units never merge freely. They merge under outside threat or are conquered (see the figure).
  • Threat: the Cherokees’ 30 or 40 village chiefdoms of about 400 people each, facing indiscriminate white retaliation, chose an overall chief, Moytoy, in 1730 (succeeded by his son in 1741) and around 1758 an annual council at Echota, a de facto capital; later a written constitution. The American colonies, as jealous as the Cherokees, found the Articles of Confederation (1781) unworkable and adopted the Constitution in 1787 only after Shays’s Rebellion (1786) and war debts. Germany failed to unite in 1848, 1850 and 1866, and did so in 1871 after France declared war in 1870.
  • Conquest: around 1807 Dingiswayo seized the Mtetwa Zulu chiefdom, drafted young men from all villages into age regiments, spared conquered chiefs’ families while installing cooperative relatives, and extended adjudication; he absorbed 30 other chiefdoms. Europeans also watched states form in Hawaii, Tahiti, Madagascar (Merina), Lesotho, Swaziland, Ashanti, Ankole and Buganda.
  • War has always existed; why did it start building states only in the last 13,000 years? Because the fate of the defeated depends on density. Where it is low, losers move away. Where moderate, with no room and no use for captives, men are killed and women taken. Where high, losers can be enslaved or left in place to pay tribute: the Aztecs received each year 7,000 tons of corn, 4,000 of beans, 4,000 of amaranth, 2,000,000 cotton cloaks, and cacao, costumes, shields, headdresses and amber, lists the conquistadors studied eagerly.
  • So the four proximate agents usually came together, but not always: the Incas had an empire without writing, the Aztecs writing without epidemic diseases. Dingiswayo’s Mtetwa had no edge in technology, writing or germs over other Zulu chiefdoms; their advantage was government and ideology alone, and it let the Zulu state conquer part of a continent for nearly a century.

Part Four

Around the World in Five Chapters

The framework tested region by region: Australia and New Guinea, China, the Austronesian Pacific, the Americas and Africa.

Chapter 15

Yali’s People

Australia and New Guinea were one continent until about 10,000 years ago. New Guinea, wet and fertile, invented farming but was held back by poor crops, no livestock, rugged terrain and a population of only a million. Australia, the driest, flattest and least predictable continent, stayed with foraging. Europeans did not build modern Australia; they imported it.

A test case

  • Hiking at noon to Aboriginal rock paintings near Menindee, Diamond and his wife Marie ran out of water, lost interest in art, and he began seeing an impossibly huge babbler: heat hallucinations. Menindee was also the base of Robert Burke and William Wills, who set out with six camels and three months’ food on the first European crossing of Australia from south to north. Aborigines rescued them three times with fish, fern cakes and roasted fat rats; then Burke fired his pistol at one, the Aborigines fled, and the explorers, with guns to hunt, starved within a month.
  • Australia is by far the driest, smallest, flattest, most infertile, most unpredictable and biologically poorest continent, and it had the least numerous and most distinctive societies: no farming, herding, metal, bows, substantial buildings, villages, writing, chiefdoms or states. An early French explorer called Aborigines “the most miserable people of the world”. Yet 40,000 years ago they had a head start: some of the earliest ground-edged and hafted stone tools, by far the earliest boats, and some of the oldest rock paintings.
  • A puzzle within the puzzle: New Guinea, a tenth of Australia’s size, had farmers and swineherds in villages, bows and arrows, often pottery, better houses and boats, and a native population several times Australia’s. Why? And why hadn’t New Guinea “progressed” further?
  • Many white Australians answer: the Aborigines themselves. Same continent, different people, and whites built a literate industrial democracy within decades, on a continent rich in iron, aluminium, copper, tin, lead and zinc. The logic seems compelling, but “contains a simple error”.

Two hemi-continents

  • Colonists from South-East Asia crossed at least eight channels up to 50 miles wide (perhaps on bamboo rafts) by 40,000 years ago and quickly spread: the south-west by 40,000 years ago, the south-east and Tasmania by 35,000, the New Guinea highlands by 30,000, the Bismarcks and Solomons by 35,000. Then no firm evidence of new arrivals from Asia until pigs in New Guinea and dogs in Australia within the last few thousand years. Australian languages and New Guinea’s Papuan languages show no clear link to Asian ones or to each other, and their original Asian stock has largely been replaced by later expansions out of China.
  • Since the Arafura Sea flooded around 10,000 years ago, the two have diverged: blood groups B and S are found in New Guinea but virtually absent in Australia; New Guineans mostly have tightly coiled hair, Australians straight or wavy. Their environments differ in almost every way (see the figure).

New Guinea: farming in the sky

  • The earliest and densest farming arose in the highland valleys between 4,000 and 9,000 feet: drainage ditches from 9,000 years ago, extensive by 6,000, widespread deforestation by 5,000. Sugarcane, leafy vegetables, edible grass stems, New Guinea’s own bananas, and taro and some yams are local; no highland crop is unequivocally Asian. The three clear imports were chickens and pigs, brought by Austronesians around 3,600 years ago (pigs perhaps earlier), and the sweet potato, which came within the last few centuries via Spanish Philippines and overtook taro. Europeans flying over in the 1930s saw landscapes “similar to Holland’s”: deforested valleys, villages, drained and fenced fields.
  • Below the highlands, coastal villagers live on fish, inland people on slash-and-burn bananas and yams, and swamp dwellers stay nomadic on wild sago, which yields three times more calories per hour than gardening: a place where foraging simply beats farming.
  • New Guinea’s strikes: low-protein root crops and too few pigs and chickens to matter, no draught animals and no crowd diseases; only a few broad valleys (the Wahgi and Baliem) and one farmable altitude band, so no trade between altitude zones as in the Andes, Alps or Himalayas; so a population that never exceeded a million before colonial medicine and peace, the smallest of any farming homeland. Terrain fragmented it: three miles a day is good progress even on trails; most highlanders never went more than 10 miles from home; 1,000 of the world’s 6,000 languages are packed into an area slightly larger than Texas, nearly half with under 500 speakers. And isolation: its neighbours were Aborigines, small islands, and eastern Indonesia, itself a backwater until the Austronesians came around 1600 BC with pottery, chickens and probably dogs and pigs. For at least a thousand years New Guinea traded bird-of-paradise plumes and spices for Dong Son bronze drums and Chinese porcelain, but the Portuguese reached the Moluccas in 1511 before metal could spread.

Australia: why no farming

  • Its big marsupials (diprotodonts, giant kangaroos, giant wombats) vanished with the first humans. The only domestic animal adopted was the dog, arriving from Asia around 1500 BC (probably in Austronesian canoes) and going wild as the dingo; Aborigines kept dingoes as companions, watchdogs and living blankets (a “five-dog night” is a very cold one), but did not eat them or hunt with them.
  • The land is the driest and least fertile, and its climate is governed by ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation) rather than regular seasons: years of drought, then floods. Farmers who multiplied in good years would starve in bad ones; even modern Australian farming is risky. Wild plants were poor: modern breeders have domesticated only the macadamia, and Australia’s two entries among the 56 prize grasses have grains of 13 milligrams against 40 for the best.
  • So nomadism, many wild foods and few possessions were sensible, keeping populations small enough to eat well in good years and enough in bad. Aborigines practised “firestick farming”, burning the land to flush game, open thickets, create kangaroo parkland and grow fresh grass and fern roots. Most were not desert people: they were densest in the Murray-Darling, the east and north coasts and the south-west, the same places Europeans wanted, which is why the last intact groups survived only in deserts.
  • In the last 5,000 years the productive regions intensified: leaching or fermenting poisonous cycad seeds; summer feasts in the south-eastern highlands on hibernating bogong moths, which grilled taste like roasted chestnuts; eel farms in the Murray-Darling marshes, with canals up to a mile and a half long, weirs and traps at different water levels, and villages of up to 146 stone houses; and wild millet (the genus of Chinese broomcorn millet) reaped with stone knives, stacked, threshed and ground, with tools like those invented independently in the Fertile Crescent. Small stone blades, ground-edge hatchets and shell fishhooks spread.

Small, isolated populations lose technology

  • A few hundred thousand foragers meant few inventors, and they lived on ecological islands in a sea of desert; Queensland’s rain forests are 1,900 miles from Victoria’s, as far as Los Angeles from Alaska. Mainland Australia shows losses: the boomerang abandoned on Cape York, south-western Aborigines not eating shellfish, and small stone points of 5,000 years ago that look like arrowheads, perhaps a sign that bows were adopted and later dropped.
  • Tasmania is the extreme. Cut off by the flooding of Bass Strait around 10,000 years ago, its 4,000 people had no contact with anyone for 10,000 years. When Europeans arrived in 1642 they lacked barbed spears, all bone tools, boomerangs, ground or hafted stone tools, hooks, nets, pronged spears, traps, fishing, sewing and even a way to start a fire. Fishing and bone awls and needles disappear from the record around 1500 BC. On Flinders, Kangaroo and King Islands, populations of perhaps 200 to 400 died out altogether (see the figure).

Why New Guinea’s and Indonesia’s ways didn’t cross

  • From Indonesia nothing new reached Australia between the first settlement and the dingo. Austronesians settled Timor and Tanimbar, 275 and 205 miles away, and must have landed repeatedly. Macassan sailing canoes from Sulawesi visited the north-west every year from about AD 1000 until the government stopped them in 1907, to collect sea cucumbers (trepang) for Chinese soups and aphrodisiacs. They planted tamarinds, fathered children, traded cloth, metal, pottery and glass, and left loan words, ceremonies, dugout sailing canoes and tobacco pipes. But the facing coast was too dry for them to settle, so Aborigines saw only a fragment of Macassan life: “Dugout sailing canoes and pipes, yes; forges and pigs, no.”
  • Torres Strait is only 90 miles wide, and its largest island, Muralug, lies 10 miles from Australia. But no Cape York Aborigine ever saw a mainland New Guinean. Goods passed along a chain like a children’s game of telephone (see the figure), New Guinea life thinning island by island to Muralug, dry and living on seafood, wild yams and mangrove fruit, and trade alternating with head-hunting raids. Coiled hair, a few sounds in four Cape York languages, shell fishhooks (which spread far), outrigger canoes, drums, masks, funeral posts and pipes got through; farming, pigs and bows did not. New Guinea’s highland farmers were 2,000 miles from Australia’s cool south-eastern highlands: “the mountains of the moon.”

Europeans arrive

  • New Guinea was “discovered” by a Portuguese navigator in 1526; Holland claimed the west in 1828; Britain and Germany split the east in 1884; Europeans controlled most New Guineans by 1960. They had the ships, compasses, writing, institutions and guns. But few settled. Lowland malaria defeated every settlement attempt until the 1880s: the Marquis de Rays’s colony on New Ireland around 1880 lost 930 of 1,000 colonists in three years (Diamond himself has had a year of malaria and a year of dysentery). New Guineans had long exposure to Eurasian germs through 3,500 years of Indonesian traders and settlers, and permanent European settlement came only in the 1880s, after public health had begun controlling smallpox. The malaria-free highlands were reached only in the 1930s, when colonial governments no longer cleared land by killing natives, and European crops and livestock do badly there. So Papua New Guinea is governed by New Guineans (in English, with the alphabet, democracy and imported guns), while Indonesia, which took the west from Holland in 1963 as Irian Jaya, is filling its towns with Indonesians, who share New Guinea’s diseases and crops: “Indonesians are modern Austronesians.”
  • Australia was different: its fertile areas suited wheat (its leading crop), barley, oats, apples and grapes, Sahel sorghum and cotton, Andean potatoes, and in Queensland New Guinea sugarcane, Asian bananas and citrus and South American peanuts; sheep opened the dry lands. It had no disease barrier except in the tropical north. So Europeans shot Aborigines (the last big massacre, of 31 people, was at Alice Springs in 1928) and infected them: within a year of Sydney’s founding in 1788, corpses from epidemics were a common sight. The Aboriginal population fell by 80 per cent, from around 300,000 to 60,000 in 1921; independent societies survived only in lands Europeans did not want. Australia now has 20 million non-Aborigines, with more Asians since the White Australia policy ended in 1973.
  • So the “controlled experiment” is false. “White English colonists did not create a literate, food-producing, industrial democracy in Australia.” They imported livestock, every crop but macadamias, metallurgy, steam engines, guns, the alphabet, political institutions and even germs, the products of 10,000 years in Eurasia. Burke and Wills could write, but could not survive where Aborigines lived. “The people who did create a society in Australia were Aboriginal Australians.”

Chapter 16

How China Became Chinese

China looks uniform because it was unified so early. It was once as diverse as anywhere, but its early food production, bronze, writing and states spread from the north and overwhelmed the south, and South Chinese farmers then repopulated tropical South-East Asia.

The exception among giants

  • Diamond’s sons’ Los Angeles classmates speak over 80 home languages. That is normal: of the world’s six most populous nations, all but one are recent melting pots. Russia began expanding beyond the Urals only in 1582; India, Indonesia and Brazil are recent creations with about 850, 670 and 210 languages. The exception is China: unified in 221 BC and mostly since, with a single writing system from the start, and with over 800 million of its 1.2 billion people speaking Mandarin, and some 300 million speaking seven languages as close to it as Spanish is to Italian.
  • That unity is astonishing. North and South Chinese differ genetically and physically (northerners resemble Tibetans and Nepalese; southerners Vietnamese and Filipinos) and live in different climates. New Guinea, with a tenth of China’s area and 40,000 years of history, has a thousand languages; western Europe acquired about 40 in the 6,000–8,000 years since Indo-European arrived. Humans have lived in China for over half a million years. Where did its languages go?

Reading the language map

  • Besides its eight “big” languages, China has over 130 “little” ones, falling into four families (see the figure). Only the Chinese branch of Sino-Tibetan is continuous: you could walk from Manchuria to the Gulf of Tonkin among its speakers. The others survive in islands.
  • Three rules turn back the clock: reverse known expansions (Thai, Lao and Burmese speakers moved south from South China within historical times; the Zhou dynasty, 1100–221 BC, records Chinese-speaking states absorbing non-Chinese peoples); a single language over a large area means a recent spread; high diversity marks an old homeland. So North China was Sino-Tibetan; South China was variously Miao-Yao, Austroasiatic and Tai-Kadai, perhaps with other lost families such as Austronesian, which survives only because it went to sea; and tropical South-East Asia’s original languages are entirely extinct.
  • Languages spread like English in America: not because they sound musical, but because invaders killed, infected and outnumbered the speakers of others, thanks ultimately to earlier food production.

China’s head start

  • Crops, domestic animal bones, pottery and polished stone tools appear by around 7500 BC, within a thousand years of the Fertile Crescent. There may have been two centres: drought-resistant millets in the cool, dry north and rice in the warm, wet south, with pigs, dogs and chickens at the earliest sites. Later came water buffalo (for ploughing), silkworms, ducks and geese; soybeans, hemp, citrus, tea, apricots, peaches and pears; and from the west, along Eurasia’s axis, wheat, barley, cattle, horses, sheep and goats.
  • Then the other hallmarks (see the figure): bronze from the third millennium BC, the world’s earliest cast iron around 500 BC, and 1,500 years of inventions from paper to gunpowder; fortified towns and graves ranging from bare to lavish; huge walls, palaces and eventually the Grand Canal, over 1,000 miles long; writing preserved from the second millennium BC. Plague and possibly smallpox reached Europe from the east, and influenza, from pigs, may well have arisen in China.
  • Geography helped unify it. The north–south gradient was a milder barrier than in Africa or the Americas, with no desert or isthmus; the long east–west Yellow and Yangtze Rivers carried crops and ideas between coast and interior; and gentle terrain let canals join the two. Western Europe, similar in area but rugged and without unifying rivers, has resisted unification to this day.
  • Iron smelting and rice spread north, but most things spread south: bronze, Sino-Tibetan languages, state formation, and writing. Unlike western Eurasia, with its cuneiform, hieroglyphs, Hittite, Minoan and alphabets, China had one well-attested script, perfected in the north, which preempted all others. The Xia, Shang and Zhou dynasties all arose in the north.
  • Chinese regarded outsiders, and northerners even southerners, as barbarians. A late Zhou writer described the eastern Yi who “had their hair unbound, and tattooed their bodies. Some of them ate their food without its being cooked by fire”, and other tribes who turned their feet inward, lived in caves and ate no cereals. Zhou-style states spread south, culminating in the Qin unification of 221 BC; the first Qin emperor ordered earlier histories burned. Such measures helped push Sino-Tibetan over most of China.

The steamroller moves south

  • Until the fourth millennium BC tropical South-East Asia belonged to hunter-gatherers making pebble and flake tools of the Hoabinhian tradition (after Hoa Binh in Vietnam). Then Chinese-derived crops, Neolithic tools, villages and South Chinese-style pottery arrived, probably with South China’s language families; later Burmese, Lao and Thai migrations completed the “Sinification”. Modern South-East Asians are recent offshoots of South Chinese.
  • Only three relict forager groups hint at the earlier people, perhaps dark-skinned and curly-haired like New Guineans: the Semang Negritos of the Malay Peninsula (who adopted an Austroasiatic language from farmer neighbours), the Andaman Islanders (whose languages are the last survivors of hundreds of extinct ones) and the Veddoid Negritos of Sri Lanka. They may be the last of the stock that colonised New Guinea.
  • Korea and Japan, protected by distance, kept their languages and genes but took rice, bronze and writing, and West Asian wheat and barley, via China. Diamond warns against exaggeration: Japan made some of the world’s oldest pottery and had settled foragers living on seafood long before farming, and some crops may have been domesticated first in Japan, Korea or South-East Asia.
  • But China’s role was disproportionate. Japan keeps its awkward Chinese-derived script out of prestige, and Korea is only now replacing its Chinese characters with han’gul: “a vivid 20th-century legacy of plant and animal domestication in China nearly 10,000 years ago.”

Chapter 17

Speedboat to Polynesia

Farmers from South China, via Taiwan, spread across half the globe from Madagascar to Easter Island. They swept away Indonesia’s hunter-gatherers but made little headway against New Guinea’s farmers: food production, not seamanship alone, decided who replaced whom.

Four friends in a shop

  • In Jayapura, capital of Indonesian New Guinea, Diamond went shopping with Achmad, a Javan government officer; Wiwor, a New Guinea highlander; and Sauakari, a north-coast lowlander; the shop belonged to Ping Wah, a Chinese merchant, who hid his Chinese newspaper when he saw Achmad (Chinese writing was nominally illegal there). Achmad held an axe upside down; no shoe fitted Wiwor’s splayed feet; Achmad’s comb snapped in Wiwor’s coiled hair. Everyone laughed, but the tensions were real: Javans dominate Indonesia’s government, which annexed western New Guinea in the 1960s and crushed resistance; in 1966 Javans slaughtered hundreds of thousands of Chinese; highlanders despise lowlanders as “effete sago eaters”, lowlanders call highlanders “primitive big-heads”; within days Wiwor and Sauakari nearly fought with axes. Achmad stayed in town, pointing to his straight hair.
  • They embody three prehistoric waves from Asia: Wiwor’s ancestors reached New Guinea by 40,000 years ago; Achmad’s reached Java from the South China coast about 4,000 years ago; Sauakari’s reached New Guinea about 3,600 years ago in the same wave; Ping Wah’s stayed in China. That wave, the Austronesian expansion, was among the biggest population movements of the last 6,000 years.

The linguistic trail to Taiwan

  • The Philippines and western Indonesia are strikingly uniform: people resembling South Chinese and Malay Peninsula peoples, and 374 languages all in one sub-subfamily (Western Malayo-Polynesian). Yet humans have lived in Indonesia for a million years; one would expect diversity and dark tropical skins, or people like New Guineans. Only the Philippine Negritos hint at the earlier population, and even they speak Austronesian languages. So the spread was recent, and the many languages just reflect islands never unified politically. English has borrowed “taboo” and “tattoo” (Polynesian), “boondocks” (Tagalog), and “amok”, “batik” and “orangutan” (Malay).
  • The Austronesian family has 959 languages in four subfamilies, and one, Malayo-Polynesian, holds 945 of them across almost its entire range: before European expansion, the most widespread language family on Earth (see the figure). The other three subfamilies, far more different from each other, are confined to the aborigines of Taiwan, 90 miles off South China, who now make up only 2 per cent of its people after Chinese settlement over the last thousand years and after 1949. The deepest divisions mark the homeland: Taiwan.

The archaeological trail

  • From around the fourth millennium BC, polished stone tools and decorated Ta-p’en-k’eng pottery, derived from South China pottery, appear on Taiwan and the facing coast, with fish bones, shellfish, net sinkers and canoe-making adzes; later sites have rice and millet. Taiwan Strait was perhaps the training ground for open-sea sailing. Stone bark beaters link it to the Pacific, where people beyond wool and fibre crops wore bark cloth; Rennell Islanders told Diamond that Westernisation’s great blessing was silence, no more pounding from dawn to dusk.
  • Then the package (plain or red pottery, ground stone tools, pigs, crops) spread (see the figure). The double-outrigger sailing canoe, with a log on each side that makes capsizing virtually impossible, may have been the breakthrough; a round-bottomed dugout, which terrified Diamond on New Guinea rivers, cannot handle modest waves.
  • Linguistics and archaeology agree twice. The route: the deepest language splits are in Taiwan, and the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra share a recent branch, so the expansion went Taiwan → Philippines → Indonesia → Malaya, not the reverse. And the baggage: comparing descendant languages reconstructs lost “proto-languages”. Proto-Indo-European had owis “sheep” (Lithuanian avis, Latin ovis, English “ewe”) and some 2,000 other reconstructable words, including goat, horse and wheel, but no shared word for gun. Proto-Austronesian had words for pig, dog and rice, and for outrigger canoe, sail, giant clam, octopus, fish trap and sea turtle; Proto-Malayo-Polynesian added taro, breadfruit, bananas, yams and coconuts, as farmers moved from Taiwan (about 23° north) into the tropics.
  • Austronesians replaced Philippine and Indonesian foragers for the familiar reasons: denser populations, better tools and weapons, better boats, and germs. They took parts of the Malay Peninsula (arriving from Sumatra and Borneo as Austroasiatic farmers came from Thailand) and parts of Vietnam and Cambodia (the Chamic minority), but could go no further against established Austroasiatic and Tai-Kadai farmers. No Austronesian language survives in mainland China, perhaps erased by Sino-Tibetan expansion, though its closest relatives are thought to be Tai-Kadai, Austroasiatic and Miao-Yao.

New Guinea holds out

  • By 1500 BC Austronesians were on Halmahera, under 200 miles from New Guinea. But New Guinea highlanders like Wiwor are dark-skinned and coiled-haired, and geneticists find no Austronesian markers in them. On the north and east coasts and in the Bismarcks and Solomons, people like Sauakari are intermediate but much closer to Wiwor: about 15 per cent Austronesian. All languages of the interior and south are Papuan; Austronesian ones (the Oceanic branch, related to those of Halmahera and western New Guinea) occupy only a narrow strip of the north and south-east coasts and most of the Bismarcks and Solomons. Along the north coast, villages alternate between Austronesian and Papuan languages with no genetic break, and each family has absorbed the other’s words and grammar: thousands of years of trade and intermarriage spread Austronesian languages more than genes.
  • Around 1600 BC the Austronesian hallmarks (pigs, chickens, dogs, red-slipped pottery, adzes of ground stone and giant clamshell) appear in the region, with two differences. The pottery was “tattooed” with geometric bands: Lapita ware, named after a site. And Lapita sites cling to small islets off larger islands (only one, Aitape, on New Guinea’s own coast, a couple in the Solomons). They ate fish, porpoises, turtles, sharks and shellfish, pigs, chickens and dogs, coconuts and other nuts.
  • In 1972 Diamond landed on Malai Islet in the Siassi group and found “the New Guinea equivalent of downtown Manhattan”: two-storey houses packed together, no gardens, rows of big canoes, the islanders living as specialist potters, carvers and traders, even buying canoe timber from nearby Umboi. Such groups monopolised inter-island trade until European motor vessels, aluminium pots and a colonial ban on long voyages ended it. The Lapita potters, he guesses, were such traders. Austronesians reached New Guinea’s south-east peninsula only around AD 1 and never colonised the interior or the south coast, though they held the Aru and Kei Islands 70–80 miles away.
  • Why did Indonesia fall and New Guinea not, if the original peoples were related? Because Indonesia held sparse foragers without even polished stone tools, while New Guinea had farmed for thousands of years (see the figure). “The variable outcomes of the Austronesian expansion strikingly illustrate the role of food production in human population movements.” Papuan languages on northern Halmahera and Timor suggest New Guineans even pushed back west.

Into the empty Pacific

  • Beyond the Solomons, the Lapita potters sailed into islands no human had reached. Their descendants, the Polynesians, “the greatest seafarers among Neolithic peoples”, speak languages close to those of the New Guinea region and grow taro, yams, bananas, coconuts and breadfruit. Eastern Polynesians lost pottery along the way.
  • One wave, many outcomes. The Yumbri of the Thai–Lao mountain forests reverted to foraging while their close linguistic relatives, the Vietnamese, built a metal-based empire in the Red River delta. Borneo’s Punan returned to foraging while their Austronesian relatives on Java’s volcanic soils built an Indian-influenced kingdom, adopted writing and raised Borobudur. Polynesians, cut off from metal and writing, diversified as Chapter 2 showed.
  • Europeans dominated the region for a time but could not settle most of it, because of indigenous germs and established farmers. Only New Zealand, New Caledonia and Hawaii, the largest, most remote and most temperate islands, have large European populations. Unlike Australia and the Americas, East Asia and the Pacific remain in the hands of their own peoples.

Chapter 18

Hemispheres Colliding

By 1492 Eurasia led the Americas in food production, germs, technology, political organisation and writing, because of a longer head start, far better wild plants and animals, and fewer barriers to diffusion. The Norse failed in the Americas; the Spanish, from the right place at the right time, succeeded.

The snapshot of 1492

  • Animals. Eurasia had 13 big domestic mammals supplying meat, milk, wool, hides, transport, war mounts, ploughing, manure and “industrial” power until watermills and windmills. The Americas had only the llama/alpaca, confined to the Andes and Peruvian coast: never milked, ridden, harnessed or taken to war. Had the late-Pleistocene extinctions not happened, Cortés might have been driven into the sea by Aztec cavalry on American horses, and killed by American germs.
  • Crops. Farming was widespread in both, but foragers held more of the Americas, including what are now its best farmlands: the Pacific states, Canada’s wheat belt, the pampas and Chile’s Mediterranean zone, which lacked only domesticates (they became productive for Native Americans too, as horsemen, herders and Navajo weavers, after 1492). Native American farming also had five handicaps: protein-poor corn; hand-planting single seeds instead of broadcast sowing; hand tilling instead of ploughing (no heavy sods); no manure; and no animal power for threshing, grinding or irrigation.
  • Germs. Eurasia had smallpox, measles, flu, plague, tuberculosis, typhus, cholera and malaria; the only crowd diseases certainly pre-Columbian in the Americas were non-syphilitic treponemas. Villages came thousands of years later there, and its three urban regions were never linked by heavy trade. Even malaria and yellow fever, which later blocked settlement of the American tropics and the Panama Canal, came from the Old World.
  • Technology. Five areas: metal (copper, bronze and iron tools everywhere complex in Eurasia; in the Americas mostly ornaments, with tools still of stone, wood and bone); weapons (steel swords, lances, guns, armour and horses against stone clubs, slings, bows and quilted armour); power (animal, water and wind power driving gears for grinding, sugar, bellows, ores, paper, oil, salt, textiles and sawing, a medieval industrial revolution); wheels (in carts, wheelbarrows, potter’s wheels and clocks; in the Americas only Mexican toys); and ships (ocean-going, wind-beating vessels with sextants, compasses, sternpost rudders and cannon; the Americas’ best were rafts sailing downwind along the Pacific coast, one of which Pizarro easily captured).
  • States and writing. Most of Eurasia was ruled by states and empires (Habsburg, Ottoman, Chinese, Mughal, Mongol) with official religions. The Americas had two empires, the Aztecs and Incas, against seven European states able to take American colonies between 1492 and 1666. Writing in the Americas was confined to a Mesoamerican elite; the Inca quipu knot-records could not carry detailed information.

Two trajectories

  • The book’s Table 18.1 (see the figure) reduces complex histories to arbitrary points on a continuum, which Diamond admits “is sure to horrify any knowledgeable scholar”; pottery in the Andes, for example, appears in coastal Ecuador by 3100 BC and Peru only by 1800 BC. American dates are the least certain: older claims from Coxcatlán Cave in Mexico and Guitarrero Cave in Peru are being revised by direct dating. But food production became the mainstay of diets about 5,000 years later in the American homelands than in Eurasia’s.
  • England shows how fast things spread once farmers are settled: 5,000 years for farming and villages to arrive from the Fertile Crescent, but only 2,000 more for bronze and just 250 years for iron.

Why the Americas lagged

  • A later start. Humans have been in Eurasia about a million years; they reached Alaska around 12,000 BC and South America’s tip by 10,000 BC, only 1,500 years before farming began in the Fertile Crescent. Filling the continents and learning their rocks, plants and animals would have taken only centuries (as Maori in New Zealand and the Tudawhe in New Guinea’s Karimui Basin show). But Fertile Crescent farmers inherited tens of thousands of years of local technology, like sickles and storage pits for wild cereals, while the first Americans arrived with gear for the Siberian tundra and had to invent everything anew.
  • Poorer domesticates. With virtually no domesticable mammals, early American farmers stayed part-time hunters for protein; and teosinte took perhaps thousands of years to become corn. So 1,500 to 2,000 years (or 5,000 on older dates) passed between the first crops (about 3000–2500 BC) and widespread year-round villages (1800–500 BC). New arrivals transformed societies: corn in the eastern United States and Amazonia, the llama in the northern Andes, the horse across both continents.
  • Barriers. A north–south axis; a continent pinched at Panama; the Darien rain forest, northern Mexican deserts, dry Texas, and the deserts and mountains fencing off the Pacific coast. So farming never reached California or Oregon; llamas, guinea pigs and potatoes never reached Mexico; sunflowers never reached Mesoamerica and turkeys never reached South America or the eastern United States; corn and beans took 3,000 and 4,000 years to cover 700 miles to the eastern farmlands, and then corn needed 700 more years to become productive there. Mesoamerican writing never reached the Andes, and Mesoamerican wheels never met Andean llamas. The Macedonian and Roman empires each spanned 3,000 miles and the Mongols 6,000, but Mesoamerica’s states apparently never heard of the chiefdoms 700 miles north or the Andean states 1,200 miles south.
  • Language families tell the same story. Eurasia’s languages fall into about a dozen families, many spread across vast areas by food producers or horse pastoralists (see the figure); Indo-European alone has about 144 languages. In the Americas, apart from Eskimo-Aleut and Na-Dene, linguists recognise only a hundred or more groups among 600 to 2,000 languages; Joseph Greenberg’s single “Amerind” family is a controversial minority view. Uto-Aztecan, Oto-Manguean, Natchez-Muskogean and Arawak may be legacies of smaller expansions.
  • Perhaps fragmentation. Why did the Andes never invent writing or wheels, and why did Mesoamerica keep wheels as toys when wheelbarrows would have helped? The Americas are 76 per cent of Eurasia’s area, but broken into poorly connected “islands” of societies, perhaps with some of the losses seen in Tasmania.

The Norse fail, the Spanish succeed

  • Before Columbus the only contacts were across the Bering Strait, where Alaskan Inuit settled a strip of Siberian coast. The Norse reached Iceland in 874 and Greenland in 986, and from Greenland visited North America’s north-east coast between about 1000 and 1350; their only known site, on Newfoundland (perhaps Vinland), was a winter camp of a few dozen people for a few years, attacked by “Skraelings”. Greenland’s Norse, never more than a few thousand, depended on Norway for food and iron and on Labrador for timber, while Inuit foragers there were self-sufficient. The Little Ice Age from the thirteenth century made things worse; the last contact was a storm-blown Icelandic ship in 1410, and by 1577 the colony had vanished.
  • The Norse failed because of source, target, latitude and time (see the figure): a few Norse with iron tools, weakly backed by one of Europe’s poorer states, could not beat Inuit and Indian hunter-gatherers, “the world’s greatest masters of Arctic survival skills”. Spain succeeded because it was rich and populous, landed at subtropical latitudes fit for farming (at first mostly with American crops, plus Eurasian cattle and horses), and sailed in 1492 after a century of ship development drawing on Islamic, Indian, Chinese and Indonesian advances.

The conquest

  • The West Indies’ million-plus inhabitants were rapidly exterminated by disease, dispossession, enslavement, war and casual murder. The first mainland colony was founded at Panama around 1508; the Aztecs fell in 1519–20 and the Incas in 1532–33, with epidemics killing emperors and much of the population, mounted Spaniards and exploited native divisions doing the rest. Germs alone destroyed the Southeast and Mississippian societies, the Mandans and the Sadlermiut; professional armies, colonial governments and then independent states finished the larger ones.
  • Smaller societies died casually. California’s roughly 200,000 foragers, in a hundred tribelets, were killed or dispossessed during and after the 1848–52 gold rush. The Yahi, about 2,000 people without firearms, were destroyed in four raids: 17 settlers attacking a village at dawn on 6 August 1865; a ravine massacre in 1866; 33 killed in a cave around 1867; and about 30 trapped in another cave by four cowboys around 1868. Amazonian groups died in the rubber boom, and the Yanomamö and others are succumbing today to disease, miners and missionaries.
  • Native societies survive intact mostly on lands Europeans didn’t want; in the tropics many were replaced by Africans, Indians and Javanese. They remain numerous in the Andes (where women of European descent have difficulty even reproducing at high altitude, and Andean crops still work best) and Central America, but of North America’s hundreds of languages only 187 are still spoken and 149 of those are learned by no children; all 40 or so New World nations have an Indo-European or Creole official language, and leaders even in Peru, Bolivia, Mexico and Guatemala are disproportionately European.
  • The native population fell by up to 95 per cent in North America, yet the Americas now hold about ten times their 1492 population, drawn from every continent but Australia, the biggest demographic shift anywhere except Australia, with “ultimate roots in developments between about 11,000 B.C. and A.D. 1”.

Chapter 19

How Africa Became Black

Africa once held five of humanity’s six major divisions. Languages, crops and archaeology show that Bantu farmers from Cameroon and Nigeria, with wet-climate crops and then iron, engulfed the Pygmies and Khoisan; that Borneans settled Madagascar; and that Europe, not Africa, colonised the other because of “differences in real estate”.

Goering Street

  • In Windhoek, capital of newly independent Namibia, Diamond saw Herero, Ovambo, whites and Nama, and a main road called Goering Street. It honoured not Hermann Göring but his father Heinrich, founding Reichskommissar of German South-West Africa, whose legacy included Germany’s 1904 war of extermination against the Herero. Africa’s past is inseparable from its present.
  • Americans equate native Africans with blacks and African history with colonialism and slavery. But by AD 1000 Africa held five major human groups: blacks, whites, African Pygmies, Khoisan and Asians, three of them native only to Africa, and a quarter of the world’s languages. Only Aboriginal Australians and their relatives were missing. Diamond uses the loose labels as shorthand while acknowledging that each group is diverse and that all shade into one another.

What the languages reveal

  • Stanford’s Joseph Greenberg sorted Africa’s 1,500 languages into five families, which roughly match the peoples (see the figure). Four discoveries follow.
  • Semitic is African. Hebrew, Aramaic (Christ’s language) and Arabic are only one of six or more branches of Afroasiatic, all the others (222 languages) confined to Africa; even 12 of the 19 surviving Semitic languages are in Ethiopia. So Africa may have given birth to the languages of the Bible and the Koran, “a big shock for Eurocentric believers”.
  • Pygmies lost their languages. Each band speaks its black farmer neighbours’ language, with some unique words and sounds, like the Semang and Philippine Negritos. Their homeland was engulfed.
  • Khoisan once lived far to the north. Their click languages (the ! in !Kung marks a click) survive in southern Africa and in two stranded Tanzanian languages, Hadza and Sandawe, over 1,000 miles away; clicks also appear in Xhosa and other Bantu languages and in two Afroasiatic languages of Kenya.
  • The Bantu came from Cameroon. Niger-Congo has 1,032 languages; nearly half, the 500 Bantu languages spoken by nearly 200 million people across the south, are so alike they are joked to be “500 dialects of a single language”, one low-order subfamily among 177. The other 176 are crammed into West Africa, and the most distinctive Bantu languages and their closest non-Bantu relatives are packed into a small area of Cameroon and eastern Nigeria. By the same logic, English, though most speakers live in North America, belongs to a Germanic family crowded in north-west Europe, with its closest relative Frisian on a strip of Dutch and German coast; and English did indeed arrive in Britain with the Anglo-Saxons in the fifth and sixth centuries.

What the crops reveal

  • By the 1400s Africans grew five sets of crops (see the figure). North Africa’s winter-rain Fertile Crescent crops, the foundation of Egypt, could not grow in the summer-rain Sahel; the Sahel domesticated its own sorghum (now grown on every continent) and pearl millet; Ethiopia its own chat, ensete, noog, finger millet (for beer), teff (for bread) and coffee; West Africa its rice, yams, oil palm and kola nut, whose caffeine West Africans chewed long before Coca-Cola. Bananas, Asian yams, taro and Asian rice came from South-East Asia.
  • Two negatives matter. Every indigenous African crop originated north of the equator, none south of it; neither Bantu nor white farmers have since domesticated southern African wild plants. And the only animal surely domesticated in Africa is the guinea fowl; donkeys and house cats probably came from Egypt, cattle perhaps partly from North Africa; sheep, goats, chickens, horses and camels came from Eurasia. Not one of Africa’s famous big mammals (zebras, wildebeest, rhinos, hippos, giraffes, buffalo) was ever domesticated.
  • Language dates the crops too. In southern Nigeria, words for yams, oil palm and kola are shared across all languages (oldest); words for bananas and Asian yams are shared only within subgroups (arrived later); words for corn and peanuts follow trade routes, often with Portuguese names (after 1492). Christopher Ehret has used such evidence, with glottochronology, to date domestications. The picture: ancestral Nilo-Saharan speakers domesticated sorghum and millet in the Sahara; ancestral Niger-Congo speakers the West African wet-climate crops; Afroasiatic speakers perhaps Ethiopia’s crops and certainly brought Fertile Crescent crops to North Africa. These families survived not because they were better languages but because their speakers were in the right place to acquire food production.

What archaeology reveals

  • African farming may have begun not in Egypt but in the Sahara, wetter between about 9000 and 4000 BC, with lakes and game: cattle and pottery, then sheep and goats, perhaps sorghum and millet, before the full Southwest Asian package reached Egypt around 5200 BC. Cattle herders crossed from Ethiopia into northern Kenya by about 2500 BC.
  • Madagascar. Austronesians arrived by AD 800, possibly by 300, with iron tools, livestock and crops, a full expedition, not a blown-off canoe, and exterminated elephant birds, gorilla-sized lemurs and pygmy hippos. The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, written by an Egyptian merchant around AD 100, describes trade between India, Egypt and East Africa; Islamic-era sites are full of Middle Eastern and even Chinese pottery, glass and porcelain; in 1498 Vasco da Gama found Swahili towns and a pilot to guide him to India. Perhaps Austronesians came via India and East Africa: Malagasy has Kenyan Bantu loanwords, though Kenyan languages have no Austronesian ones, and East Africa shows few Indonesian traces besides crops, xylophones and zithers. Or perhaps, incredibly, they sailed straight across. The mystery remains.
  • The Bantu expansion (see the figure). In Zambia, north of today’s Khoisan range, archaeologists have found Khoisan-like skulls and tools. The Bantu began spreading from the savanna into the coastal forest perhaps as early as 3000 BC, with cattle and yams but no metal, still fishing and hunting; they lost their cattle to tsetse flies in the forest and pushed the Pygmies into it. By soon after 1000 BC they reached the Great Lakes, where their wet-climate crops let them farm land unsuited to the Afroasiatic and Nilo-Saharan millet and sorghum farmers already there; they gained sorghum and millet (with Nilo-Saharan names), regained cattle, and got iron.
  • Sub-Saharan iron appears soon after 1000 BC, suspiciously close to its arrival at Carthage; but West Africans had smelted copper since at least 2000 BC, and African smiths’ techniques were so different, reaching temperatures that made steel over 2,000 years before the Bessemer furnace, that independent invention is plausible. With iron and wet-climate crops the Bantu had “a military-industrial package that was unstoppable”: to the south lay 2,000 miles of thinly settled Khoisan land, and within a few centuries they reached Natal.
  • It was not a simple stampede: Khoisan already had sheep and cattle; the first Bantu leapfrogged dry country, traded and intermarried; only gradually did they fill it in. But the result was the same, probably through expulsion, killing, taking wives, and diseases like malaria, bred around villages, to which the Bantu had resistance and the Khoisan probably did not.

Why Europe colonised Africa

  • That the Cape’s winter rains stopped the Bantu shaped South Africa. Whites could claim, correctly but irrelevantly, to have occupied it before the Bantu; more importantly, the first Dutch ships met only sparse Khoisan herders. Had they faced dense, steel-equipped Bantu farmers, they might never have established themselves at all.
  • An extraterrestrial 10,000 years ago might have predicted Europe would become vassal states of an African empire: Africa had the longest human history, the most varied habitats and the greatest human diversity. The proximate reasons for the opposite are guns, literacy and states: four years after reaching East Africa in 1498, Vasco da Gama returned with a cannon-bristling fleet and forced the surrender of Kilwa, which controlled the Zimbabwe gold trade.
  • The ultimate reasons are four. Animals: Africa’s were all imported from Eurasia, thousands of years late; its buffalo, zebras, bush pigs, rhinos and hippos were never domesticated (Hannibal’s elephants were only tamed). “Rhino-mounted Bantu shock troops could have overthrown the Roman Empire. It never happened.” Plants: fewer domesticates, so farming perhaps began several thousand years later than in the Fertile Crescent. Area: Africa is about half Eurasia’s size, only about a third of it in the sub-Saharan farming zone north of the equator before 1000 BC; its population is under 700 million against Eurasia’s 4 billion. Axis: north–south.
  • The axis stalled everything: Egypt’s wheat and barley stopped in the Sudan and reached the Cape only in 1652; Bantu crops stopped at the Fish River; Asian bananas had to wait for Indian Ocean shipping in the first millennium AD; tsetse flies killed the Bantu’s cattle; horses, in Egypt by about 1800 BC, crossed the Sahara only in the first millennium AD and never went further south; livestock took over 2,000 years to cross the Serengeti. Pottery, in the Sudan and Sahara around 8000 BC, reached the Cape around AD 1. Writing spread from Egypt to Meroë, and reached Ethiopia perhaps from Arabia, but elsewhere came only with Arabs and Europeans.
  • So Europe’s colonisation of Africa “had nothing to do with differences between European and African peoples themselves, as white racists assume.” It stemmed from area, axes and wild species: “differences in real estate”.

Epilogue

The Future of Human History as a Science

The answer to Yali: four sets of environmental differences, not differences between peoples. Within Eurasia, the Fertile Crescent destroyed its fragile environment, and unified China could switch off innovation with one decision while fragmented Europe could not. Culture and individuals are wild cards. History can be a historical science, like geology or evolutionary biology.

The answer to Yali

  • “The striking differences between the long-term histories of peoples of the different continents have been due not to innate differences in the peoples themselves but to differences in their environments.” Swap Aborigines and Eurasians in the late Pleistocene, Diamond expects, and Aborigines would now hold most of the world. That cannot be tested directly, but related tests can: the same ancestral peoples went extinct, reverted to foraging or built states depending on where they landed (European farmers in Greenland and on the Great Plains; Chinese-derived farmers on the Chathams, in Borneo’s forests, on Java and Hawaii; Aborigines on Flinders Island, in Tasmania and in south-eastern Australia).
  • A laundry list of differences is not an answer; four sets matter most (see the figure). They are objective and quantifiable: one may dispute Diamond’s impression that New Guineans are smarter, but not that New Guinea is smaller than Eurasia and has fewer big animals.
  • “Geographic determinism” raises hackles, as if humans were passive robots. But without human inventiveness “all of us today would still be cutting our meat with stone tools and eating it raw”. Every society has inventive people; some environments give them more materials and better conditions.
  • Compressing 13,000 years into 400 pages means about one page per continent per 150 years, but long-term comparison yields insights no short study can. The agenda: quantify further, for example tallying big-seeded pulses by continent, how many mammal candidates each disqualifying factor removes (especially in Africa), and the rates of diffusion along each axis.

Why Europe and not the Fertile Crescent

  • A historian at any time between 8500 BC and AD 1450 would have rated European dominance the least likely outcome. Until Greece and Italy rose after 500 BC nearly every major western Eurasian innovation (domestication, writing, metallurgy, wheels, states) came from in or near the Fertile Crescent; Europe north and west of the Alps contributed nothing significant until water mills proliferated after about 900; from 1000 to 1450 science and technology flowed into Europe from Islam; China led the world in technology.
  • Proximate explanations (a merchant class, capitalism, patents, no absolute despots, a Greco-Judaeo-Christian tradition of inquiry) need ultimate ones. For the Fertile Crescent the answer is ecological suicide. Power moved west: Babylon, the Hittites, Assyria, Persia; then Alexander in the late fourth century BC, Rome in the second, and finally western and northern Europe.
  • “Fertile Crescent” now sounds absurd: much of it is desert, steppe, or eroded and salinised land, its oil wealth concealing long poverty. It was once largely forested, as was Greece, but was cleared for fields, timber, firewood and plaster; with low rainfall, regrowth could not keep up, goats overgrazed, soil eroded, valleys silted and irrigation salted the land. The last forests near Petra were felled by the Ottomans for the Hejaz railway just before the First World War. Northern and western Europe escaped not by wisdom but by luck, with rain enough for vegetation to regrow, and still farms intensively 7,000 years after farming arrived.

Why Europe and not China

  • China had an early start, great ecological diversity, the world’s largest regional population and a less fragile environment, and in the Middle Ages led the world in technology, power and the seas. Seven treasure fleets sailed between 1405 and 1433, each of hundreds of ships up to 400 feet long with crews up to 28,000, as far as East Africa, decades before Columbus’s “three puny ships”.
  • Then a court struggle between the eunuchs, who ran the fleets, and their opponents ended them; the shipyards were dismantled and ocean shipping forbidden. Such reversals happen everywhere (London’s strangled electric lighting in the 1880s, American isolationism between the wars), but in unified China one decision stopped fleets everywhere, and with no shipyards left, it could not be reversed. China also abandoned an elaborate water-driven spinning machine, stepped back from the verge of an industrial revolution in the fourteenth century, virtually abolished the mechanical clocks it had led the world in, and retreated from mechanical technology after the late fifteenth century; in the Cultural Revolution a few leaders closed the whole school system for five years.
  • In fragmented Europe, Columbus, Italian by birth, tried the duke of Anjou, the king of Portugal, the duke of Medina-Sedonia and the count of Medina-Celi, and succeeded on his fifth try with the Spanish monarchs, who had first refused (see the figure). Once Spain grew rich, six other states followed. Cannon, printing, firearms and electric lighting were likewise opposed somewhere, adopted somewhere else, and spread.
  • Why is China chronically united and Europe chronically divided? Geography (see the figure). China was unified in 221 BC and always reunified after breaks; Europe defeated Charlemagne, Napoleon and Hitler, and Rome never held more than half of it. Connectedness cuts both ways: too much lets one despot turn off the tap, too little stops ideas spreading. Diamond suggests technology developed fastest over the last thousand years where connectedness was moderate: Europe, against China (high) and perhaps India (low). Other factors also mattered: the Mongols wrecked the irrigation of Iran and Iraq but never settled Europe’s forests; the Fertile Crescent sat astride trade routes; China was “a gigantic virtual island within a continent”.

Does geography still matter?

  • The Fertile Crescent and China show that past primacy guarantees nothing. But the “new rules” of instant ideas and overnight air freight are variations on old ones: the transistor, invented at Bell Labs in 1947, leapt 8,000 miles to Japan but not to Zaire or Paraguay. The rising powers (Taiwan, Korea, Malaysia, Japan) were long ago drawn into the old food-producing centres, with long histories of literacy, machinery and government. The Fertile Crescent and China still dominate through their successors, their early neighbours and their emigrants’ states (the United States, Australia, Brazil). “The hand of history’s course at 8000 B.C. lies heavily on us.”

Wild cards: culture and individuals

  • Some cultural traits arise for trivial reasons and become fixed. QWERTY won through 1860s typewriter design, salesmanship, a decision in 1882 by a Ms Longley of Cincinnati’s Shorthand and Typewriter Institute, and her star pupil Frank McGurrin thrashing Louis Taub in a typing contest in 1888. The Sumerians counted in twelves (hence 60-minute hours, 24-hour days, 12 months, 360 degrees), Mesoamericans in twenties (two calendar cycles of 260 and 365 days). Chinese children learn faster with pinyin than with characters, which may exist because Chinese has so many homophones. Why no Andean writing, Indian castes, Chinese Confucian conservatism, or proselytising religion driving conquest in Europe and West Asia but not China? These are open questions, best approached after environmental effects are accounted for.
  • Individuals too. The bomb under the table on 20 July 1944 nearly killed Hitler; in summer 1930 a truck braked just in time to avoid crushing the car in which he rode in the “death seat”. Carlyle thought history “the History of the Great Men”; Bismarck that the statesman can only catch God’s coat-tails as He marches past. But no Great Man explains why western Eurasia had literate, iron-using states when Australia had only hunter-gatherer tribes.

History as a science

  • History is usually counted among the humanities, and historians are rarely trained in science. But “science” means knowledge (scientia), gained by whatever methods suit the field. Historical sciences (astronomy, climatology, ecology, evolutionary biology, geology, palaeontology) differ from physics and chemistry in four ways (see the figure).
  • Their answer is the comparative method and natural experiments, as epidemiologists compare high- and low-salt populations and anthropologists compare Polynesian islands. Human history offers many: the continents, large islands such as Japan, Madagascar, Hispaniola, New Guinea and Hawaii, hundreds of smaller islands, and regions within continents. Epidemiology, ecology and evolutionary biology have worked out methods for confounding variables and causal inference. Since introspection gives us more insight into humans than into dinosaurs, Diamond is optimistic that human history can be studied as scientifically, “with profit to our own society today”.

Thirty years on

What held up

The book was written in 1996–97 and dates its own evidence (“within the present decade”, “currently in doubt”). Here is how its checkable claims look from 2026.

Held

Out of TaiwanCh. 17

The Austronesian expansion from Taiwan through the Philippines and Indonesia into the Pacific is now supported by genetics and ancient DNA. One refinement: ancient genomes from Vanuatu and Tonga (Skoglund and colleagues, 2016) show the first Lapita settlers of the remote Pacific had almost no Papuan ancestry, which arrived in later waves.

Held

The Bantu expansionCh. 19

Genetic studies since have confirmed a spread of Bantu-speaking farmers from the Cameroon–Nigeria region across central, eastern and southern Africa, with the admixture with foragers that Diamond inferred from clicks and loanwords.

Held

Axes and diffusionCh. 10

Quantitative tests have found support: Peter Turchin and colleagues (2006) showed large historical empires tended to spread east–west, and David Laitin, Joachim Moortgat and Amanda Robinson (2012, PNAS) found languages and cultural traits more alike along east–west than north–south axes. Ola Olsson and Douglas Hibbs (2005) found Diamond’s biogeographic variables predict the timing of farming and even modern incomes.

Partly

Where crowd diseases came fromCh. 11

The principle stands, and Diamond himself helped refine it (Wolfe, Dunavan and Diamond, Nature, 2007). Measles did come from rinderpest. But several entries in the book’s Table 11.1 have changed: human tuberculosis is not from cattle (the cattle strain derives from a human-adapted lineage); smallpox’s closest relatives are camelpox and a gerbil poxvirus, not cowpox; and falciparum malaria came from gorillas (2010), not birds.

Partly

Where and when farming beganCh. 5, 8, 15

The Fertile Crescent’s early lead holds. But research has added centres and complexity: excavations at Kuk Swamp (2003) confirmed early independent farming in the New Guinea highlands, as Diamond thought likely, and later syntheses count more independent centres than his five to nine. Domestication is now seen as more protracted, over millennia, than a quick set of mutations.

Partly

Settlement datesCh. 1, 2

New Zealand’s settlement is now dated to around AD 1250–1300, not AD 1000, and the settlement of East Polynesia generally later than the book assumed. The megafauna “overkill” debate continues, with most researchers now seeing human hunting and climate both involved.

Missed

Clovis firstCh. 1

Diamond expected pre-Clovis sites like Monte Verde to be “reinterpreted”. Instead Monte Verde (about 14,500 years old) was accepted in 1997, and later finds such as Cooper’s Ferry in Idaho (about 16,000 years) and footprints at White Sands, New Mexico (reported in 2021 at 21,000–23,000 years and still debated) push human arrival well before Clovis.

Missed

No Neanderthal interbreedingCh. 1

He saw “little or no evidence of hybridization”. The Neanderthal genome (2010) showed that people outside Africa carry roughly 1–2 per cent Neanderthal DNA. The African origin of modern humans, which he called “currently in doubt”, is now firmly established, with this archaic admixture.

Missed

The end of foraging within a decadeCh. 4, 6

He predicted the last hunter-gatherer bands would be gone within ten years. Some Hadza of Tanzania still live mainly by foraging, and uncontacted groups survive in Amazonia and on North Sentinel Island.

Context

Where it has been argued with

Geographic determinism2000 →

The most common charge is that the book explains too much by environment and too little by human choice. The geographer J. M. Blaut included Diamond in Eight Eurocentric Historians (2000), arguing that environmental explanations can smuggle in European superiority. Diamond’s reply is in the Epilogue: the factors are objective and quantifiable, and inventiveness is universal; environments differ in what they give it to work with.

Institutions versus geography2002 →

Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson and James Robinson’s “Reversal of Fortune” (2002) found that the richest colonies of 1500 became among the poorest, which they attribute to the institutions Europeans imposed rather than geography; Why Nations Fail (2012) opens by rejecting the geography hypothesis. Diamond’s review in the New York Review of Books (2012) granted institutions’ importance but argued that they, too, have deep geographic and agricultural roots.

The last 500 years1981 →

The book explains Eurasia versus the rest far better than Europe versus China or Islam. Its fragmentation argument echoes E. L. Jones’s The European Miracle (1981); Kenneth Pomeranz’s The Great Divergence (2000) argues Europe and China were comparable until about 1800 and that coal and colonies made the difference; Philip Hoffman’s Why Did Europe Conquer the World? (2015) develops the competition idea as a military “tournament”.

Anthropologists’ objections2004 →

Some anthropologists object to the broad-brush treatment of particular peoples and to reducing local histories to continental ones. Frederick Errington and Deborah Gewertz’s Yali’s Question: Sugar, Culture, and History (2004) returns to Papua New Guinea, through the story of a sugar company, to argue that recent colonial and economic history matters more to Yali’s question than deep geography. Diamond’s claim that New Guineans are probably genetically smarter has also been criticised as a mirror image of the reasoning he rejects.

What it leaves out1997

Critics note how little space goes to ideas, religion, law and individual agency, which the Epilogue itself flags as “wild cards”, and to India and the Islamic world. Later editions added a chapter on the origins of the Japanese and an afterword, which this summary does not cover.

Terms of art

Glossary

Proximate cause
The immediate reason for an outcome, such as steel swords at Cajamarca.
Ultimate cause
The deep, underlying reason, such as the species available to domesticate.
Calibrated date
A radiocarbon date corrected (by tree rings and other methods) for past changes in atmospheric carbon 14; the book uses calibrated dates, which look older than uncalibrated ones.
Great Leap Forward
Diamond’s name for the burst of modern behaviour around 50,000 years ago: standardised and bone tools, art, jewellery, compound weapons.
Overkill hypothesis
The idea that newly arrived human hunters exterminated the big mammals of Australia and the Americas.
Natural experiment
A historical comparison in which conditions differ in a way that tests a hypothesis, as on Polynesian islands.
Food production
Growing crops and raising livestock, as opposed to hunting and gathering.
Fertile Crescent
The arc of uplands from the Levant through south-eastern Turkey to western Iran where farming began earliest.
Founder crops
The eight plants that launched farming in the Fertile Crescent: emmer and einkorn wheat, barley, lentil, pea, chickpea, bitter vetch and flax.
Preemptive domestication
When a crop spreads so fast that nobody domesticates its wild ancestor or close relatives again elsewhere.
Non-shattering mutant
A wild cereal whose seeds stay on the stalk: fatal in the wild, but the only kind harvesters could collect and sow.
Selfer
A plant that usually pollinates itself, so a useful mutant breeds true.
Teosinte
The wild Mexican grass from which corn was slowly developed.
Anna Karenina principle
Success requires avoiding every one of many possible failures; applied to which animals could be domesticated.
Ancient Fourteen
The big mammals domesticated before the twentieth century: the Major Five (sheep, goat, cow, pig, horse) and the Minor Nine.
Axis orientation
Whether a continent’s main extent runs east–west (easy spread along one latitude) or north–south (spread across climate zones).
Crowd diseases
Acute, immunising epidemics that need large, dense host populations, mostly evolved from diseases of herd animals.
Rebus principle
Writing an abstract word with the picture of a word that sounds the same: the Sumerian breakthrough to phonetic writing.
Blueprint copying / idea diffusion
Borrowing an invention in detail, versus borrowing only the idea and reinventing the details, as Sequoyah did.
Autocatalysis
A process that speeds itself up, like technology building on technology, or farming and population growth.
Band / tribe / chiefdom / state
Diamond’s four types of society by size and centralisation.
Kleptocracy
Rule by elites who transfer wealth from commoners to themselves, justified by ideology or religion.
Redistribution, tribute
A chief collecting produce and giving it back; when the elite keeps most of it, it becomes tribute, the ancestor of taxes.
Austronesian expansion
The spread of farming, seafaring peoples from South China via Taiwan to Indonesia, the Pacific and Madagascar.
Lapita
The geometric-banded pottery that marks the first Austronesians in the Bismarcks and Remote Oceania.
Bantu expansion
The spread of Bantu-speaking farmers, later with iron, from Cameroon and Nigeria over most of Africa south of the equator.
Proto-language
An unwritten ancestral language reconstructed by comparing its descendants, such as Proto-Indo-European or Proto-Austronesian.
Glottochronology
Estimating when languages split from average rates of vocabulary change.
Firestick farming
Aboriginal Australians’ deliberate burning of land to boost game and edible plants.

Using it

A reading path

The book runs to some 400 pages and repeats its framework region by region. For the argument:

  1. Prologue and Chapter 3

    Yali’s question and Cajamarca: the puzzle, made vivid.

  2. Chapters 4, 8 and 9

    Farming, starter kits and the Anna Karenina principle.

  3. Chapters 10 and 11

    Axes and germs: the two most famous ideas.

  4. Chapters 13 and 14

    Technology and the rise of states: how food became power.

  5. Chapter 15 and the Epilogue

    Australia as the test case, then Europe versus China and history as a science.

Read alongside

  • Why Nations Fail, summarised on this site: institutions as the rival answer.
  • The Beginning of Infinity, summarised on this site: Deutsch’s objection that creativity, not environment, drives history.
  • William H. McNeill, Plagues and Peoples (1976): disease as a force in history.
  • Alfred W. Crosby, Ecological Imperialism (1986): how European plants, animals and germs conquered new lands.
  • Kenneth Pomeranz, The Great Divergence (2000): why Europe, not China, industrialised.
  • David Reich, Who We Are and How We Got Here (2018): what ancient DNA has since shown about migrations the book reconstructs from languages and pots.
