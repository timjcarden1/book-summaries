Guns, Germs, and Steel

The Fates of Human Societies

“Why is it that you white people developed so much cargo and brought it to New Guinea, but we black people had little cargo of our own?”

Yali, a New Guinean politician, to Diamond in 1972. The question the book answers.

Jared Diamond · 1997 · Pulitzer Prize 1998

The whole book in one breath

Why did Eurasians conquer the Americas, Australia and much of Africa, and not the other way round? Diamond’s answer is that history followed different courses for different peoples because of differences in their environments, not in the peoples themselves. The Fertile Crescent and the rest of Eurasia began with the richest set of wild plants and animals that could be domesticated. Eurasia’s east–west axis let crops, animals and ideas spread along similar latitudes. Farming meant food surpluses, dense populations and settled societies with specialists, states, writing and technology. Crowded living beside livestock bred epidemic diseases, to which Eurasians slowly built immunity. When the continents collided, the proximate weapons were guns, germs and steel, but the ultimate causes lay thousands of years back, in geography and biology.

The author

A physiologist who became a geographer in New Guinea

Jared Diamond, born in 1937, trained in physiology and spent decades doing fieldwork on the birds of New Guinea, which is how he came to know Yali and the question that frames the book. He became a professor of geography at UCLA. Guns, Germs, and Steel won the 1998 Pulitzer Prize for general nonfiction and became one of the most widely read works of big history. It followed The Third Chimpanzee (1991) and was followed by Collapse (2005).

The book is deliberately anti-racist in purpose. Diamond sets out to show that the lopsided outcomes of modern history need no appeal to differences in intelligence or character between peoples, and that the evidence rules such explanations out.

The argument

Proximate causes, ultimate causes

  • Proximate causes explain the moment of conquest: steel weapons, guns, horses, ocean-going ships, writing, centralised states, and above all the germs Europeans carried.
  • Ultimate causes explain why those advantages were on the Eurasian side: which wild plants and animals were available to domesticate, and how easily crops and ideas could spread across each continent.
  • Food production is the hinge. Wherever farming and herding began early and spread widely, populations grew dense, societies stratified, and technology, writing and states followed.
  • The differences are environmental, not biological. Given the same starting kit, any people would have followed a similar path. When peoples gained access to crops and animals, they adopted them quickly.

If you remember nothing else

Twelve ideas that carry the book

Prologue

Environment, not biology

History diverged because continents offered different raw materials, not because their peoples differed in ability.

Ch. 1

Everyone started level

Around 11,000 BC, the end of the last Ice Age, humans on every continent were hunter-gatherers. The divergence came after.

Ch. 2

Environment shapes society

Maori and Moriori shared Polynesian ancestry. Different islands made one people farmers and warriors and the other peaceful hunter-gatherers.

Ch. 4

Farming powers everything else

Food surpluses support larger populations and specialists: kings, soldiers, scribes, inventors.

Ch. 8

Starting kits differed

The Fertile Crescent had wild cereals and pulses easy to domesticate. Other regions had few or none.

Ch. 9

The Anna Karenina principle

A species is domesticable only if it passes every test (diet, growth, breeding, temper, panic, social structure). Failing any one rules it out.

Ch. 10

Axes matter

Crops spread easily east–west along similar latitudes. North–south continents forced them across climates, and they stalled.

Ch. 11

Germs did most of the conquering

Diseases from livestock, bred in dense populations, killed far more Native Americans than steel did.

Ch. 12–13

Ideas spread more than they are invented

Writing was invented only a few times. Most societies borrowed it or the idea of it. Connected, populous regions innovate fastest.

Ch. 14

Bigger societies need kleptocrats

Bands, tribes, chiefdoms and states each follow from population size, and chiefs and states keep power partly through ideology.

Part IV

The same logic, continent by continent

China, Polynesia, the Americas and Africa each follow the book’s framework.

Epilogue

Europe, not China, because of fragmentation

Unified China could halt innovation with one decision. Divided Europe could not, so competition kept it moving.

Prologue

Yali’s Question

Why did wealth and power end up so unequally distributed?

  • In 1972, on a New Guinea beach, a local politician named Yali asked Diamond why white people had so much “cargo” (manufactured goods) and New Guineans so little. The book is a twenty-five-year answer.
  • Diamond rejects the answer, explicit or implied, that some peoples are smarter. If anything, he argues from his experience, New Guineans are at least as intelligent as Westerners.
  • He sets out the distinction that organises the book: proximate causes of conquest versus the ultimate, environmental causes behind them.

Part One

From Eden to Cajamarca

Where humans were at the end of the Ice Age, a Polynesian test of the theory, and the collision that makes the question vivid.

Chapter 1

Up to the Starting Line

By 11,000 BC, no continent had a decisive head start.

  • Humans evolved in Africa, spread across Eurasia, reached Australia and New Guinea tens of thousands of years ago, and entered the Americas by around 11,000 BC.
  • Around the same times, big mammals vanished from Australia and the Americas, probably hunted out by newly arrived humans who had evolved alongside no such prey. That loss mattered later: fewer large animals left to domesticate.
  • At the end of the last Ice Age, people everywhere were hunter-gatherers. That is the starting line for the book’s race.

Chapter 2

A Natural Experiment of History

One ancestral people, different islands, very different societies.

  • The Maori of New Zealand and the Moriori of the Chatham Islands descended from the same Polynesian settlers.
  • On the small, cold Chathams, where crops wouldn’t grow, the Moriori became hunter-gatherers in a small, peaceable society. In New Zealand the Maori farmed, grew numerous and developed dense, warlike chiefdoms.
  • In 1835 Maori invaders landed on the Chathams and killed or enslaved the Moriori, a miniature of the whole book’s story.
  • Across Polynesia, island size, climate and resources predicted how complex each society became.

Chapter 3

Collision at Cajamarca

How 168 Spaniards captured an emperor surrounded by tens of thousands of soldiers.

  • In November 1532, Francisco Pizarro’s small force ambushed and captured the Inca emperor Atahuallpa at Cajamarca, in the midst of his army.
  • The proximate advantages are listed one by one: steel swords and armour, guns, horses, and ships that brought the Spaniards there at all.
  • Smallpox, spreading ahead of the Europeans, had killed the previous emperor and his heir and set off the civil war Atahuallpa had just won.
  • Writing played a part too. Pizarro knew how Cortés had conquered the Aztecs. Atahuallpa knew almost nothing about the strangers.

Part Two

The Rise and Spread of Food Production

The core of the book: why farming began where and when it did, and why it spread fast in some places and not in others.

Chapter 4

Farmer Power

Food production is the root of the proximate advantages.

  • Farming produces far more calories per acre than foraging, so it supports far larger populations.
  • Stored surpluses let societies feed people who don’t produce food: chiefs, bureaucrats, soldiers and craftsmen.
  • Domestic animals add meat, milk, fertiliser, traction, transport and, later, war horses. They also add germs.

Chapter 5

History’s Haves and Have-Nots

Only a handful of places invented farming on their own, and at very different times.

  • By Diamond’s estimates, food production began independently in the Fertile Crescent (around 8500 BC), China (by about 7500 BC), Mesoamerica and the Andes (by about 3500 BC), and the eastern United States (about 2500 BC), with New Guinea and parts of Africa as further candidates.
  • Everywhere else, farming arrived by spreading from these centres, carried by migrants or adopted by locals.
  • The thousands of years’ difference in start dates is the head start that mattered.

Chapter 6

To Farm or Not to Farm

Nobody decided to invent farming. It crept in.

  • Early farming was often a worse life than foraging, so the shift was gradual, not a conscious choice.
  • Wild game declined, domesticable wild plants became more available, storage and harvesting techniques improved, and growing populations needed more food per acre.
  • Once farming societies existed, they outnumbered and displaced hunter-gatherers around them.

Chapter 7

How to Make an Almond

Crops were created by unconscious selection.

  • Wild almonds contain a deadly bitter compound. A rare mutation made some trees sweet, and people who gathered the sweet ones spread their seeds.
  • Harvesting selected for bigger seeds, seeds that stay on the stalk instead of scattering, and seeds that sprout together. That turned wild grasses into wheat and barley.
  • Plants that self-pollinate, like the early Fertile Crescent crops, were easiest to domesticate because desirable traits bred true.

Chapter 8

Apples or Indians

Regions lagged because of their plants, not their people.

  • The Fertile Crescent’s Mediterranean climate favoured big-seeded annual plants. Its eight founder crops were emmer wheat, einkorn wheat, barley, lentil, pea, chickpea, bitter vetch and flax.
  • New Guinea and the eastern United States did domesticate plants, but their starter kits were poor in protein and yield.
  • The title asks whether Native Americans failed to domesticate the apple because of the apples or because of the Indians. The evidence points to the plants: where good crops arrived from elsewhere, locals adopted them fast.

Chapter 9

Zebras, Unhappy Marriages, and the Anna Karenina Principle

Almost all the large domesticable animals were in Eurasia.

  • Tolstoy’s Anna Karenina opens by saying happy families are all alike, while each unhappy family is unhappy in its own way. Domesticable animals are alike in passing every test, and wild ones fail in different ways (see the figures below).
  • Only 14 large mammal species were domesticated in ancient times. Five became widespread: sheep, goats, cattle, pigs and horses. Thirteen of the 14 were native to Eurasia. The Americas had only the llama and alpaca, and Africa south of the Sahara and Australia had none.
  • Zebras look like horses but bite and don’t let go, and they are nearly impossible to lasso. Africans didn’t fail to try. The animals failed the test.

Chapter 10

Spacious Skies and Tilted Axes

Crops spread easily east–west, and slowly or not at all north–south.

  • Places at the same latitude share day length, seasons and often climate, so a crop or animal suited to one suits the others.
  • Eurasia’s long axis runs east–west, and Fertile Crescent crops spread quickly to Europe, Egypt and the Indus valley.
  • The Americas and Africa run north–south, across deserts and tropics. Maize took thousands of years to reach what is now the eastern United States. The llama and potato of the Andes never reached Mesoamerica.
  • The same geography slowed the spread of writing, wheels and other technologies (see the figure below).

Part Three

From Food to Guns, Germs, and Steel

How food production turned into the proximate advantages: diseases, writing, technology and political organisation.

Chapter 11

Lethal Gift of Livestock

Epidemic diseases came from animals and needed dense populations to survive.

  • Measles, smallpox, influenza, tuberculosis and other crowd diseases evolved from pathogens of domestic animals.
  • They need large, dense populations to persist, which only farming societies provided. Survivors passed on resistance over generations.
  • When Europeans reached the Americas, their diseases killed most of the native population, by some estimates up to 95%, often ahead of any European soldier.
  • Few diseases travelled the other way, because the Americas had few domestic animals to breed them.

Chapter 12

Blueprints and Borrowed Letters

Writing was invented only a few times, and spread by copying.

  • Writing arose independently in Sumer (by about 3000 BC), in Mesoamerica, and probably in China. Most other systems were borrowed.
  • Borrowing happens either by copying a system in detail (blueprint copying) or by adopting only the idea (idea diffusion). The Cherokee syllabary, created by Sequoyah after seeing English writing, is the classic case of the second.
  • Early writing served the needs of states and elites, such as tax records and royal propaganda, so it arose only in complex, food-producing societies.

Chapter 13

Necessity’s Mother

Invention is often the mother of necessity, and societies differ in what they accept.

  • Many inventions came first and found their uses later. Edison’s phonograph was not built for music.
  • Whether a society adopts an invention depends on economics, prestige and politics. Japan largely gave up guns in the Tokugawa era, and societies can reject useful technology for centuries.
  • Technology builds on itself, so large, connected populations with many competing societies innovate fastest. Isolated ones can even lose technology, as Tasmania did after it was cut off from Australia.

Chapter 14

From Egalitarianism to Kleptocracy

As societies grow, they need centralised power, and it extracts a price.

  • Societies fall into rough types by size and organisation (see the table below).
  • Bigger societies can’t resolve conflicts among strangers informally, so power centralises. Rulers keep it by disarming the populace, redistributing some tribute, keeping order and promoting an ideology or religion that justifies them.
  • States typically form by the merger of smaller units, under external threat or by conquest.

Part Four

Around the World in Five Chapters

The framework tested region by region.

Chapter 15

Yali’s People

New Guinea and Australia: why their peoples didn’t develop the Eurasian package.

  • New Guinea’s highlanders invented farming independently, growing taro, bananas, yams and sugarcane. But they had few protein-rich crops and no large domesticable animals, and the rugged terrain split the island into many small societies.
  • Australia is arid and unpredictable, with almost no domesticable plants or animals. Aboriginal Australians remained hunter-gatherers.
  • Tasmania, cut off by rising seas, even lost technologies it once had, which shows how population size and contact sustain innovation.

Chapter 16

How China Became Chinese

Early unification made China one culture over a huge area.

  • China’s geography, with long rivers and few internal barriers, favoured early political and cultural unification.
  • Chinese farmers and languages spread outward, absorbing or displacing other peoples. That is why China today is so linguistically uniform compared with, say, New Guinea.
  • Rice-farming expansion from southern China also seeded the migrations of the next chapter.

Chapter 17

Speedboat to Polynesia

The Austronesian expansion from Taiwan across half the world.

  • Farmers speaking Austronesian languages spread from Taiwan through the Philippines and Indonesia, then across the Pacific to Polynesia, and west as far as Madagascar.
  • They displaced or absorbed hunter-gatherers almost everywhere, except in New Guinea, where established farmers held their ground.

Chapter 18

Hemispheres Colliding

Eurasia and the Americas compared, factor by factor.

  • Eurasia had the earlier start, more domesticates, an east–west axis, crowd diseases, writing spread widely, and many competing states.
  • The Americas had fewer domesticates, a north–south axis, barriers between their civilisations, and little immunity to Eurasian germs.
  • So the collision after 1492 was lopsided. Even the Norse, who reached North America around AD 1000, failed there, because they arrived without the full Eurasian package.

Chapter 19

How Africa Became Black

Africa’s peoples, the Bantu expansion, and why Africa didn’t colonise Europe.

  • Africa held several very different peoples. From West Africa, Bantu-speaking farmers, later with iron, spread across much of the south and centre of the continent, displacing hunter-gatherers.
  • Africa had fewer domesticable plants and animals, a north–south axis, and tropical diseases that affected livestock and people.
  • Madagascar was settled not from nearby Africa but by Austronesians from Borneo, a surprise the book’s framework explains.

Epilogue

The Future of Human History as a Science

History can be studied scientifically, like astronomy or evolutionary biology.

  • Human history can’t be run as an experiment, but it offers natural experiments, like the Polynesian islands, that can test explanations.
  • The Fertile Crescent lost its lead partly by degrading its own fragile environment through deforestation and salinisation.
  • China, not Europe, looked set to lead around 1400. But China’s unity let a single court decision halt its treasure fleets in the 1430s, while Europe’s fragmentation meant no one could stop innovation everywhere.
  • Individuals and culture matter, Diamond grants, but in the long run they operate inside the limits geography sets.

Context

Where it has been argued with

Geographic determinism

The most common charge is that the book explains too much by environment and too little by human choice. Critics include the geographer J. M. Blaut, who called it Eurocentric despite its intentions (2000), Acemoglu and Robinson in Why Nations Fail (2012), and David Deutsch (2011).

The last 500 years

The argument explains Eurasia versus the rest well, but says less about why western Europe and not China or the Islamic world industrialised. Economic historians such as Kenneth Pomeranz in The Great Divergence (2000) treat that as a separate, later question.

Anthropologists’ objections

Some anthropologists object to how the book portrays specific peoples and to its broad-brush use of their histories. They note that local agency, trade and colonial policy shaped outcomes in ways a continental model flattens.

What held up

The core claims about domestication, the head start of the Fertile Crescent and the role of Eurasian diseases in the Americas remain widely accepted. Later quantitative work has found support for the axis hypothesis.

Terms of art

Glossary

Proximate cause
The immediate reason for an outcome, such as steel swords at Cajamarca.
Ultimate cause
The deep, underlying reason, such as the species available to domesticate.
Fertile Crescent
The arc from the eastern Mediterranean through Mesopotamia where farming began earliest.
Founder crops
The eight plants domesticated first in the Fertile Crescent, from emmer wheat to flax.
Anna Karenina principle
Success requires avoiding every one of many possible failures.
Crowd diseases
Epidemics that need large, dense host populations to persist, most of them from livestock.
Axis orientation
Whether a continent’s main extent runs east–west (easy spread) or north–south (hard spread).
Blueprint copying
Borrowing a technology or writing system in detail.
Idea diffusion
Borrowing only the idea, then inventing the details anew.
Band / tribe / chiefdom / state
Diamond’s sequence of societies by size and centralisation.
Kleptocracy
Rule by elites who take wealth from commoners and justify it with ideology.
Natural experiment
A historical comparison in which conditions differ in a way that tests a hypothesis.

Using it

A reading path

The book is long and repeats its framework region by region. For the argument:

  1. Prologue and Chapter 3

    Yali’s question and Cajamarca: the puzzle, made vivid.

  2. Chapters 4, 8 and 9

    Farming, starter kits and the Anna Karenina principle.

  3. Chapters 10 and 11

    Axes and germs: the two most famous ideas.

  4. Chapter 14

    Bands to states: how food became political power.

  5. Epilogue

    Europe versus China, and history as a science.

Read alongside

  • Why Nations Fail, summarised on this site: institutions as the rival answer.
  • The Beginning of Infinity, summarised on this site: Deutsch’s objection that creativity, not environment, drives history.
  • William H. McNeill, Plagues and Peoples (1976): disease as a force in history.
  • Alfred W. Crosby, Ecological Imperialism (1986): how European plants, animals and germs conquered new lands.
